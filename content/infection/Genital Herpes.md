# Genital Herpes
#bpt/infection #bpt/racp_cls
- - -
### Overview
Genital herpes is the most common cause of genital ulcers globally, and is in fact so common that its true incidence is difficult to estimate. The incidence in ANZ is somewhere between 1:5-8 adults, with a slight female predominance.

The viruses that cause genital herpes, HSV-1 and HSV-2, belong to the family of [[human herpesviruses]], _Herpesviridae_, which includes VZV, CMV, and EBV, that is characterised by its members’ ability to establish a state of latent infection. While HSV-2 only causes genital ulcers, HSV-1 can cause both oral and genital infection. 

Routine screening for genital herpes is not recommended.
- - -
### Pathogenesis
During the initial infection, HSV enters the skin or mucous membranes and replicates in the epithelial cells. From there, it is transported to the dorsal root ganglia via the axons, where it can lie latent and symptomatic in a non-replicative form. 

HSV can then go on to reactive from latency, leading to recurrent outbreaks. While the exact mechanisms of this remain to be elucidated, factors that may trigger reactivation include stres, illness, hormonal changes, and exposure to ultraviolet light. 

Asymptomatic viral shedding is very common, meaning that most infections are acquired from someone who is unaware that they are infected. Transmission occurs during contact with the mucosal or epithelial surgace of a person who is shedding virus in their genital or oral secretions, typically during genitogenital or orogenital (esp. HSV-1) contact. 

Infection is divided into four groups:
1. Primary infection - recently acquired infection without antibodies to either HSV-1 or HSV-2
2. Non-primary infection - recently acquired infection with antibodies to the other virus
3. First episode - first clinical episode of genital HSV-1 or HSV-2
4. Recurrent episode - symptomatic recurrences of previously acquired HSV-1/2 with antibodies against _the same_ virus. HSV-1 is less likely to cause this. 
- - -
### Clinical Presentation
The ‘classic’ primary episodes presents with:
- Severe multiple bilatearl genital ulcers
- Pain/itching
- Dysuria
- Vagina/urethral discharge
- Cervicitis/urethritis/proctitis
- Constitutional symptoms

It usually peaks within 3-5 days of onset, and then recedes over the next 3-5 days, although this can be longer without antivirals. 

Recurrent episodes tend to be milder, and with fewer localised blisters and ulceratoin. There may be preceding prodromal neuropathic symptoms. Recurrent episodes may present atypically with fissuring and erythema. 
- - -
### Diagnosis
Diagnosis is made by _type-specific_ PCR from an active lesion, although a negative test does not always rule out HSV infection. Type-specific serology is not recommended for routine diagnosis, and is typically only used in pregnancy.
- - -
### Complications
Include:
- Aseptic meningitis, particularly in HSV-2 infection
- Recurrent aseptic meningitis (Mollaret syndrome)
- Herpetic lumbosacral radiculoneuropathy - triad of urinary retention, constipation, and sacral neuralgia (Elsberg syndrome)
- HSV-2 myeloradiculitis, particularly with immunosuppression
- Bell’s palsy
- HSV encephalitis, particularly during primary HSV-1 infection
- Erythema multiforme
- Systemic infection, often in pregnancy
- Neonatal herpes

### Management 
#### First Episode
Treatment is usually with a 7-10 course of PO antivirals, e.g.:
- Valaciclovir 500mg BD
- Aciclovir 400mg TDS

Severe cases may require IV aciclovir, dosed at 5-10mg/kg TDS.

#### Recurrent Episode
Shorter courses, e.g.:
- Valaciclovir 500mg PO BD for three days
- Aciclovir 800mg PO DS for two days
- Famiciclovir 1gm PO for one day 

#### Suppressive Therapy
Antiviral therapy can be given continuously over a period of time (e.g. 6-12 months) to reduce the frequency of recurrences and of transmission to a sexual partner (as shedding is also reduced), e.g.:
- Valaciclovir 500mg PO daily 
- Aciclovir 400mg PO BD
- Famiciclovir 250mg PO BD

#### Non-Pharmacological
- Symptomatic treatment
- Education
- STI testing
- Follow up 
- Condom use
- Disclosure to partner (delays acquisition)
- Vaccines (in development)
