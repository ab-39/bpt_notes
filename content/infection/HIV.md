### Basic Pathobiology
HIV is introduced to the body via the blood or bodily secretions, after which its _glycoprotein 120_ trimer binds to CD4 on the surface of human T-cells, and the coreceptors CCR5 or CXCR4.
![](HIV/image%202.png)
The virus envelope and cell membranes fuse to release the nucleocapsid, RNA, and enzymes into the cell.

Within the cytoplasm, reverse transcriptase makes a _DNA copy_ of the HIV RNA, which the enzyme integrase inserts into the host cell chromosome. RNAse H separates the DNA from the RNA.
![](HIV/image%203.png)
HIV is able to lie dormant within the T-cells until they are activated, at which point the DNA is transcribed into mRNA and then translated into HIV proteins. The virion is assembled and then buds off the host cell, after which the enzyme protease activates the viral proteins. 

CD8 T-cells are able to recognise HIV peptides expressed on MHCI (especially HLA-B57*01 - which while being protective also contraindicates the use of abacavir) and kill the affected cell. The humoral response against HIV plays a less important rule due to the diversity in gp120 epitopes, and the fact that important epitopes are often glycosylated. Broadly neutralising antibodies can arise, but typically after _years_ of infection. Once evolved, they are important in reducing viral load. 

After exposure, HIV begins to replicate widely, most importantly in the GALT. This leads to chronic inflammation which is the cause of many of the sequelae of HIV infection. 

T-cell depletion occurs both due to immune destruction and through direct toxicity by HIV. The advantage of ‘elite controller’ alleles such as HLA-B57*01 is that they allow the early immune response to be targeted and rapid, preventing widespread infection of T-cells with HIV and controlling the size of the population. 

Slower rate of progression of HIV is associated with:
- Low HIV viral load
- HLA-B57 
- HLA-B27
- Robust CD8 response
- CCR5 deletion homozygote
- Younger age
- Shorter duration of infection
- Effective treatment

Quicker rate of progression of HIV is associated with:
- CMV coinfection
- Depression and other mental illnesses
- Substance misuse
- Infection with drug-resistant virus
- Changes in gut microbiome

### Clinical Presentation
Acute HIV presentation can present like any viral infection, with myalgias, headache, etc., usually presenting 3-6 weeks after exposure. Common laboratory features include:
- Deranged LFTs
- Thrombocytopenia
- Leucopenia 

Untreated in the long term, HIV/AIDS can present with a myriad of syndromes. Two to be aware of are:
- HIV dementia
- HIV associated nephropathy - collapsing FSGS

### Diagnosis 
Diagnosis of HIV has always centred around immunoassays. The current (fourth-generation) ELISA involves screening for:
- HIV IgG and IgM
- HIV p24 antigen 

Detection timeframes:
- HIV viral load - 3 days
- 4th Gen ELISA - ~7 days 

If this is positive, we proceed to further testing to differentiate HIV1 from HIV2.

### Treatment
HIV treatment is indicated irrespective of the patient’s progression or CD4+ cell count due to the public health implications (‘treatment as prevention’). 

At present, there is no cure for HIV as antiretroviral targets _actively replicating_ virus in cells. As there is a large cohort of latently infected CD4+ cells, eradication is currently impossible, although HSCT may prove to be a treatment in the future. 

Antiretroviral therapy (ART) typically consists of the three-drug regimen, and targets various parts of the viral life cycle:
![](HIV/image%204.png)
1. Binding -> CCR5 antagonists (e.g. maraviroc)
2. Fusion -> fusion inhibitors (e.g. enfuvirtide)
3. Reverse transcription -> NRTIs (e.g. AZT, TAF, TDF, FTC) and NNRTI (e.g. RPV, EFV)
4. Integration -> integrase inhibitors (e.g. dolutegravir, bictegravir)
5. Replication/transcription
6. Translation
7. Assembly and release
8. Maturation -> protease inhibitors (e.g. ritonavir, darunavir)

Patients udnergoing treatment for HVI should receive an HIV viral load, CD4 cell count, and genotypic resistance testing at baseline.

#### Nucleoside Reverse Transcriptase Inhibitors 
Bind to viral reverse transcriptase as the deoxynucleotide binding site, thereby blocking DNA synthesis. 

Examples include:
- Thymidine analogues:
  - Zidovudine (AZT) - lipodystrophy
- Cytosine analogues:
  - Lamivudine (3TC) - fairly high risk of resistance, well tolerated
  - Emtricitabine (FTC) - well tolerated
- Guanine analogues:
  - Abacavir (ABC) - caution if HLA-B57*01 positivity
- Adenine analogues:
  - Tenofovir - also treats HBV:
    - Disoproxil (TDF) - nephrotoxic, reduces BMD
    - Alafenamide (TAF) - prodrug converted to TDF intracellularly -> less nephrotoxic, less BMD loss, more weight gain

FTC/3TC and TAF/TDF all have activity against [[gastroenterology/Hepatitis B|hepatitis B]], and so are recommended as first-line in patients with HIV-HBV coinfection, but need to be used as part of combination therapy to avoid the emergence of resistance. 

#### Non-Nucleoside RTIs
Also bind viral reverse transcriptase, but _not_ at the deoxynucleotide binding site, altering conformation of the enzyme thus blocking DNA synthesis. Low barrier to resistance. Not effective for HIV2. 

Examples include:
- Nevirapine (NVP) - hypersensitivity reactions including fatal hepatitis ->==🔴stop if rash develops==
- Efavirenz (EFV) - CNS side-effects: e.g. sedation, insomnia, vivid dreams, rash, dyslipidaemia
- Rilpiverine (RPV) - prolonged QTc
- Delaverdine (DLV) - rash (not an indication to stop), headache
- Etravirine (ETR) - rash (not an indication to stop), GI upset 
- Doravirine (DOR)

#### Protease Inhibitors
Block viral protease, preventing maturation of the virus during and after budding of the virion, leading to defective virus. High barrier to resistance **if** boosted. Side effects include GI upset, dyslipidaemia, impaired glucose tolerance, and lipodystrophy. 

Examples include:
- Ritonavir (/r) - poorly tolerated -> only used in small doses to boost other PIs by inhibiting CYP3A4 (hence has _many_ drug interactions)
- Lopinavir (LPV) - needs BD dosing
- Atazanvir (ATV) - elevated bilirubin, renal stone
- Darunavir (DRV) - rash (not an indication to stop)

#### Integrase Inhibitors
The mainstay of treatment. Inhibit viral integrase, preventing integration of viral DNA into the host DNA causing a _rapid_ reduction in viral load.

Examples include:
- Raltegravir (RAL) - low barrier to resistance, elevated CK/rhabdomyolysis
- Elvitegravir (EVG) - coformulated with CYP3A4 inhibitor (cobicistat) so has _several_ drug interactions
- Dolutegravir (DTG) - high barrier to resistance, headache, depression, anxiety, insomnia 
  - Previous signsl towards neural tube defects in pregnancy, now generally thought to be safe if commenced _after_ conception
- Bictegravir (BIC) - headache, GI upset, cannot be used in combination with the antiarrhythmic dofetilide
- Cabotegravir - long-acting INSTI used as PrEP -> if the patient develops HIV infection while using CAB-LA PrEP, further treatment with INSTIs is not recommended 

#### Other Drugs
- Fusion inhibitors (e.g. enfurvitide) - prevents the fusion of viral membrane with cell membrane - requires injections
- CCR5 antagonists (e.g. maraviroc) - prevent CCR5-tropic virus from entering cells - requires a tropism analysis; virus can switch tropism 
- Ibalizumab is a monoclonal antibody that is given as an intravenous infusion every two weeks and binds the CD4 molecule in such a way that it does not block viral attachment, but does block entry. It is indicated for the treatment of heavily antiretroviral-experienced HIV-1-infected adults with multidrug-resistant virus who are failing their current regimen. 

#### Choice of Regimen
In 2025, initial treatment for HIV is generally with an INSTI with two NRTIs, e.g.:
- Biktarvy = BIC/TAF/FTC
  - First-line in patients with HBV
- Triumeq = DTG/ABC/3TC 
  - Cannot be used in patients with HBV
  - Contraindicated in patients with the HLA-B57*01 mutation
- DTG + Descovy (= TAF/FTC)
- RAL + Descovy (= TAF/FTC)

In some cases where the virus has demonstrated a non-resistant genotype and there is no HBV-coinfection, an INSTI with a single NRTI can be considered. Alternative regimens include:
- PI/r + 2xNRTI 
- NNRTI + 2xNRTI

#### Timing of ART
In general, immediate commencement of ART is recommended. The exception is if the patient has a _treatable_ opportunistic CNS infection:
- Tuberculous meningitis -> delay until TB therapy initiated, ideally within 8wk
- Cryptococcal meningitis -> delay until 4-6w of LAMB treatment
Here, ART is delayed due to the risk of deterioration with IRIS. 

#### Additional Treatments
- Statins - the [_REPRIEVE_ study](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2304146) found that addition of a statin reduced MACE in patients with well-controlled HIV on ART

### Prophylaxis
#### Post-Exposure Prophylaxis
PEP is indicated in the following situations:
- Condomless anal intercourse or receptive vaginal intercourse with an HIV-positive individual with an unknown or detectable viral load
- Shared injecting injection with a known HIV-positive person
- Following sexual assault
- Condomless anal intercourse with a person from a high HIV prevalence risk group

PEP must be initiated with 72h and continued for at least 28 days. The recommended regimen is generally a three-drug combination therapy _unless_:
- Exposure is occupational (e.g. NSI or mucous membrane exposure) and the source is known to have an undetectable viral load -> two-drug regimen
- Non-occupational exposure and the source viral load is known to be undetectable -> PEP not recommended
- Non-occupational exposure and the source’s HIV status is unknown -> two-drug regimen generally recommended 

#### Pre-Exposure Prophylaxis 
PrEP is indicated for:
- MSM, particularly if high-risk sexual behaviours or known coinfection with other STIs
- Others who have receptive intercourse with any casual MSM partner 
- People who inject drugs and share equipment with a known HIV-positive individual 

The typical regimen is with TAF/FTC (Descovy), which can be administered:
- Daily
- On-demand in a 2+1+1 regimen:
  - Two doses prior to sex
  - One dose at each of +24h and +48h after sex 

PrEP is associated with significantly increased rates of _other_ [[sexually transmissible infections]]. 

#### Emerging Therapies
Recently, the _PURPOSE_ trials found that six-monthly injections with lenacapavir provided significant reductions in the rate of acquisition of HIV in both [women](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2407001) and [men and gender-diverse populations](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2411858). 

[Cabotegravir](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2101016), a long-acting INSTI given via 8-weekly IM injection, has also found to be superior to TDF/FTC (Truvada) as PrEP in at-risk MSM and transgender women.

### Opportunistic Infections in HIV
Opportunistic infections (and some malignancies) occur in HIV-positive individuals as the disease progresses and the CD4+ cell count drops. Indeed, the emergence of some opportunistic infections and variants of non-Hodgkin lymphoma are sufficient to make the diagnosis of AIDS. In general, a cell count of 200/µL is the threshold below which AIDS-defining illnesses begin to emerge. It is also the threshold above which live vaccines can be administered. 
![](HIV/image%205.png)
#### *Pneumocystis jirovecii* pneumonia
Characterised by a subacute onset of progressive exertional dyspnoea, fever, and dry cough. Often see bilateral ground glass opacities on CT of the chest. Caused by a unicellular fungus that does not respond to antifungals, but, rather, TMP-SMX. There is a risk of pneumothorax. Diagnosis can be made with HRCT, PCR of induced sputum (sensitivity and specificity close to 100%), or BAL. Treatment is with TMP-SMX and steroids, which have been found to reduce mortality in hypoxic patients (PaO2 <70mmHg). Prophylaxis is indicated if CD4+ <200/µL. 

> [!NOTE] Why TMP-SMX?
> Despite being a fungus, _P. jirovecii_ does not have ergosterol in its cell membrane, and hence azoles and polyenes cannot be used. It does, however, synthesise its own folic acid. Hence, TMP is the agent of choice. 

#### *Cryptococcus neoformans* meningitis 
_Cryptococcus neoformans_ is an environmental yeast that can cause meningoencephalitis in immunocompromised hosts, typically in a subacute fashion. Diagnosis is typically made after an LP demonstrates a raised opening pressure, and India ink stain of the CSF shows the organisms or the antigens are detected. 

Treatment is with:
- Developed settings: 
  - Induction: LAMB + flucytosine (prodrug for 5-FU that is activated by cytosine deaminase in fungal cells) for 2-6 weeks
  - Consolidation: eight weeks of high-dose fluconazole
  - Maintenance: 12 months of daily fluconaozle
- Developing settings:
  - Induction: single dose LAMB + 2-4 weeks of flucytosine and high dose fluconazole
    - Due to the high toxicity of amphotericin, based on the results of the [_AMIBITION_ study](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2111904)
  - Consolidation and maintenance as above

Timing of ART initiation should be _after_ the patient is on consolidation treatment, as there is increased mortality with early initiation of ART, particularly in resource-limited settings ([Makadzange et al., 2010](https://academic.oup.com/cid/article-abstract/50/11/1532/508551?login=false)). Additionally, there is a risk of IRIS as the immune system recovers. 

#### Toxoplasmosis
Toxoplasmosis is the most common CNS infection in patients with advanced HIV (typically CD4+ <100/µL). Diagnosis is generally based on neuroimaging (ring enhancing lesions with surrounding oedema) and positive serology (best test) or CSF NAAT (less sensitive but highly specific). Treatment with TMP-SMX is generally commenced if there is clinical suspicion (e.g. compatible enhancing CNS lesions). Lack of improvement with treatment should raise suspicion for CNS lymphoma, and may warrant a brain biopsy. TMP-SMX prophylaxis is also used until CD4+ >200/μL.

#### CMV Retinitis
The most feared complication of CMV infection in advanced HIV is retinitis. This typically emerges at _very_ low CD4+ counts, and presents as blindness. Funduscopy reveals widespread lesions throughout the retina. Treatment is with systemic +/- intravitreal ganciclovir. 

#### IRIS
The immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome is a paradoxical worsening of infections (particularly TB, also ) as the immune system recovers. Predictors of IRIS include high viral load, low CD4+ count, or a high pathogen burden. Timing of onset is usually six weeks after commencement of ART.

### Perinatal Transmission
The idea that U=U holds - if the maternal viral load is <50 and the mother is on ART, the risk of vertical transmission is <0.1%. If the viral load is >1000 or unknown, IV zidovudine and C-section is recommended. Most antiretrovirals are safe in pregnancy, and most first line regimens are able to be initiated/continued.

Prior to the advent of antiretroviral therapy, epidemiological studies placed the risk of Mother-to-child transmission between 15-45% with 90% of this risk being in the perinatal period. The risk of transmission through breastfeeding was estimated to be 14%.
The risk of transmission is linked to the mother's viral load. In women who have viral suppression on antiretroviral treatment (ART) at the time of conception and who maintain adherence to ART and viral suppression throughout pregnancy, delivery, and breastfeeding, perinatal HIV transmission is reduced to <1 percent.
