### With a focus on Dengue, Zika, and Chikungunya

### Overview
The term ‘arbovirus’ is actually an initialism to describe the ARthopod-BOrne VIRUSES, i.e those viruses for which the main vectors are mosquitoes, ticks, and sandflies. 

The viruses themselves belong to several different families, including _Flaviviridae_ and _Alphaviridae_. 
- - -
### Transmission
Dengue, Zika, and Chikuingunya are all transmitted by mosquitoes belonging to the genus _Aedes_, which can be identified by their propensity to bite during the day (with peaks at dawn and dusk) and the white stripes on their bodies. They typically breed in stagnant water.
![](Arboviruses/image.png)
The infection cycle has four stages:
1. Acquisition of the virus by the mosquito after a blood meal
2. Extrinsic incubation (8-10 days) in the. mosquito’s midgut and then salivary glands
3. Human infection
4. Intrisic incubation (4-7 days) prior to onset of clinical disease
- - -
### Epidemiology and Prevention
All three of these arboviruses have been characterised by explosive outbreaks when the virus moves into a new population, followed by settling into an endemic status.

General management strategies include surveillance, vector control, water management and sanitation, vaccination, bite prevention, and safe sex.

Interestingly, releasing _Wolbachia_ bacteria into the environment can infect mosquito populations, leading to vector control.

### Dengue
Dengue is a flavivirus, with four serotypes that can affect humans: DENV1-4. It is mosquito borne by _Aedes_ mosquitoes, and humans are the primary hosts. 

Dengue is endemic in over 125 countries, and infects an estimated 400 million people each year, a quarter of whom progress to clinical dengue. There are approximately 500,000 cases of dengue haemorrhagic fever each year. Large outbreaks typically occur when new serotypes are introduced into a population, displacing the existing serotype. 

Dengue has a short incubation period, of 4-7 days on average. The clinical presentation varies significantly with host and viral factors, and in most cases results in a subclinical or mild infection. The clinical illness has three phases:
1. Febrile phase (first 4d)
   - Fever, malaise, **retroorbital headache**, arthralgias, myalgias
   - May be a rash 
2. Critical phase (d4-7)
   - Increase capillary permeability leading to extravasation of fluid
   - Thrombocytopenia and internal haemorrhage
   - End-organ damage - myocarditis, hepatitis, CNS involvement
   - Variations in haematocrit - typically rises in 
3. Recovery phase (d7+)
   - Reversal of capillary leakage
   - Prolonged asthenia, fatigue, depression
   - Classic dengue rash - ‘sea of red with islands of sparing’, blanching 

Laboratory tests often show:
- Thrombocytopenia
- Lymphopenia
- Transaminitis 

Infection with one serotype of dengue confers lifelong protection to that serotype, but not others. This can, actually, be problematic, as if there are e.g. circulating DENV1 antibodies during an infection with DENV2, this can cause a _more severe_ infection with DENV2, as the immune response is ineffective but the virus is able to replicate in immune cells. 

Diagnosis is with RT-PCR: the NS1 antigen in particular is highest in the first week of infection. Thereafter, serology is typically positive. 

Treatment is supportive. 

There is a live attenuated, tetravalent chimeric vaccine used in endemic areas for people with serological prior infection (as it may increase severity in non-exposed individuals). It is not recommended for travellers. There are two live attenuated vaccines in development.

### Chikungunya
Chikungunya is an alphavirus (like rubella and RRV), coming from the Makonde word for ‘that which bends up the joints’. It was first identified on the Makonde Plateau in Tanzania in 1952, although has likely been circulating for centuries prior to that. It is also transmitted by _Aedes_ mosquitoes. Humans are not a reservoir, but rather a spillover host. 

There is only one known serotype of chikungunya, and it has fourgenotypes - West African, East/Central/South African, Asian, and Indian Ocean. 

After an incubation period of three days, the majority of patients proceed to a clinical illness characterised by a symmetrical, erosive, non-deforming small joint polyarthropathy, that can last as long as three years. Fewer than 1/1000 patients proceed to severe disease with encephalitis, myocarditis, hepatitis, pneumonitis, pancreatitis, and possibly multiorgan failure. 

Diagnosis is with PCR in the acute phase, and serology thereafter. 

There are no targeted antiviral therapies, and trials of existing immunomodulatory drugs have been futile. There is no licenced CHIK vaccine, but there are several in development.

### Zika
Zika is a flavivirus closely related to dengue. It was first identified in rhesus monkeys in the Zika forest in Nigeria in 1947, with the first human cases identified shortly thereafter. The total case load was relatively low until two explosive outbreaks in 2007 (Yap) and 2013 (the Pacific Islands). 

The clinical syndrome of Zika is typically mild, either subclinical or a mild febrile illness +/- rash. Fewer than 1/1000 will have severe disease. Zika can be neurotropic, triggering Guillain-Barré, meningitis, and myelitis. In maternal infection in the first half of pregnancy, Zika can cause microcephaly, FGR, hydrops fetalis, and fetal loss. 

Like other arboviruses, early diagnosis is with RT-PCR, while serology is used after the first week. Management is supportive, and should NSAIDs/aspirin shoul dbe avoided. Pregnancy should be avoided for 6m after returning from a Zika endemic region. There are vaccines in phase 1-3 trials.
