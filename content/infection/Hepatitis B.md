### Virology
Hepatitis B is a compact, double shelled partially-ds-DNA Hepadnavirus that has a complex structure despite its relatively compact genome (3.4kbp). It has several important components that are of utility to diagnostic testing:
- HBV DNA - partially-ds-DNA
- Nucleocapsid core - the hepatitis B core antigen
- Surface envelope protein - the hepatitis B surface antigen 

> [!TIP] Fun Fact
> _Fun fact: the hepatitis B surface antigen was first discovered in the blood of an Australian by the American physician and biochemist Baruch Blumberg_ 

Part of the reason that HBV is so hard to treat is that it acts like a retrovirus and injects itself into the host genome. 
- - -
### Epidemiology
Hepatitis B is a massive contributor to the global burden of disease, with an estimated ~1 million infections and an additional ~1 million deaths each year. It is the cause of ~80% of all [[gastroenterology/Hepatocellular Carcinoma|hepatocellular carcinoma]], and is the second most important human carcinogen after tobacco.

In Australia, there are an estimated 225,000 people living with HBV, which is thought to represent only one third of the total disease burden.

There are ten genotypes of hepatitis B, of which the most relevant to the Australian context is HBV-C4, which affects predominantly indigenous people and is associated with a higher risk of progression to [[cirrhosis]] and HCC.

### Screening
The current Australian guidelines recommend that the following populations should be screened for HBV:
- ATSI/Maōri
- People born in areas with ≥2% prevalence: Mongolia, Indochina, Pacific Islands, North Africa, etc.
- Prisoners
- People who inject drugs
- People with [[gastroenterology/Hepatitis C|hepatitis C]] or HIV
- Homosexuals
- In NZ: anyone >35y who is unvaccinated
- Pregnant women
- Immunosuppressed 
- People with other chronic liver disease
- People on dialysis

### Natural History
Like any acute viral hepatitis, the range of presentations for acute HBV infections from asymptomatic or a mild viral illness ranging to fulminant hepatitis and death in about 1% of cases. In general, 90% of perinatal infections will progress to chronic HBV, while only 5% of adult infections will.

Extrahepatic symptoms include:
- Polyarteritis nodosa
- Glomerular disease
- Serum sickness

If the surface antigen persists for more than 6 months, the patient is said to have _chronic hepatitis B_. Of chronic patients:
- 5% will go onto develop HCC _without_ previously developing cirrhosis (unlike HCV)
- ~30% will go onto develop cirrhosis and then possible liver failure or HCC

![](Hepatitis%20B/image.png)
Phase I - Immune Tolerance (age 0-40):
- High viral load
- Low hepatic inflammation
- HBeAg present
- HBeAb negative

Phase II - Immune Clearance/Immune Active:
- Fluctuating viral load
- Fluctuating hepatic inflammation
- HBeAg is positive, but can become negative (seroconversion rate of about 5%/y)
- HBeAb can become positive
  -> should be treated

Phase III - Immune Control:
- Low viral load
- Low hepatic inflammation
- HBeAg negative
- HBeAb positive 
- ~1%/y of sAg loss -> functional cure (undetectable viral load)

Phase IV - Immune Escape:
- High viral load
- High hepatic inflammation
- HBeAg negative
- HBeAg positive 
  -> should be treated

### Diagnosis
Diagnosis is typically based on serological markers:
- Hepatitis B core antigen - HBcAg - not testable in serum
- Hepatitis B core antibody:
  - IgM HBcAb -> acute or recent infection
  - IgG HBcAb -> past exposure (may be the only positive marker in distant quiescent infection)
- Hepatitis B surface antigen (the Australian antigen) - HBsAg -> elevated in any current (acute or chronic) infection
- Hepatitis B surface antibody - HBsAb -> indicative of immunity to HBV 
  - This will not be elevated in chronic HBV infection as the patient has not attained immunity 
  - If the HBcAb is negative, this suggests the patient was immunised 
- Hepatitis B e antigen - HBeAg - rises in acute infection to allow immune evasion

### Management
There is no cure, as hepatitis B is able to close its genome covalently into a small ‘chromosome’. This ccc DNA appears to have a long half-life in non-dividing hepatocytes and is not directly inhibited by approved nucleos(t)ide analogue therapies, accounting for the difficulty in achieving virus clearance during treatment of chronic hepatitis B. Treatment should be initiated in response to the presence of HBV DNA, as this indicates active replication of the virus. 

Current treatment is with antivirals:
- Tenofovir - viral polymerase inhibitor 
  - Safer in pregnancy, renal and bone side effects
- Entecavir - guanisine analogue that inhibits HBV polymerase
- Lamivudine - good, but very prone to developing resistance
  - Early resistance to lamivudine may portend future resistance to entecavir 

![](Hepatitis%20B/image%202.png)

These both tend to be safe in decompensated liver disease. 

Or with pegylated interferon for patients with mild hepatitis B. Generally safe albeit with some psychiatric and autoimmune side effects. Contraindicated in acute HBV infection or cirrhosis.

![](Hepatitis%20B/image%203.png)

HCC surveillance is similar as for patients with HCV, and also includes patients coinfected with hepatitis D.

### Hepatitis B and Pregnancy
Perinatal transmission is _very_ high (up to 90%) if the baby does not receive:
- Hepatitis B immune globulin within 12hrs of birth
- Vaccination at birth with a three-dose regimen 

Risk of transmission increases with eAg positivity and viral load. If the viral load is greater than >10,000 MiU/mL, tenofovir disoproxil is used to reduce it. Breastfeeding is not contraindicated even on treatment.

### Hepatitis D
In 5-10% of patients, there is coinfection with hepatitis D, a small, negative-sense ssRNA virus. HDV is a _defective_ virus, and needs the presence of HBV (or another hepadnavirus) to replicate. Although less common, it can actually cause the most aggressive acute viral hepatitis. Treating HBV does not treat HDV, as the helper function can persist with sAg. Bulevirtide is the treatment of choice. 

Note that coinfection with HCV and HIV is also common. 
- - -
### Hepatitis B and Immunosuppression
Patients undergoing immunosuppression or high-risk cancer therapy (particularly with B-cell depleting agents such as rituximab).who are sAg or cAb positive should receive treatment, while patients receiving low-risk immunosuppression for non-malignant conditions should be surveilled. Treatment is typically continued for up to 24 months for high-risk immunosuppression and 6-12 months for low-risk.

### Hepatitis B and Hepatocellular Carcinoma
Hepatitis B is a major risk factor for hepatocellular cancer, and is estimated to cause 50% of worldwide HCC. In Australia, [GESA recommends](https://www.gesa.org.au/public/13/files/Education%20%26%20Resources/Clinical%20Practice%20Resources/Hep%20B/HBV%20consensus%20Mar%202022%20Updated.pdf) screening the following subsets of patients with HBV for HCC:
- Those with cirrhosis
- Those without cirrhosis who fall into the following groups:
  - Asian men ≥40y
  - Asian women ≥50y
  - Subsaharan Africans ≥20y
  - Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people ≥50y
  - HDV coinfection
  - FDR with HCC
- Other high risk groups - e.g. Māori/Pasifika men ≥40y/women ≥50y
