# Cytomegalovirus
#bpt/infection #bpt/racp_cls
- - -
### Overview
Cytomegalovirus, or human betaherpesvirus 5, is a common cause of infection in humans. 

CMV is a common infection, affecting perhaps a quarter of the population. 
- - -
### Clinical Manifestations - Immunocompetent Patients 
In immunocompetent hosts, the syndrome can range from completely asymptomatic, to a mild fever and lymphocytosis, to severe symptoms such as:
- Pneumonitis
- Hepatitis
- Guilliain-Barré syndrome
- Meningoencephalitis
- Myocarditis

In terms of laboratory findings, we often see:
- Lymphocytosis
- Thrombocytopenia
- Haemolytic anaemia

Congenital and neonatal ifnection is associated with particular morbidity:
- 30-40% risk of congenital infection if primary infection in the mother
- Greatest risk in the first trimester
- ~15% affected at birth, with:
  - IUGR
  - Pneumonitis
  - Thrombocytopenia
  - Microcephaly
  - Chorioretinitis
  - Sensorineural hearing loss
- ~15% are asymptomatic at birth, but go on to develop sequelae
- - -
### Clinical Manifestations - Immunocompromised Patients 
CMV can be deadly in immunocompromised patients, but again, range from a mild, flu-like illness to disseminated infection, with:
- Colitis
- Hepatitis
- Pneumonitis
- Meningoencephalitis
- Retinitis (particularly in HIV) - fluffy retinal infiltrates with areas of haemorrhage

Risk factors include:
- Mismatched serostatus:
  - In solid organ transplant, D+/R- runs the risk of introducing primary infection into the recipient
  - In [[allogeneic stem cell transplant]], conversely, D-/R+ runs the risk of the virus running unchecked by the new immune system as its myeloid reservoir expands with engraftment
- Graft type - espeically solid organs
- Immunosuppression - particularly lymphodepletion
  - Interestingly, MTOR based therapies appear to be protective
- Host factors - age, comorbidities, etc.
- - -
### Diagnosis
Serology is the key diagnostic test in immunocompetent host, but has a more limited role in the immunosuppressed. Here, the main tests include:
- CMV viral load - although the absence of viraemia
- CMV PCR
- Histopathology looking for viral ‘owl’s eye’ inclusion bodies 
- - -
### Treatment
First line treatment - comparable efficacy found in the _VICTOR_ study:
- Ganciclovir (IV, poor bioavailability)
- Valganciclovir (PO)

Other options include:
- (Val)ganciclovir:
  - Guanosine analogues inhibiting CMV DNA polymerase
  - Renally dosed
  - ADRs include myelosuppression and neutropenua 
- Foscarnet:
  - Pyrophosphate analogue that binds directly to DNA polymerase
  - Significantly nephrotoxic 
  - Marked derangement of electrolytes -> reduced Mg2+, Ca2+, and PO4
- Cidofovir:
  - Nucleotide analogue of cytosine
  - Very long intracellular half life enabling weekly dosing 
  - Can cause damage to the proximal convoluted tubule
  - Has a prodrug - brincidofovir
- Maribavir:
  - Targets viral UL97, comeptitive inhibitor of cellular kinases
  - Reliant on both CYP450 and p-glycoprotein
  - Strong trial evidence supporting use for refractory or resistant infection
- Adoptive T-cell therapies - e.g. cytotoxic T-lymphocyte infusions

#### Resistance:
In general, suspect resistance if the viral load does not decline after two weeks. Key mutations include:
- UL97 mutations -> GCV/VGCV/CDF resistance
- UL54 -> cross resistance 

#### Prophylaxis
Typically with valganciclovir or letermovir (a CMV-terminase inhibitor). There are generally two strategies:
- Prophylaxis - utilised after solid organ transplant
- Close monitoring and early treatment - twice-weekly blood tests and early initiation of treatment. Used after HSCT due to the myelotoxic effects of VGCV. 
  - Alternatively, letermovir is now increasingly used as prophylaxis after HSCT, although it has a low barrier to resistance and does not protect against other herpesviruses
  - In patients for whom there are complications of VGCV (e.g. neutropenia), the watch/wait option can be feasibly used 

There is active research into vaccines for CMV.
