# Infective Endocarditis
#bpt/infection #bpt/cardiology #bpt/racp_cls
- - -
### Overview
Infective endocarditis refers to infection of the heart valves, either native or prosthetic. This most commonly occurs due to bacteraemia, or else due to damaged or prosthetic valves. The most common valvular lesion associated with IE is mitral valve prolapse with regurgitation (although previously, mitral stenosis secondary to rheumatic heart disease was the most common). 
- - -
### Microbiology
Most commonly _Staphylococcus aureus_, followed by:
- Viridans streptococci
- Coagulase-negative staphylococci - commonly spp. _epidermidis_ 
- Enterococci

The streptococcal species with the highest risk of endocarditis include: 
- _S. cristatus_
- _S. gordonii_
- _S. sanguinis_
- _S. gallolyticus_ (né _bovis_) - BSI/IE with this organism is _strongly_ associated with colonic pathology, particularly cancer
- _S. mitis_ 

#### Culture-Negative Endocarditis
The most common cause of culture-negative IE is antibiotics having been given prior to antibiotic administration. It is also important to consider fastidious organisms:
- _Bartonella_ spp.
- _Coxiella burnetii_ is another rare but significant cause of ‘culture-negative’ endocarditis (Q-fever)
- _Brucella_ spp. - zoonosis from dairy/livestock/etc., associated with LBP and spondylitis; cultures typically take ~1 week before becoming positive 
- _Legionella_ spp.
- _Tropheryma whipplei_ 

These organisms cannot be detected via culture, but can usually be identified via serology or PCR. 

#### The HACEK Group
- _Haemophlius_ spp.
- _Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans_
- _Cardiobacterium_ spp.
- _Eikenella corrodens_
- _Kingella_ spp.
These are a group of oropharyngeal, Gram-negative bacteria that are slow to grow in traditional culture media. They are a rare cause of IE, in total accounting for ~5% of cases, but when present in blood cultures are _strongly_ suggestive of IE.

### Diagnosis
The main set of diagnostic criteria for infective endocarditis are the modified _Duke criteria_:
| Pathologic Criteria                                          | Major Clinical Criteria                                      | Minor Clinical Criteria                                      |
|--------------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------|
| Culture of a pathogen from tissue or implanted material with clinical syndrome consistent with IE | Typical pathogens for IE from two separate blood cultures    | Predisposing heart abnormality, injecting drug use, history of IE, or an implanted device |
| Histology from tissue consistent with IE                     | Non-typical organisms for IE from three separate cultures    | Fever >38.4’c<br>                                            |
|                                                              | _C. burnetii_ in a single blood culture or another study     | Vascular phenomena (e.g. emboli, Janeway lesions)            |
|                                                              | _Bartonella_ spp. or _T. whipplei_ on blood molecular testing | Immunologic phenomena (e.g. Osler nodes, Roth spots)         |
|                                                              | _Bartonella_ serology                                        | Positive blood cultures not fulfilling major criteria but consistent with IE |
|                                                              | Classic findings of IE on TTE/TOE or cardiac CT              | Positive culture or molecular test consistent with IE in a non-cardiac sterile site |
|                                                              | PET scan suggestive of IE                                    | Abdnormal findings on PET within three months of valve or device insertion |
|                                                              | Surgical evidence of IE on inspection during cardiac surgery | New regurgitation on auscultation                            |

Diagnosis requires:
* ≥ 1 pathological criterion
* ≥ 2 major criteria
* 1 major criterion and ≥ 3 minor criteria
  * 1 major and <3 minor criteria or 3 minor criteria alone -> possible IE  
* ≥ 5 minor criteria

[[Echocardiography]] is the mainstay of diagnosis of endocarditis, with most patients beginning with a TTE, and all but the lowest-risk patients with negative TTEs proceed to a TOE. Echocardiographic signs of endocarditis include:
- Vegetations
- Evidence of abscess formation (hypodense)
- New regurgitation

The _VIRSTA_ score can be used to stratify patients with a SBI who need an early TOE:
- Evidence of embolism
- Staphylococcal meningitis
- Intracardiac device or Hx of IE
- Native valve disease
- IVDU
- Persistent bacteraemia
- Vertebral osteomyelitis
- Community acquisition
- Sepsis/shock
- CRP >190mg/L

A score ≤2 and a negative TTE are generally sufficient to rule out IE. 

PET/SPECT can be useful for prosthetic valve IE or device infection.

### Treatment
Infective endocarditis generally requires a prolonged course of therapy (6w), and can be quick to recur. This is likely due to the impaired host ability to monut immune responses against vegetations, which are also difficult for antibiotics to penetrate. As a principle, antibiotic concentrations should be maintained over the MIC for the duration of treatment, unless synergistic treatment is being used.

Treatment is generally intravenous, but a recent study ([Iversen et al., 2019](https://www.nejm.org/doi/10.1056/NEJMoa1808312?url_ver=Z39.88-2003&rfr_id=ori:rid:crossref.org&rfr_dat=cr_pub%20%200pubmed)) suggests that oral antibiotics can be non-inferior with respect to mortality, progression to surgery, embolism, and relapse when used to treat eendocarditis due to the common streptococcal and staphylococcal species. Oral switch is generally achieved with a two-drug combination (e.g. amoxicillin + rifampicin, amoxicillin + fusidic acid, linezolid + rifampicin, etc.)

#### Synergy in the Treatment of _E. faecalis_ 
In general, all enterococci demonstrate high-level resistance to several β-lactams, particularly cefalosporins, due to the production of low-affinity penicillin-binding proteins. Despite the fact that many isolates of _E. faecalis_ can demonstrate sensitivity to amoxicillin, this means that amoxicillin monotherapy often results in unacceptably high rates of relapse. In these settings, synergistic therapy is often used.

A classic example of synergy is a pencillin in combination with an aminoglycoside, e.g. amoxicillin with gentamicin. Here, the effect of amoxicillin on the cell wall allows for greater penetration of the aminoglycoside into the bacterial cell. The usual regimen is combination therapy for two weeks, followed by four weeks more of penicillins.

An alternative combination can be amoxicillin with a third-generation cefalosporin (e.g. cefotaxime), which allows for greater saturation of the various subtypes of penicillin-binding protein (amoxicillin -> 4/5, cefotaxime -> 2/3). Current data suggests ampcillin/benzylpenicillin + ceftriaxone is likely as effective as amoxicillin + gentamicin at reducing relapse, while having fewer side-effects.

#### Surgery
Surgery is indicated for three broad indications:
- Heart failure:
  - Severe valvular dysfunction
  - Poor cardiac function
  - Pulmonary oedema
- Uncontrolled infection:
  - Aortic root abscess
  - Persistent fevers and positive blood cultures despite >7d treatment
  - Fungal or multi-drug resistant organism implicated
- Prevention of embolism:
  - Vegetation >15mm 
  - Embolic episodes with a large vegetation (>10mm)

#### Prophylaxis
Prophylaxis (generally with amoxicillin) is generally only recommended if both of the following criteria are satisifed:
- There is a high risk cardiac condition, e.g.:
  - Prosthetic heart valve
  - Rheumatic heart disease
  - Previous endocarditis
  - Unrepaired (or repair <6 months ago) cyanotic congenital heart disease
- **And**, the patient is undergoing a high-risk procedure, e.g.:
  - Dental procedures that manipulate or perforate oral mucosa
  - Tonsillectomy or adenoidectomy
  - Surgery at the site of an established infection

Procedures that do _not_ require prophylaxis include bronchoscopies, colonoscopies, TURP, PCI, etc.
