### Overview
The spleen is, in a sense, the largest lymph node in the body, and is important in its role as a _phagocytic filter_ for old cells. In addition to this, it plays critical roles in immunity:
- White pulp -> production of lymphocytes and macrophages
- Marginal zone -> filtration
- Red pulp -> reservoir for red cells

Importantly, the spleen plays an important role in the body’s defence against _encapsulated bacteria_. Here, the polysaccharide capsule prevents the binding of complement, so these bacteria are only able to be cleared by splenic macrophages after recognising spleen-derived opsonins such as properdin. 

The most common reasons for asplenia are therapeutic (e.g. in ITP) or traumatic. Medical causes for hyposplenism include HSCT and chronic GvHD and autoimmune disease. Splenectomy for haematologic indications are generally associated with higher risk of infection. 

The pathognomic haematological finding for asplenia is the presence of _Howell-Jolly bodies_ in the peripheral red cells. These inclusions, arising in the peripheries of red cells from nuclear remnants, usually mark the red cell for destruction in the spleen:

The rate of sepsis and overwhelming post-splenic infection (OPSI)  is up to fifty times higher than the general population, and is associated with a mortality rate as high as 70%. The most common pathogens causing OPSI are:
- _Streptococcus pneumoniae_
- _Haemophilus influenzae_ b (Gram-negative bacillus)
- _Neisseria meningitidis_ (Gram-negative coccus)
- _Capnocytophagia canimorsus_ -> animal bites/scratches
- Some Gram-negative organisms -> _E. coli_, _P. aeruginosa_ 
- _Salmonella_ spp. -> particularly in [[sickle cell disease]]

### Treatment
#### Antibiotics
Prophylactic daily antibiotics (e.g. amoxicillin daily or BD penicillin VK) is recommended for three years post-splenectomy, or lifelong if the patient is immunocsuppressed immunosuppressed or has a history of OPSI.  

After three years, patients should receive a “stand-by” course of 2g amoxicillin to take if th patient develops a fever.

#### Vaccination 
~Pneumococcus~
- The conjugated vaccine PCV13 should be given at least once in the adult’s lifetime, and prior to the polysaccharide vaccine PPV23 (this is due to the fact that PPV23 is associated with hyporesponsiveness, which can then delay PCV13 being administered)
- There should be three doses of PPV23 administered, at least 5y apart
- Ideally, the vaccine should be given at least 2 weeks prior to splenectomy 

~Meningococcus~
- Various options available - both polysaccharide and conjugated
- Serotype MenB most common for invasive disease, but the most difficult to vaccinate against 
- Suggest:
  - 4MenCV x2 doses, two months apart, then every five years
  - MenBV x2 doses, two months apart, then every five years

~Influenza~
- Recommended annually
