### Overview
ESBL is an extracellular _enzyme_ encoded by a gene found in any aerobic, Gram-negative bacillus (but most commonly _E. coli_ and _Klebsiella_ spp.) that confers resistance to β-lactam antibiotics. The defining feature of ESBLs is _resistance to all third-generation cephalosporins_ (e.g. ceftriaxone, ceftazidime), as well as all penicillins, **aztreonam**, and lower-generation cephalosporins. It is typically mediated by a plasmid, and thus can spread rapidly through bacterial populations.
- ESBLs technically demonstrate susceptibilty to β-lactamase inhibitors (e.g. clavulanic acid), but in reality they are often able to produce such large amounts of the enzyme that inhibitors are insufficient to counter their effect

ESBL infections are associated with poorer outcomes, longer length of stay, and higher healthcare costs. 

While colonisation with an ESBL producer does not necessarily warrant treatment, it still may be transmitted person-person.

In many communities worldwide, particularly in Asia, ESBL-producers are hyperendemic, and make up the majority of GNBs. Travel to India and other parts of Asia is the key risk factor.

There are numerous specific genes, including:
- _CTX-M_ - the most common today
- _TEM_
- _SHV_

Carbapenems are the drugs of choice for severe ESBL-producing infections, as established by the [_MERINO_](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2702145) trial. If susceptible, aminoglycosides are a reasonable alternative. The exception to this is  for uncomplicated UTI, where fosfomycin, mecillinam, and nitrofurantoin can be used.
