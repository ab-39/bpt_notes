### Overview
Cellulitis is an _acute, spreading_ bacterial infection of the dermis and subcutaneous tissues. 

It is difficult to pin down a causative organism for most cases of cellulitis (as there is nothing to swab!), but it seems that the most commonly implicated organisms are are β-haemolytic _Streptococcus_ species (typically Lancefield groups C/G and A), or less commonly (but more severely) _Staphylococcus aureus_ (‘purulent’ cellulitis). Gram-negative organisms, on the other hand, rarely cause cellulitis (<1%), except in specific cases:
- Certain populations -> immunocompromise, cirrhosis, chronic leg ulceration
- On the abdomen and groin - ‘nipples to knees is diluted faeces’
- Marine exposure -> _Vibrio_ and _Aeromonas_ spp.
- Dog/cat bites -> _Pasteurella_ and _Capnocytophagia_ spp. 

Most cellulitis does not require hospitalisation - the general aim of treatment is to prevent rare complications. Meta-analytic data from _healthy_ patients with cellulitis showed no benefit to IV when compared with PO for up to moderate cellulitis, but these studies did not include obese or immunocompromised patients, or those with severe sepsis. So, in general IV therapy is generally reserved for those with:
- Evidence of sepsis -> treat in hospital
- Comorbidities that increase the risk of PO ABx failure (e.g. obesity, venous insufficiency, PVD, CCF) -> treat through HITH
- Failure of oral antibiotic therapy, once mimics have been excluded

Note that in the majority of patients, evidence of inflammation can persist for weeks.

In patients at high risk of recurrent cellulitis, the _PATCH-1_ and _PATCH-2_ trials found that low-dose penicillin VK reduced the risk of recurrence. Additionally, compression stockings to reduce the level of oedema in the legs showed a profound reduction in recurrence of cellulitis.
