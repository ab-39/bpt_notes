### Overview
Antibodies are the key component of the humoral immune system, and play a key role in defending the organism against encapsulated organisms (e.g. _S. pneumoniae, H. influenzae, E. coli, Giardia lamblia_) by:
- _Opsonisation_ - IgG binding to the surface of microorganisms to trigger phagocytosis by neutrophils and macrophages
- _Complement activation_ - activation of the classical pathway (C1q) through antibody/antigen complexes 

Humoral responses are dependent on three cell types:
- Antigen presenting cells - expressing MHCII to present antigen to CD4+ T-cells
- CD4+ T-cells - activate B-lymphocytes at the borders of the B-cell follicles, requires CD40/40L costimulation
- B-cells - stimulate antibody response, and eventually mature into plasma cells
- Plasma cells

### B-Cell Physiology
Initially, mature B-cells secrete low-affinity antibodies: IgM, pentameric, low-affinity, high-avidity antibodies -> these are the most potent activators of the classical pathway of complement. They also express IgD, which lies immediately 3’ of the locus of IgM. 

Once activated and secreting IgM, B-cells are also triggered to begin the process of class switching to higher affinity antibodies. This occurs after a stepwise process:
- B-cells endocytose antigen
- They process and present them on MHC II
- This is detected by CD4+ cells, which triggers a CD40/CD40L interaction 
- This triggers the release of cytokines from the helper T cell that triggers the process of class switching
This will also push both the B- and Th-cells towards becoming memory cells. The memory response serves to expedite the immune response against a pathogen on subsequent exposures - reducing it from 5+ days to <2 days. 

#### B-Cell Subsets
- B1 - respond to antigens without T-cell help, but the antibody is restricted to the IgM isotype
  - Do _not_ become memory cells
  - Express CD5
- B2 - activated B-cells that respond to T-dependent antigens, undergo class switching so all isotypes of antibody can be produced
  - Produce memory cells
  - Do _not_ express CD5

#### Generation of Antibody Diversity
There are seven gene segments that combine to encode a complete antibody molecule:
- Heavy chain segments - V, D, J, C
  - There are more V segments (~100) than any other
  - C segment (on Ch14) = μ, γ, α, δ, or ε (which, helpfully, correspond to the relevant Ig class)
- Light chain segments - V, J, C
  - C segment = κ (Ch2) or λ (Ch22)
  - Typically, the κ segment is encoded first, and only progresses to λ if there is failure -> there is normally a κ:λ ratio of 2:1

The first three processes that contrbute to immunoglobulin diversity occur during the generation of antibodies, prior to the maturation of B-cells:
1. Rearrangements between different V, D, and J segments
2. Junctional diversity at the joints of the different gene segments
3. Combinatorial diveristy between different heavy and light chains

The construction of a unique antigen receptor takes four steps:
- V(D)J recombination
- Transcription
- Splicing
- Translation
![](Humoral%20Immunity/image%203.png)
This process is effected by recombinase enzymes that are encoded by RAG genes - deficiencies of which lead to humoral immunodeficiencies. The first antibody generated in this manner will be an IgM that serves as the B-cell receptor. After generation, this is followed by _allelic exclusion_, in which other possible antibodies are suppressed. As the B-cell matures, it then also expresses IgD. 

The final step that leads to increased diversity is **somatic hypermutation**:
![](Humoral%20Immunity/image%202.png)
This process is mediated by _activation-induced cytidine deaminase_, which binds to and deliberately introduces uridine, a molecule foreign to DNA, into the gene structure of the V region. These mutations and the bases adjacent are then removed by the mismatch repair proteins MSH2 and MSH6, and the ‘hole’ is ‘filled in’ by DNA polymerases. Other processes are mediated by base-excision (e.g. PARP) proteins. The process of somatic hypermutation occurs in the germinal centres of the lymphoid tissues, and leads through a Darwinian process of the antibodies becoming ultimately _even more_ specific for their targets. This is referred to as affinity maturation (note that this process does not occur in T-cells, which only generate diversity through gene recombination).

### Antibody Structure
Antibodies are composed of four chains:
- Heavy chains:
  - Encode antibody isotype
  - Site of complement binding
  - Contribute to antigen binding = V + D + J segments
- Light chains:
  - Contribute to antigen binding = V + J segments
![](Humoral%20Immunity/image.png)
Each antibody is made up of two sections:
- Fab = the antigen-binding fragment = the variable region (with the antigen binding site) with a small portion of the constant region
- Fc = the crystallisable fragment = the majority of the constant region

### Assessment of Humoral Immunity 
Several tests can assess humoral immunity:
- Serum immunoglobulins
  - IgG +/- subclasses
    - Need to exclude secondary causes of low Ig with serum and urine protein and albumin
    - Also consider haematological malignancies 
    - In terms of circulating quantities, expect IgG > A > M > D > E
  - IgA
    - Dimeric antibodies that bind to a secretory piece to persist on mucosal epithelia to neutralise virus
    - The IgA in the plasma is different from the IgA in the secretions
    - Coated with glycans to trap pathogens 
  - IgM
    - The most potent activator of complement: M > G3 > G1 > G2 
  - IgE 
- Lymphocyte counts and subsets
  - Absent B-cells _may_ indicate X-linked agammagloblulinaemia (or [[common variable immunodeficiency]])
  - Low T-cells is suggestive of combined immunodeficiency 
- Functional antibody testing - functional antibody testing pre-and-post vaccination
- Specific antibody responses 
- Lymphocyte proliferation studies
- Bone marrow aspirate
