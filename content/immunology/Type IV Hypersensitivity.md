# Type IV Hypersensitivity
#bpt/immunology #bpt/racp_cls
- - -
### Overview
Type IV [[hypersensitivity reactions]] are caused by T-cell mediated attack, typically an hour or more (up to days or weeks!) after exposure to the offending antigen. The presentation can range from relatively mild maculopapular or morbiliform rash to severe skin reactions such as SJS-TEN syndrome. 

These typically involve T-cell receptor binding either to:
- Haptens - small chemically reactive molecule binding covalently to proteins or peptides
- Prohaptens - drugs that are not chemically active, but which become reactive during metabolism 
- - -
### Diagnosis
Delayed hypersensitivity reactions can be investigated using lymphocyte transformation tests  and patch tests, but these tend to have low sensitivity. 
- - -
### Severe Cutaneous Adverse Events
Include:
- Acute generalised exanthematous pustulosis
  - Often have neutrophilia with raised inflammatory markers 
  - Biopsy shows sub corneal pustules filled with neutrophils
- Drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms
  - Develops over several days 2-8 weeks after administration of the offending agent
  - Strong association with vancomycin, anticonvulsants, sulphonamide antibiotics, and some gout treatments 
  - HLA associations:
    - Vancomycin - HLA-A32:01
  - Systemic symptoms include fever, widespread rash, facial swelling, and cervical lymphadenopathy
  - Rarely, there can be hepatitis, pneumonitis, and interstitial nephritis 
  - There is typically a leucocytosis with eosinophilia, and atypical lymphocytes on blood film
- SJS-TEN overlap 
  - Commonly implicated drugs include sulphonamides, anticonvulsants, β-lactam antibiotics, allopurinol, and NSAIDs
  - There are specific HLA associations with certain ethnic groups:
    - Allopurinol - HLA-B58:01
    - Carbamazepine - HLA-B15:02
  - Mucosal involvement can be severe
  - Pathology is defined by epidermal detachment
  - There is often a significant fever
