### Overview
Unlike diabetes mellitus, which is an deficiency or resistance to insulin, _diabetes insipidus_ is a problem of water balance due to deficiency or resistance to antidiuretic hormone (AVP). These two states are now helpfully called AVP-D (formerly central diabetes insipidus) and AVP-R (formerly nephrogenic diabetes insipidus). 

#### AVP-Deficiency
The clinical presentation of AVP-D is polydipsia and polyuria. If intake cannot keep up with loss, the patient eventually develops hypernatraemia. In some cases, AVP-D can only become ‘unmasked’ by glucocorticoid replacement. Although AVP-D can often transiently follow pituitary surgery, it is very rare for it to arise due to a pituitary adenoma. It is, however, more common with other sorts of tumours such as craniopahryngiomas. 

AVP-D has traditionally been diagnosed with the water deprivation test. In a healthy individual, overnight deprivation of water should trigger release of ADH and increase the urine osmolality. This does not happen in AVP-D. Of late, however, an immunoassay against _copeptin_, the C-terminal copeptide of AVP has been developed. This has allowed copeptin stimulation with hypertonic saline or arginine to be developed as a test for AVP-D. 

Treatment is with desmopressin:
- Subcutaneously in the acute phase after pituitary surgery
- Orally or intranasally in the long term 1-3 times a day

### Diagnostic Approach
1. Confirm hypotonic polyuria:
   - Polyuria = >50mL/kg/d
   - Polydipsia = >3L/d 
   - Hypotonic urine = uOsm <300mOsm/kg
2. uOsm >750mOsm/kg -> diabetes insipidus ruled out
   - -> need to exclude other causes of polyuria
3. Check serum sodium:
   - sNa >147mmol/L (or sOsm >300mOsm/kg) -> diabetes insipidus
   - sNa <135mmol/L (or sOsm <280mmol/L) -> primary polydipsia
4. Equivocal bloods -> proceed to water deprivation test or copeptin stimulation test
