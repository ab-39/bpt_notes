### Overview
![](Parathyroid%20Hormone/image.png) 
Parathyroid hormone is produced by the chief cells of the parathyroid glands in response to reduced levels of serum ionised calcium, sensed by the same calcium-sensing receptor (CaSR) found in the loop of Henle. Chronic hypomagnesaemia, elevated levels of FGF23, and elevated levels of calcitriol all inhibit PTH release, while high levels of phosphate stimulate it (recall: PTH is the **P**hosphate **T**rashing **H**ormone). 

This leads to four key downstream effects:
- Increased hydroxylation of 25-OH-cholecalciferol in the kidneys -> increased 1,25-OH-cholecalciferol (calcitriol) in the serum -> increased intestinal absorption of calcium
  - Note that the pathway is that cholecalciferol is synthesised in the skin, and ‘activated’ by sunlight, following which it is transported to the liver and hydroxylated for the first time into 25-OH-cholecalciferol
- Increased bone resorption -> increased release of calcium into the plasma
- Increased transcellular renal tubular reabsorption of calcium -> decreased urinary loss of calcium
  - i.e. _Not_ via paracellular effusion
- Decreased renal tubular reabsorption of phosphate -> increased urinary loss of phosphate -> decreased plasma phosphate concentration -> reduced binding of calcium
  - Note that phosphate is implicated in secondary hyperparathyroidism in CKD, as phosphate retention occurs with renal impairment, leading to increased calcium binding -> reduced serum calcium -> increased PTH secretion
