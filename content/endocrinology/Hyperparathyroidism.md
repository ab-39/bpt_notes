### Overview
Hyperparathyroidism is a relatively common condition, affecting 20/100,000 people/y, and predominantly women. It is most commonly due to a single functional adenoma, or else four-gland hyperplasia. Multiple adenomas and cancer of the parathyroid gland are relatively rare. The most common [[hereditary cancer syndromes]] associated with hyperparathyroidism are MEN1 and MEN2, with the former having 75% penetrance by age 50y and 100% penetrance overall. Familial hypocalciuric hypercalcaemia is an important differential that is differentiated from hyperparathyroidism on the basis of its low urinary calcium-creatinine ratio (<0.01). 

The clinical syndrome is typically due to the secondarily elevated levels of calcium, with the “classic” symptoms of “bones, stones, groans, and moans”, as well as polyuria. Bone mineral density is reduced, most commonly at areas with high levels of cortical bone such as the distal radius and the proximal femur, although fracture risk is elevated at all sites. Relevant biomarkers include:
- Elevated serum/ionised calcium
- Hypophosphataemia (~25%)
- Raised or normal PTH
- Raised or normal 24h urinary calcium (RR = 100-300mg/d)

Ultrasound or sestamibi scans can be used to localise a parathyroid adenoma, followed by a 4DCT if unsuccessful.

Parathyroidectomy is indicated when:
- Young patients (<50y)
- Serum calcium >0.25mmol/L above the upper limit of normal (~2.9mmol/L)
- Evidence of osteoporosis or vertebral fractures
- CKD
- Recurrent [[renal calculi]]
- Significant neurocognitive symptoms or functional impairments 

A key complication of parathyroidectomy is ‘hungry bone disease’ which leads to profound, life-thretening hypocalcaemia postoperatively. One treatment strategy for this is loading with vitamin D preoperatively. 

Other therapies to consider include:
- Bisphosphonates if osteoporosis
- Cinacalcet (i.e. synthetic calcium) to stimulate the CaSR and reduce PTH levels - typically in patients who are unfit for surgery
