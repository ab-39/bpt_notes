### Overview
Calcium is critical for structural and functional roles in the body:
- Structural - component of hydroxyapatite, found in bones and teeth
- Functional - critical intracellular secondary messenger for:
  - Muscle contraction
  - Presynaptic neurotransmitter release
  - Endocrine/exocrine secretion
  - Apoptosis 
  - Mitochondrial energy production
  - Cell proliferation
  - Leucocyte activation and phagocytosis
- Cofactor - factor IV in the clotting cascade

In the circulation, calcium is found in three forms:
- Ionised = free = biologically active (on a VBG) = 1.1-1.13mmol/L
- Protein-bound (usu. albumin)  =biologically inactive = 0.9-1.0mmol/L
- Complexed with PO4, HCO3, and citrate = 0.2-0.3mmol/L

Ionised and complexed calcium are filtered at the kidney at a rate of ~10g/d, of which ~9.8g is reabsorbed, primarily in the proximal tubule and thick ascending limb as outlined below.

### Calcium Homoeostasis
The major regulatory hormone of calcium is [[endocrinology/Parathyroid Hormone|parathyroid hormone]], which is released in response to low serum calcium and acts on the bones and kidney to increase calcium levels by two mechanisms:
- Direct release from the bone = _resorption_ 
- _Reabsorption_ in the kidneys (predominantly TAL and DCT)

Additionally, low calcium is also sensed by CaSRs in the kidney, activating both calcium reabsorption and 1⍺-hydroxylase activity, activating vitamin D and thus promoting calcium _absorption_ in the gut.

Note that PTHrP is not always pathological, as it is in fact released by lactating women to allow for the rapid release of calcium into milk. 

#### The Calcium Sensing Receptor 
CaSR is a G-protein heterodimer that is found in the parathyroid glands and the kidneys. It ‘traps’ circulating free calcium, which activates the G-protein -> PLC -> cAMP inhibition cascade, with two key outcomes:
- Parathyroids -> reduced PTH secretion
- Kidney -> increased urinary calcium excretion 

Activation of the CaSR in the kidney leads to inhibition of ROMK channels, which reduces the level of urinary potassium, which leads to secondarily reduced NKCC2 activation and consequently reduced transepithelial voltage. This reduces the electrochemical gradient required for paracellular effusion of calcium and magnesium by claudin 16/19 mediated mechanisms, overall leading to reduced reabsorption of calcium and reduced serum levels of calcium.
![](Calcium/image%202.png)
Deficiency of the CaSR leads to familial hypocalciuric hypercalcaemia, an autosomal dominant dominant condition that is characterised by a urinary calcium-creatinine ratio <0.01:
1. Reduced CaSR activity
2. Increased ROMK activity
3. Increased NKCC2 activity
4. Increased electrochemical gradient
5. Increased calcium reabsorption
