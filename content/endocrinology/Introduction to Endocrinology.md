### Key Emergencies in Endocrinology
1. Severe [[renal/Hyponatraemia|Hyponatraemia]] - typically presents with neurological symptoms: confusion, drowsiness, seizures, coma, etc.
2. Severe [[Hypercalcaemia]] - thirst, dehydration, polyuria
3. Hypoadrenal/[[Addisonian Crisis]] - collapse, postural hypotension, hypoglycaemia; Hx of chronic steroid use
4. Rapid Dehydration in [[Diabetes Insipidus]] - dehydration with polyuria, thirst, drowsiness, coma, seizures; Hx of chronic lithium use
5. Pituitary Apoplexy - sudden headache; Hx of pituitary tumour
6. [[Thyrotoxicosis]] - fever, tachycardia/tachyarrhythmia, anxiety, tremor
7. Severe [[Hypothyroidism]] - bradycardia, hypotension, drowsiness, coma
8. Hypoglycaemia.- sweating, confusion, drowsiness, coma, seizures; Hx of insulin or sulfonylurea use
9. [[Diabetic Ketoacidosis]] - vomiting, fever, dehydration, acidosis with Kussmaul respiration, hyperglycaemia; Hx of [[type I diabetes mellitus]] or SGLT2i use
10. [[Hyperglycaemic Hyperosmolar State]] - drowsiness, coma, dehydration, hyperglycaemia, signs of intercurrent illness; Hx of [[Type 2 Diabetes|type II diabetes mellitus]]

### Overview of Endocrinology
#### Hormonal Activity
Fundamentally, the endocrine system consists of glands that exert their actions at distant parts of the body via the production of biologically active messengers known as _hormones_. These molecules can be either polypeptides, glycoproteins, steroids, or amines. Hormones are typically transcribed or synthesised, and then secreted into the systemic (or pituitary portal) circulation to exert their effect, either bound to a transport protein, or ‘free’ (and biologically active). Hormones exert their action via activating receptors, which fall into two main classes:
1. Membrane receptors:
   - G-protein coupled receptors - activate a downstream signalling cascade ultimately leading to protein phosphorylation through one of several pathways (e.g. cAMP, PLC, DAG) - typically how peptide hormones act
   - Dimeric transmembrane receptors - directly phosphorylate intracellular messengers after being hormones - e.g. growth hormone, prolactin, IGF-1
2. Nuclear receptors:
   - Dissolved in the cytosol, these bind steroid hormones and translocate to the nucleus to directly bind and active/inactivate sections of DNA, leading to changes in protein transcription - e.g. steroids and thyroid hormone
Once activated, these receptors lead to the release of hormones, either from a preformed state in vesicles or through stimulation of production. In the systemic circulation, hormones then act on their receptors, and typically also inhibit their own formation through negative feedback loops. 

#### What can go wrong?
The delicate balance of the endocrine system can be disrupted in a number of ways:
- Primary gland failure - disorder of the endocrine end-organ (e.g. destruction of the thyroid gland) leading to deficiency of the hormone, a loss of negative feedback, and elevation of the corresponding anterior pituitary hormone
  - e.g. Hashimoto disease -> hypothyroidism -> elevated TSH
- Secondary gland failure - (typically) disorder of the pituitary gland, leading to low or ‘inappropriately normal’ levels of the pituitary trophic hormone _as well as_ low end-organ hormone levels 
  - e.g. pituitary adenoma -> low ACTH -> low cortisol
- Hormone excess - can arise in two patterns:
  - Elevated end-organ hormone and low pituitary hormone -> exogenous administration or primary end-organ overactivity (e.g. Grave’s disease)
  - Elevated end-organ hormone and high pituitary hormone -> pituitary overactivity (e.g. Cushing’s disease)
- Hormone resistance - typically typified by elevated end-organ hormone levels with a non-suppressed pituitary hormone level

#### Measuring hormones 
Hormones can be measured by direct assays, and through classes of special tests. It is possible to measure the levels of both end-organ and pituitary hormones directly in the serum, but hypothalamic hormones are typically present in too small concentrations to do so. 

Endocrinology also makes use of dynamic tests:
- Stimulation tests: assessing for hormone deficiency by administrating a pituitary hormone (analogue) and measuring the change in serum end-organ hormone levels - e.g. the short synacthen test for adrenal insufficiency 
- Suppression tests: assessing for hormone excess by administering an end-organ hormone and measuring the change in serum pituitary hormone levels - e.g. the dexamethasone suppression test for Cushing’s disease 

Other common tests in endocrinology involve urine collection, saliva collection, and medical imaging.

#### Patterns of hormone secretion 
At a basic level, hormones are released in either a continuous (e.g. thyroid hormone) or pulsatile (e.g. FSH/LH) fashion. This can be complicated slightly in the case of cortisol, which follows a circadian pattern. This is typically modulated by factors such as stress, sleep, and feeding/fasting.
