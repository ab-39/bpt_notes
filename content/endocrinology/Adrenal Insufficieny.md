# Adrenal Insufficieny
#bpt/endocrinology
- - -
### Overview
Adrenal insufficiency is a relatively uncommon condition, affecting ~1/10,000 people and peaking in the fourth decade of life. In the developing world, most of the burden of disease is due to tuberculosis, while in the developed world, [[endocrinology/Autoimmune Endocrine Disease|autoimmune]] destructino of the 21-hydoxylase enzyme accounts for >90% of adrenal insufficiency, either as isolated cases (40%) or part of the [[Defects of Immune Dysregulation#Autoimmune Polyendocrinopathy/Candidiasis/Ectodermal Dysplasia Syndrome|APECED]] syndrome (60%). HLA-DR3/DQ2 and -DR4/DQ8 confer significantly increased risk for autoimmune adrenalitis. 

Other, less common causes of adrenal insufficiency include:
- X-linked adrenoleukodystrophy - accumulation of VLFAs due to mutations in the _ALD_ gene
- Congenital adrenal hyperplasia - enzymatic deficiencies most common in early childhood
- Adrenal hypoplasia congenita
- Familal glucocorticoid deficiency
- Bilateral adrenal haemorrhage - most often in the setting of meningococcaemia or the antiphospholipid syndrome
- Adrenal infiltration

### Clinical Features
Clinical features of adrenal insufficiency include:
- Decreased appetite
- Unintentional weight loss
- Nausea and abdominal pain
- Fatigue, lethargy
- Salt-craving
- Orthostatic hypotension
- Hypoglycaemia (particularly in children)
- Hyperpigmentation (due to ACTH deriving from proopiomelanocortin)
- Hyponatraemia and hyperkalaemia
- Increased TSH (due to loss of cortisol inhibition)

### Diagnosis
The gold standard test is the short synacthen test, in which the administration of tetracosactide, a SYNthetic ACTH molecule, should result in cortisol levels _greater_ than 400nmol/L. In equivocal cases, the insulin-induced hypoglycaemia test can be used (expect cortisol >500nmol/L), although this is rarely used in clinical practice due to the side-effects of hypoglycaemia. 

Additional signs include:
- Elevated serum ACTH
- Reduced aldosterone
- Elevated renin
- Low DHEAS
- Positive anti-21 hydroxylase antibodies

In antibody negative cases, consider testing for:
- VLFAs (in males)
- Imaging the adrenals
- Antiphospholipid antibodies

### Treatment
Treatment is with glucocorticoid replacement, usually with hydrocortisone:
- 5-10mg/sqm of BSA
- = ~15-25mg/d
- Divided doses to mimic physiologic diurnal variations, e.g.
  - 10mg mane
  - 5mg midi
  - 5mg evening
- Requirements increase when:
  - Sick
  - Pregnant (third trimester)

Mineralocorticoids may also need to be replaced, as in some cases these may also be deficient. The usual starting dose is ~100microg/d, and this is titrated until renin (which is inhibited by aldosterone activity) is in the upper end of the normal range. 
> Note that of the steroids available, hydrocortisone has the highest mineralocorticoid activity. So, when treating a patient in an Addisonian crisis with high doses of hydrocortisone, mineralocorticoid replacement is not needed.

> [!NOTE] Stress Dosing
> - Mild illness -> continue current dose of steroids
> - Moderate illness -> increase dose to ~50mg/d hydrocortisone equivalent in a divided dose (roughly double the dose, or triple if vomiting/diarrhoea)
> - Severe illness -> IV hydrocortisone 100mg bolus followed by IV 50mg QID 
> Note that at doses ≥40mg/d hydrocortisone typically has enough mineralocorticoid activity to not require additional replacement.

Progesterone has an anti-mineralocorticoid effect, so in the third trimester of pregnanct fludrocortisone doses may need to be increased.

The evidence for DHEA (adrenal androgen) replacemnet is conflicting, but it may be trialled in women to improve mood.
