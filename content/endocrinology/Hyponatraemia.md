# Hyponatraemia
#bpt/endocrinology #bpt/renal
- - -
### Physiological Basis
Hyponatraemia is a disorder of water balance, that manifests as low serum sodium. This can occur in two situations:
- The body is losing water (and salt) -> low volume _and_ low sodium
- The body is retaining water (but not salt) -> normal or high volume with low sodium 
- - -
### Diagnostic Approach
#1 = ensure the patient is hypotonic:
- Only effective osmoles affect tonicity - i.e., other than sodium, high levels of glucose and mannitol can cause hyponatraemia in a corrective effort (isotonic)
  - Inffective osmoles spread equally across compartments and thus do not ‘pull’ water with them - e.g. alcohol, urea, etc. 
- Additionally, large molecules such as lipids or immunoglobulins can interfere with the assay (pseudohyponatraemia = hypertonic)

#2 = check the urine osmolality - this is a marker of urine concentration and thus ADH activity, which _should_ be in response to a hypotonic state:
- Normal urine osmolality ranges between 50mOsm/kg (maximally diluted) to 1200mOsm/kg (maximally concentrated), which also depends on the patient’s solute load 
- In contrast, the serum osmolality _does_ have a normal range: 275-290mOsm/kg, which will influence the urinary osmolality accordingly -> _incongruence_ between the urine and serum osmolality is the real problem here 
  - If the sOsm is _not_ low -> go back to step #1!
  - uOsm >100mOsm/kg = high -> abormal response to hypotonia (the body should be trying to _conserve_ solutes)
    - In SIADH this can be as high as _serum_ osmolality 
  - uOsm <100mOsm/kg = low -> appropriate response to hyponatraemia

#3 = check the urine sodium - this is a marker of RAAS activity, which should be activated in response to a hypovolaemic (or _perceived_ hypovolaemic state):
- uNa >40mmol/L = sodium wasting, e.g.:
  - Thiazide diuretics
  - SIADH
  - Hypothyroidism
  - Cerebral salt wasting
  - Mineralocorticoid deficiency
- uNa <20mmol/L = (attempted) sodium retention (in response to perceived hypovolaemia), e.g.:
  - Heart failure
  - Cirrhosis
  - Hypovolaemia
  - Polydipsia 
  - Low dietary osmolar load (e.g. beer potomania, tea and toast, etc.)

Putting these together should allow a group of diagnoses to be arrived at while bypassing the pesky fluid status examination.

### Management
Management depends on the aetiology of the hyponatraemia:
- Appropriately high ADH = hyponatraemia is being cause by increased body volume (even though the lack of perfusion in e.g. CCF may cause secondary RAAS activation) -> address the volume issue 
- Low or inappropriately high ADH (e.g. primary polydipsia, tea and toast) = hyponatraemia is being caused by solute-water mismatch -> fluid restriction +/- adequate nutrition

In general, chronic hyponatraemia should be corrected slowly (6-10mmol/L/d) to avoid the risk of osmotic demyelination. The exceptions, however, are:
- If there is moderate hyponatraemia (121-130mmol/L) **and** symptoms
- If there is severe hyponatraemia (≤120mmol/L)
