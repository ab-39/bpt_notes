# Physiology of the Pituitary and Hypophysis 
#bpt/endocrinology
- - -
### Overview
The ‘conductors of the endocrine orchestra’ are the hypothalamus and pituitary gland, the former of which sits in the brain at the base of the third ventricle below the optic chiasm, and is connected to the pituitary gland, below in the sella turcica. The hypothalamus is part of the ventral diencephalon, and contains nuclei that regulate a variety of functions such as appetite, thirst, thermoregulation, and sleeping/waking. It releases hormones into the portal hypophyseal system that act on the pituitary gland to stimulate or inhibit hormone release.  Embryologically, the anterior pituitary  (adenohypophysis) is formed from oral ectoderm (Rathke’s pouch), while the posterior pituitary (neurohypophysis) is formed from neural ectoderm. The neurohypophysis is connected to specific hypothalamic nuclei which regulate the release of vasopressin and oxytocin. The adenohypophysis is made up of three cell types:
- Chromophils: stain with the respective dyes, and secrete hormones:
  - Acidophils -> secrete prolactin and growth hormone
  - Basophils -> secrete FSH, LH, ACTH, and TSH (“B-FLAT”)
- Chromophobes: do not stain, and do not secrete hormones
- - -
### Embryology
The pituitary gland arises from the oral ectoderm via Rathke’s pouch. Genes critical to this process include:
- _HesX-1_ -> affects everything + causes septo-optic dysplasia
- _Prop-1_ -> affects everything
- _Pit-1_ -> affects GH, TSH, and prolactin
Variants of these genes in early development can lead to congenital [[hypopituitarism]].
