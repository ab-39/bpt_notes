# Grave Disease
#bpt/endocrinology
- - -
### Epidemiology
- Uncommon ~20-50/100,000 people/y
- Generally a disease of middle age
- Sixfold predilection for women
- Highly heritable, likely related to HLA-DR3
- Environmental risk factors include: iodine, smoking, alcohol, stress, and infections
- - -
### Pathogenesis
Grave disease is a B-cell mediated disease, where the key interaction is the presentation of antigens from T-cell to B-cell with the costimtulatory interaction between CD154 (on T-cells) and CD40 (on B-cells). This leads to the production of anti-TSH-receptor antibodies - colloquially, _TRABs_ - which bind to the TSH receptor on the thyroid and stimulate the release of thyroid hormone. 
![](Grave%20Disease/image.png)
Grave orbitopathy arises due to the presence of TSH receptors on retro-orbital fibroblasts. On binding of TRABs, this leads to secretion of glycosaminoglycans, which results in swelling of the retro-orbital tissue, leading to the classic findings of proptosis, impaired extraocular muscle relaxation, and diplopia.

Drugs associated with the development of Grave disease include:
- Lithium
- Antiretroviral therapy 
- Alemtuzumab
- [[oncology/Immune Checkpoint Inhibition|Immune Checkpoint Inhibition]] 
- - -
### Treatment
In general, Grave disease is fairly responsive to treatment, with about 50% of patients achieving remission. Good prognostic factors include age over forty, mild disease (fT4 <40), low TRAB levels (<3x ULN), and the absence of goitre. 

First line treatment is with _carbimazole_, a prodrug for methimazole, which in turn prevents TPO from iodinating thyroglobulin, inhibiting the synthesis of thyroid hormone. This is usually given for a 12-18 month course, after which it is discontinued to determine if the patient has achieved remission.

PTU is generally not used, with two exceptions:
- [[obstetrics/Thyroid Disease in Pregnancy#Grave Disease|Pregnancy]]
- Thyrotoxic crisis - inhibits conversion of T4 to T3 in the periphery

For relapsed Grave disease, options include:
- Radioactive iodine (generally first second-line option) - causes thyroid cell damage and death. 
  - Contraindicated in: active Grave orbitopathy, pregnancy, breastfeeding
  - Hypothyroidism eventually develops in most patients
  - No risk of cancer
  - Can be given in orbitopathy if absolutely necessary under steroid cover
- Total thyroidectomy
  - Safe, but requires lifelong treatment with thyroxine
- Long-term carbimazole (can increase rate of remission at 5y)
- Iscalimab - anti CD40 antibody that blocks B-cell activation. 

Risks of carbimazole include:
- Agranulocytosis
- Rash 
- Arthralgia
- Pancreatitis

Risks of PTU include:
- Hepatotoxicity
- ANCA vasculitis 

Treatment specific to orbitopathy includes:
- Smoking cessation - independent risk factor
- Selenium
- Severe disease:
  - IV methylprednisolone
  - Additional immunosuppression or local radiotherapy
  - Tocilizumab
  - _**Teprotumumab**_ - anti-IGF1R
  - If refractory -> urgent surgical decompression
