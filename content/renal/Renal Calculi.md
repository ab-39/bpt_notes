### Epidemiology
In terms of prevalence:
- Calcium oxalate - ~70%
- Calcium phosphate - ~15%
- Uric acid - ~10%
- Struvite  = magensium/ammonium/phosphate 
- Cystine
- - -
### Aetiology
#### Calcium Oxalate
Can occur in healthy people due to the presence of _Randall’s plaques_ - aggregations of calcium and oxalate in the renal papillae. Risk factors include:
- Low urine volume
- High urine calcium -> high salt diet 
- High urine oxalate -> animal protein, leafy greens, nuts, vitamin C, low calcium diet, etc.
  - Fat malabsorption
  - Primary hyperoxaluria
  - Ethylene glycol toxicity
- Hyperuricosuria - uric acid is so soluble in urine at normal pH, it can actually force calcium out of solution -> calcium stones 
- Low urine citrate - citrate chelates calcium, preventing it from forming stones

#### Uric Acid
Almost exclusively occurs at low urine pH (acidic) levels, as otherwise uric acid is _highly_ soluble in the urine. Key risk factor is metabolic syndrome/insulin resistance -> reduce ammonia production -> increased free protons in the urine. 

#### Struvite
Generally caused by urease producing bacteria during a UTI, particularly _Proteus_ spp., but also _Pseudomonas_, _Providencia_, _Morganella_, and _Klebsiella_ spp. These organisms are able to break down urea to ammonia, which alkalinises the urine, forcing struvite into solution. 

#### Calcium Phosphate
Typically tend to occur in the context of an underlying medical condition - e.g.:
- Type I [[Renal Tubular Acidosis|RTA]]
- [[Hyperparathyroidism]]
- Iatrogenic alkalisation of the urine
Risk factors include low urine volume, high urine calcium, low urine citrate, high urine phosphate, and basic urine. 

> [!NOTE] DDx
> A rare differential for kidney stones is autosomal recessive APRT deficiency, a problem with the purine salvage pathway. This leads to adenine being metabolised into 2,8-DHA by xanthine oxidase and forming kidney stones that are insoluble at any pH. Treatment is with allopurinol.

### Diagnosis
#### Urine
There are three key aspects of the urine to consider when approaching renal calculi:
- Volume 
- Urine pH - different solutes precipitate at different levels of acidity:
  - Acidic urine (pH <5.5) -> uric acid stones, cystine stones
  - Normal urine (pH 5.5-6.5) -> calcium oxalate stones
  - Basic urine (pH >6.5) -> calcium phosphate stones, struvote stones 
- Solutes and solubility

### Management
#1 = drink more fluid (2-2.5L _or more_ per day) -> targeting >2.2L/d urine 

Stones <5mm generally pass by themselves, while stones 5-10mm may need intervention if located proximally. Stones >10mm generally require intervention (e.g. lithotripsy) to pass. 

This advice applies to all patients. Otherwise, management is typically only required if patients are presenting with stones recurrently (typically defined as ≥3 stones in 5y). Management needs to be tailored to the _stone_ and the _urine_. 
- Calcium stones -> low salt diet, minimise hypercalcaemia, allopurinol (see above), citrate (30-80mEq/L/d, or 60mL BD of lemon juice)
  - Note that dietary calcium can be used to bind oxalate in the gut in patients with hyperoxaluria, particularly in the case of fat malabsorption (which promotes oxalate uptake)
- Uric acid stones -> reduce animal protein, alkalinise the urine

Recent evidence found that hydrochlorothiazide was _not_ more effective than placebo with respect to preventing stone recurrence.
