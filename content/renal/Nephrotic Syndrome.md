### What is nephrotic syndrome?
Nephrotic syndrome is a protein-wasting state of the kidney that is defined by:
- Proteinuria - >3.5g/day (some say >3g/day)
  - Classic EM finding is ‘podocyte foot process effacement’ 
  - PCR 90mg/mmol -> ~1g/24h
  - ACR superior for vascular prognosis
- Hypoalbuminaemia 
  - Develops as a consequence of both protein loss and increased catabolism of reabsorbed protein
- Generalised oedema 
  - Due to secondary sodium retention rather than reduced oncotic pressure, as classically thought
- Hyperlipidaemia 
  - Due to up-regulation of PCSK9

There is also an increased risk of thrombosis due to increased urinary loss of anticoagulant proteins along with a relative increase in hepatic procoagulant synthesis, and an increased risk of infection due to immunoglobulin loss. The general threshold for anticoagulation is albumin < 20g/L. 

Management is centred around improving proteinuria:
- Salt restriction
- RAAS blockade
- Mineralocorticoid antagonist
- SGLT2 inhibition
Additionally, loop diuretics can be used (at high doses) to improve fluid status. Anticoagulation and management of dyslipidaemia should be also considered.

### Causes of the Nephrotic Syndrome
#### Minimal Change Disease 
MCD is characterised by heavy oedema with a normal histopathologic appearance of the glomeruli (hence, minimal change) and podocyte foot process effacement on SEM. The proteinuria is typically ‘selective’, i.e. consisting of predominantly albumin. 

MCD is most common in male children, and is the most common cause of nephrotic syndrome in childhood. In adults, MCD is responsible for ~10-20% of nephrotic syndrome, and rarely progresses to CKD. 

Treatment is generally with high-dose glucocorticoids, with immunosuppression (e.g. CYC, MMF, rituximab) also used in resistant cases. 

#### Finnish Type Congenital Nephrotic Syndrome
This is an autosomal recessive disorder caused due to mutations in the transmembrane protein nephrin. It is characterised by an increase in ⍺FP in the amniotic fluid, and a relentless progression to CKD. It occurs in ~1/8000 live births in Finland. 

#### Focal Segmental Glomerulosclerosis
FSGS is a pattern of sclerotic glomerular lesions that affect some but not all (i.e. focal) glomeruli, and some but not all (i.e. segmental) regions of the tuft. Primary FSGS is a rare but severe cause of nephrotic syndrome attributed to a yet-unknown circulating permeability factor - possible culprits include anti-nephrin antibodies, suPAR, CLCF1, or an uncharacterised miRNA. Histopathology is characterised by segmental glomerulosclerosis which falls into five variants:
1. Classic FSGS
2. Glomerular tip lesion - sclerosis clustered at the tubular pole, marker of good response to steroids
3. Collapsing FSGS - enlarged and vacuolated podocytes, associated with HIV
4. Perihilar FSGS - associated with secondary FSGS
5. Cellular variant - characterised by proliferation 

Management is with steroids for at least six months, and resistance is common, typically requiring long term immunosuppression with TAC/CYC. 50% of patients progress to ESKD within ten years, with the remaining 50% following them in the next ten years. Post-transplant recurrence is common, and typically treated with PLEX. There is also evidence for anti-CD80 antibodies (e.g. abatacept) and topical ACTH gel.

Secondary FSGS can arise as the final common pathway from a number of pathological processes.

#### Membranous Nephropathy
MN is the a common cause of nephrotic syndrome in adults, particularly men. It is associated with antibodies against anti-PLA2R, a glycoprotein constituent of the podocyte. Serum anti-PLA2R antibody has a high specificity for diagnosing PMN, and any changein the serum anti-PLA2R antibody level is closely related to the status of the PMN - i.e. if the anti-PLA2R antibody level has decreased, it indicates that the condition has improved; and if the serum anti-PLA2R antibody continues to show high levels of positive or quantitative increase, the condition is not in remission or has relapsed. In 10% of PLA2R-negative patients, anti-THSD7A or anti-NELL1 may be positive. 

Secondary (i.e. seronegative) membranous nephropathy can be due to drugs (e.g. penicilliamine, gold, NSAIDs), autoimmune diseases, cancer, or infections. Histopathology is characterised by a ‘spiky’ basement membrane when staining with periodic acid-Schiff. 

One-third of patients spontaneously remit, one-third respond well to immunosuppression (CsA, anti-CD20) and one-third progress to CKD. All patients should receive ACE inhibition. The [*MENTOR* trial](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1814427) found rituximab to be non-inferior to cyclosporine in achieving remission, and superior at maintaining it.

#### Diabetic Nephropathy
Renal damage as a consequence of [[Type 2 Diabetes|type II diabetes]] is the most common cause of the nephrotic syndrome in adults. The pathophysiology is related to hyperfiltration and vascular damage with mesangial expansion (≥II RPS DN), and eventual glomerulosclerosis characterised by _Kimmelstiel-Wilson lesions_ (-> ≥III RPS DN) and hyaline glomerular arteriolosclerosis. Treatment involves management of the underlying disease process, with a potential role for drugs such as pentoxyphylline (anti-TNF⍺) or atrasentan (endothelin A antagonist). It is important to remember that rapidly-progressive renal failure in T2DM is rare and should be worked up accordingly.
