### What is an AKI?
An AKI is an _abrupt_ decrease in kidney function, represented by a reduction in glomerular filtration rate and urine output. This subsequently affects the functions of the kidney, leading to retention of urea and other nitrogenous wastes, dysregulation of extracellular volume, and acid-base and electrolyte disturbances. 
- - -
### Defining AKI
eGFR is not a reliable measurement in AKI as it (by definition) needs to be calculated in a steady state. Instead, creatinine should be used. The current guidelines, developed by KDIGO, define AKI as any of:
- Increase in serum creatinine (SCr) **≥26.5µmol/L** within 48h
- Increase in SCr to **≥1.5x baseline** within 7d
- Urine volume <0.5mL/kg/h for 6h

AKI is also staged as follows:
|                            | Stage One             | Stage Two            | Stage Three                    |
|----------------------------|-----------------------|----------------------|--------------------------------|
| Absolute SCr               | ≥26.5µmol/L           |                      | ≥353.6µmol/L                   |
| Relative SCr (to baseline) | 1.5-1.9x              | 2.0-2.9x             | 3.0x                           |
| Urine Output               | <0.5mL/kg/h for 6-12h | <0.5mL/kg/h for >12h | <0.3mL/kg/h for >12h or anuria |
| Additional                 |                       |                      | Initiation of KRT              |

There are some limitations to this model, notably:
- AKI is not homogenous - different clinical processes can cause AKI with different outcomes 
- Urine output is not an independent predictor of mortality 
- How do we define a patient’s baseline?

### Epidemiology
AKI is common! It occurs in ~1/5 hospital inpatients, and up to as many as 3/5 ICU patients. The incidence is currently 6% a year in Australia, and is rising. Importantly, AKI is associated with significant mortality, cost, risk of CKD, length of stay, and readmission.

### Pathophysiology
The pathophysiology of AKI is best understood in terms of those factors that make the kidney, and particularly its outer medulla, vulnerable to insult:
1. High metabolic demand
2. Marginal oxygenation 

Clinically, we consider AKI as arising in one of three places:
- Before the kidney -> pre-renal
- Within the kidney -> intrinsic _or_ renal 
- After the kidney -> post-renal 

#### Pre-Renal AKI
Pre-renal AKI is typically due to renal hypoperfusion, either due to true depletion of the extracellular fluid, as in haemorrhage or hypovolaemia, or reduced effective arterial blood volume, as in heart failure or sepsis. This tends to cause acute tubular necrosis. It can also occur due to local impairments in blood delivery to the kidney, either due to drugs affecting the tone of the glomerular arterioles, or renal arterial stenosis. Treatment is with fluid resuscitation. 

#### Intrinsic Renal AKI
Intrinsic renal AKI will arise due to damage to one of the components of the nephron, namely:
- Glomerulus - glomerulonephritis
- Tubules - acute tubular necrosis (toxic or ischaemic)
  - Clinical course: injury -> increase in SCr -> plateauing of SCr once injury establish -> polyuria -> renal recovery -> reduction in SCr
  - Oliguria is the best predictor of the need for RRT
  - Severe ATN can result in cortical necrosis 
  - Endotoxins - myoglobin, casts
  - Exotoxins - aminoglycosides, contrast nephropathy (note for CN: no benefit for NAC as per _PRESERVE_ study)
- Interstitium - acute interstitial nephritis 
  - Biopsy: diffuse cellular infiltrate
  - Tend to see eosinophils in the urine
  - Culprit drugs: β-lactams, PPIs, NSAIDs, immunotherapy
  - Also consider: infection, Sjögren, sarcoidosis, IgG4 disease, idiopathic
- Vasculature - acute microvascular disease, e.g.:
  - Thrombotic microangiopathy (e.g. HUS, TTP)
  - DIC
  - CAPLS
  - SSc
  - Cholesterol emboli post-CAGS, PCI, IABP, etc. (note livedo reticularis commonly associated sign)

#### Post-Renal AKI
Post-renal AKI is almost always due to obstruction of one sort or another in the urinary system:
- PUJ -> stone
- Ureter -> stone
- Bladder -> stone, malignancy
- Prostate -> BPH, malignancy
- Urethra -> stricture
- Neurological -> e.g. spastic paraparesis, SCI, [[multiple sclerosis]], etc.

### Diagnostic Approach
Clinical:
- History
- Medication review
- Fluid assessment

Blood Tests:
- FBE + blood film:
  - Schistocytes -> acute microvascular disease 
  - Eosinophilia -> AIN, EGPA, cholesterol emboli
- CK -> rhabdomyolysis
- Urate -> TLS
- Vasculitis screen (including ASOT for PSGN)

Urine Tests:
- Dipstick
  - Protein = albumin
  - Blood = haemoglobin or myoglobin 
- Microscopy, culture, and sensitivity
- Acute phase microscopy -> casts:
  - Hyaline/uromodulin/Tamm-Horsfall protein -> reduced renal perfusion 
  - Epithelial cells (RTEC) -> ischaemic or toxic injury, usually ATN (need to consider what other cells are being seen in the urine)
  - Granular casts -> RTEC + degraded lysosomes -> ATN
  - White cells -> likely AIN
  - Red cells -> glomerular lesion
- Red cell morphology -> dysmorphic RBCs -> glomerular lesions
- Quantification of albuminuria and proteinuria:
  - uACR
  - uPCR
- Urinary sodium - beware: less accurate 
  - Low urinary sodium (<40mmol/L) -> pre-renal AKI
  - High urinary sodium -> ATN
- Fraction of excreted sodium:
  - <1% -> pre-renal AKI
  - 2+% -> ATN

Imaging:
- Ultrasound:
  - Exclude obstruction - look for hydronephrosis
  - Additional valuable information:
    - Kidney size
    - Cortical thickness 
    - Echogenicity
    - Doppler - perfusion, renal vein thrombosis 

#### Renal Biopsy
Indications:
- Active urinary sediment
- Heavy proteinuria
- Aetiology unclear
- Diagnostic confirmation, prognosis, response to treatment 
- Exclude dual pathologies 

Contraindications:
- Kidney failure
- Coagulopathy/antiplatelets/anticoagulation
- Bilateral cysts
- Uncontrolled hypertension
- Hydronephrosis
- UTI

### Management 
Management of AKI is largely supportive! The first step is to work-up thoroughly, and treat the underlying cause. Further injury can be avoided through judicious volume/fluid management and appropriate adjustment of medications. The complications of AKI, such as fluid overload, electrolyte derangement, and acid-base abnormalities also need to be managed as appropriate. In some cases, renal replacement therapy may be indicated, particularly in intensive care. 

#### Volume Optimisation 
_RELIEVE_ study - liberal vs. restrictive approach to resuscitation:
- Abdominal surgery -> increased AKI with restrictive approach
- Cardiac surgery, ARDS -> no increased AKI with restrictive approach 

Need to be cautious of the possibility of precipitating AKI!

In terms of the choice of fluid, NaCl is recommended by KDIGO but is associated with a hypochloremic [[physiology/Metabolic Acidosis|metabolic acidosis]]. This suggests that colloids may be an appropriate choice. The target MAP is usually >65mmHg. 

#### [[renal/Renal Replacement Therapy|Renal Replacement Therapy]]
Some considerations:
- Early vs late initiation -> no difference in mortality, less progression to RRT in late group
- Intermittent vs CRRT -> no difference in mortality or kidney outcomes
- High vs low intensity -> no difference in mortality or dialysis dependence
