### What is CKD?
Chronic kidney disease is defined in terms of two factors:
- An impairment in glomerular filtration rate - ≤60mL/min/1.73sqm
- An increase in albuminuria (indicative of kidney damage) - ≥2.5(M)/≥3.5(F)

To be called ‘chronic’, these findings must have been on two occasions at least three months apart. Additionally, having a kidney transplant means that a patient has CKD. 

Pathologically, the ‘final common pathway’ that all CKD-processes converge on is in a secondary FSGS with endotehlial and podocyte injury in the glomerulus. 

#### Glomerular Filtration Rate
The glomerular filtration can be either measured or estimated, the latter of which is vastly more common using several equations:
- Cockroft-Gault equation:
  - `eGFR = (x0.85 if female)[(140 - Age) * Wt] / (0.814* SCr)` 
- MDRD equation
- CKD-EPI

Despite being easier than running tests, equations can be biased, and are only validated for adults.

### Staging CKD
| GFR Stage | GFR (mL/min/1.73sqm) |
|-----------|----------------------|
| 1         | ≥90                  |
| 2         | 60-89                |
| 3a        | 45-59                |
| 3b        | 30-44                |
| 4         | 15-29                |
| 5         | <15                  |

Note that stage 1 and 2 CKD refer to the situation in which a patient has normal GFR with evidence of kidney damage (e.g. albuminuria).

This staging is on the basis of mortality. Both lower GFR and albuminuria are independently risk factors for both all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. Consequently, there are also albuminuria stages for CKD:
| Albuminuria Stage | Albuminuria level - male (uACR mg/mmol) | Albuminuria level - female (uACR mg/mmol) |
|-------------------|-----------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------|
| 1                 | <2.5                                    | <3.5                                      |
| 2                 | 2.5-25                                  | 3.5-35                                    |
| 3                 | >25                                     | >35                                       |

### Pathophysiologies and Management of CKD 
The physiological roles of the kidney (viz. [[renal/Renal Physiology|renal physiology]]) are varied:
- Creation of urine/maintenance of fluid status 
- Maintenance of blood pressure
- Maintenance of electrolyte balance
- Maintenance of acid-base balance 

As such, in CKD, any of these processes can go wrong, both as the driver of CKD, or as one of its ultimate sequelae:
- Impaired filtration -> increased urea/SCr/toxins
- Proteinuria -> nephrotic syndrome, oedema
- Dysregulation of [[physiology/The Renin-Angiotensin System|the renin-angiotensin system]] -> hypertension
  - Appropriate management of hypertension is critical to slow progression of kidney disease, reduce cardiovacsular events, and improve mortality
  - First-line management is with an ACE or an A2RB (which also reduces proteinuria), with additional:
    - SGLT2i
    - GLP1RA
    - MRA
  - These form the ‘four pillars’ of CKD similar to GDMT for heart failure 
- Reduced EPO -> anaemia
- Na+ disturbance -> fluid retention -> hypertension 
  - Sodium restriction - aim <2.5g/d of sodium
- Acid-base disturbance -> acidosis
- Other electrolyte disturbances
- Bone disease
- Vascular calcification
- Cardiovascular disease
