### Overview
CTLA4 is a cell-surface marker that is expressed on T-cells _after_ stimulation to act as a regulator of the immune system. If CTLA4 is deficient, the immune system can essentially act in an unregulated fashion, leading to the development of autoimmune disease, immunodeficiency, and some types of malignancies. 

In the normal state, CD80 costimulation of CD28 on the T-cell surface is required for T-cell activation. CTLA4, however, can also bind to CD80 on antigen presenting cells (which normally should bind CD28), causing T-cells to be inactivated. 
![](Immune%20Checkpoint%20Inhibition/image.png)
Deficiency in LRBA leads to the same syndrome, and LRBA is the chaperone protein responsible for transporting CTLA4 to the cell surface. 

We can treat CTLA4 deficiency with _abatacept_.

Similarly, PD-1 on T-cells and its ligand on APCs or tumour cells can cause peripheral inactivation of T-cells. 

#### Summary
1. CTLA4 is on the T-cell, and interacts with B7 on the APC - it is inhibited by ipilimumab
   - This process occurs in the lymph nodes
2. PD1 is on the T-cell, and interacts with PDL1 on the tumour cell
   1. PD1 is inhibited by pembrolizumab
   2. PDL1 is inhibited by avelumab
   - This process occurs in the peripheral tumour microenvironment C

### Role in cancer
Many cancers can exploit CTLA4 to suppress the immune system to grow unchecked. Immune checkpoint inhibitors, specifically **_ipilimumab_** and **_tremelimumab_**, antagonise CTLA4, essentially blocking cancer cells from exploiting this mechanism. 

Likewise, PD-1 (which is expressed on CD8+ cells) can be blocked by:
- **_Pembrolizumab_**
- **_Nivolumab_**

And its ligand (which is expressed on the tumour cells) can be blocked by:
- Atezolizumab
- Avelumab
- Durvalumab

We can predict the response to immune checkpoint inhibitors based on:
- Tumour mutational burden - i.e. tumour is producing more abnormal proteins to be recognised by the immune system once it is ‘reactivated’ ([Tumor Mutational Burden and Response Rate to PD-1 Inhibition](https://www.nejm.org/doi/10.1056/NEJMc1713444?url_ver=Z39.88-2003&rfr_id=ori:rid:crossref.org&rfr_dat=cr_pub%20%200pubmed))
- [[Genomic Instability|Microsatellite mismatch/repair deficiency]] (dMMR) - i.e. accumulated mutations lead to more antigens for immune recognition

### Immune Related Adverse Effects
Typically evolve within the first three months, but can even occur after discontinuation. Can be general (fatigue, nausea, rash), endocrine (adrenalitis, hypophysitis, thyroiditis, CIADM), or solid-organ related (lungs, colon, kidney, heart). Skin, GI, endocrine, and lung tend to be most common. Some broad associations:
* CTLA4 (tends to be early): colitis, hypophysitis, rash
* PD1/L (tends to be late): pneumonitis, diabetes, hypophysitis, arthralgias
* Combination therapies: colitis, myocarditis 

⠀Management is an evidence free zone!
* Grade 1 = monitor, continue medication, supportive treatment
* Grade 2 = withhold medication, oral glucocorticoids, supportive treatment
* Grade 3+ = cease medication, IV glucocorticoids (if refractory can consider other immunosuppression e.g. MMF)

Salvage therapy for specific IRAEs:
- Colitis -> infliximab or vedolizumab
- Hepatitis -> ATG

### Mechanisms of Resistance to Immunotherapy
Resistance to immunotherapy can be primary or adaptive, and can arise through a number of mechanisms:
~Tumour cell intrinsic~:
- Absence of antigenic proteins - e.g. low mutational burden
- Absence of antigen presentation - e.g. silencing of HLA
- Genetic T-cell exclusion - e.g. oncogenic PDL1 inhibition
- Insensibility to T-cells - e.g. mutations in interferon gamma pathway signalling

~Tumour cell extrinsic~:
- Absence of T-cells
- Other inhibitors immune checkpoints - e.g. VISTA, LAG3, TIM3
- Immunosuppressive cells - e.g. TAMs, Tregs
