### Epidemiology
Colorectal cancer is a common disease, especially in Australia, which ranks #2 among countries for CRC incidence. It is predominantly a disease of the elderly (median age at diagnosis of 70y), although the incidence in younger people is increasing. 

It is the second-leading cause of cancer death. 
- - -
### Screening
Bowel screening has been carried out since 2006, consisting of:
- Faecal occult blood testing every second year between ages 50-74y
- The current test looks for the presence of human global in the stool
  - This is more specific than the older, guaiac based tests that just looked for haem
- Typically, 8% of the cohort will have a positive result, prompting procession to a colonoscopy
  - CT colonograph is _not_ recommended as a first line screening tool due to its poor detection rate cf. colonoscopy 
- Of those who proceed to colonoscopy, ~1/29 were diagnosed with cancer
- The most common cause of positive results were haemorrhoids and diverticular disease 
- Poor compliance (~40%)

Recommendations for colonoscopy:
- Category 1 = one first-degree relative >55y  = ~2x risk -> no recommendation
- Category 2 = one FDR <55y **or** two FDR of any age on the same side of the samily = ~5x risk -> q5y colonoscopy from age 50y or 10y younger than the age at diagnosis of the earliest cancer in the family
- Category 3 = hereditary syndrome or multiple relatives with CRC = ~10x risk -> q1-5y colonoscopy

Note that although the colonoscopy is a better _individual_ test for CRC than faecal occult blood screening, the latter is a much more cost effective population based intervention.

#### Polyp Screening
- Hyperplastic polyps -> generally do not need to be followed up unless associated with serrated polyposis
- Tubular adenomas -> concerning if villous morphology or high grade dysplasia
  - Low-grade adenomas in themselves do not appear to confer an increased risk of cancer 
- Sessile serrated polyps -> difficult to detect and high risk of malignancy -> dysplasia is concerning

### Risk Factors
IBD:
- [[Ulcerative Colitis]]:
  - 0.5%/y incidence, increased if concurrent primary sclerosis cholangitis 
  - RR of 2.8 for CRC and 1.9 for rectal cancer specificsally
- [[Crohn Disease]]:
  - RR of 2.6 for CRC

Previous abdominopelvic radiation - likely an example of survivorship bias. Screening should commence at 5y after end of radiation or age 30y.

Family history:
- **One** first degree relative: RR = 2.2
- **More than one** first degree relative: RR = 4
- Relative with diagnosis **before age 45**: RR = 3.9

Other risk factors - all roughly RR = 1.2-1.3:
- Obesity, for every 5-unit increase in BMI
- Diabetes and insulin resistance
- Alcohol
- Processed meats
- Red meat

### Prevention
There is good evidence that increased physical activity and reduced body fat decrease the risk of colorectal cancer. In terms of interventions without such strong evidence, consider:
- Low-dose aspirin
- Selenium supplementation
- Reducing red meat and alcohol
- Additional dietary fibre
- Reduced dietary fat and sugar

### Molecular Pathogenesis - the ‘Adenoma-Carcinoma Sequence’
CRC can arise from both germline and sporadic mutations. In general, there are three main pathways:
- CIN = APC ‘classical’ pathway -> due to chromosomal instability
- MSI-H/MMRd -> due to microsatellite instability
  - Often right-sided 
- CIMP -> CpG island methylator phenotype
  - Associated with sessile-serrated adenomas

Either way, these typically end up resulting in _KRAS_ or _BRAF_ stations, that ultimately increase pro-inflammatory cytokines and result in the formation of an adenoma.  _BRAF_ mutations in a _non_-dMMR cancer is a very poor prognostic marker.
![](Colorectal%20Cancer/image.png)
As these evolve, the final step is typically the acquisition of a _TP53_ or _BAX_ mutation, that ultimately give rise to malignancy. 

In general, the sequence of mutations is:
- WNT
- MAPK
- PI3K
- TGFβ
- TP53

No treatments for _NRAS_ mutations.

### [[Hereditary Cancer Syndromes]] and Colorectal Cancer
Genetic cancer syndromes account for about 5% of all CRC, but a familial influence confers about 35% of total risk. 

There are, broadly, two classes of hereditary syndromes:
#### Polyposis Syndromes
- Familial Adenomatous Polyposis 
  - Autosomal dominant condition with 90% penetrance by middle age
  - <1% of CRC
  - Loss of the _APC_ TSG on chromosome 5, leading to a ‘carpet’ of >100 polyps in the colon and duodenum
  - Can be ‘attenuated’ with <100 polyps
  - Should receive q3y colonoscopy from age 12y then annually from age 35y + gastroscopy
  - ~1/4 patients will _not_ have a damily history
  - Associated cancers: papillary thyroid, ileal carcinoid, gastric cancer
- MUTYH-associated Polyposis 
  - Due to loss of MUTYH gene, leading to failure of base excision
  - Autosomal recessive mutation, estimated 1/100 carrier rate

#### Non-Polyposis Syndromes 
- Hereditary Non-Polyposis Coli (Lynch Syndrome)	
  - Autosomal dominant heterozygous germline mutation in one of the five key MMR genes:
    - Due to _loss of MLH1_ (which can also happen sporadically), _MSH2, PMS2,_ or _MSH6_
    - Or, loss of _EPCAM_, the epigenetic regulator of _MSH2_ 
  - Surprisingly common - ~1:300 prevalence, 3% of CRC
  - Loss of these genes leads to ‘microsatellite instability’ - i.e. large repeat sequences of DNA
  - The presence of the BRAF V600E variant makes Lynch syndrome _extremely_ unlikely
  - Lifetime risk is >50% - most common phenotype is right sided cancer 
  - Pembrolizumab is used to treat 
  - Associated with gastric, ovarian, and endometrial tumours
  - Should get q2y colonoscopy from age 25y or 5y younger than the earliest cancer Dx in a family member + gastroscopy + pelvic US

### Management 
The mainstay of treatment for colorectal cancer is surgery:
- Stage I -> surgery
- Stage II -> surgery
  - Plus adjuvant chemotherapy if high risk:
    - T4
    - Obstruction
    - High grade
    - Extramural venous invasion
    - Adjacent organ invasion
    - Low LN yield
  - Usual duration of doublet therapy with 5-FU/capecitabine or 5-FU/oxaliplatin is 3-6 months 
- Stage III -> surgery + adjuvant chemotherapy
- Stage IV -> systemic chemotherapy (often with irinotecan)

In general, rectal cancers can be harder to manage surgically due to the anatomy of the rectum. The _OPRA_ trial ([viz.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35483010/)) recently found that organ preservation is feasible in patents with stage II-III rectal adenocarcinoma by combining total neoadjuvant therapy with a watch and wait strategy thereafter.

#### Targeted Therapies:
- EGFR inhibition with cetuximab and panitumumab:
  - This requires the patient to have wild type _RAS_ and _RAF_ alleles, as otherwise inhibition of EGFR will not affect downstream signalling 
  - Retrospective analysis suggests that benefit is limited to left sided cancers
  - Improves progression free survival in first-, second- and third-line
  - Cetuximab can be used in _BRAF_ mutated cancers in combination with downstream inhibitors (e.g. encorafenib)
  - ADRs: acneiform rash (on-target), diarrhoea, renal salt wasting
- VEGF inhibition wth bevacizumab
  - Marginally improves progression-free survival
  - Not used in adjuvant treatment
  - ADRs include hypertension, proteinuria, GI perforation, thromboembolic events, and delayed wound healing

#### Immunotherapy:
- Most effective in MSI-H/dMMR colorectal cancer 

#### Emerging Treatments:
- TAS102 - nucleic acid analogue -> modest OS benefit in refractory patients
- Regorafenib, fruquintanib - multitargeted TKI -> modest OS benefit with high toxicity 
- HER2 antagonism = trastuzumab, lapatinib
- BRAF inhibitors = encorafenib, dabrafenib (used in combination with EGFR inhibition)
- Sotorasib - KRAS inhibitor -> modest benefit
