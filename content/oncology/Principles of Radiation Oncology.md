### Overview
Radiation oncology has a rich history, dating back to its first use in 1896 to treat recurrent breast cancer. 

#### The Basics 
Photons and particles come from linear accelerators or isotopes, and are then able to do one of two things:
- Directly ionise DNA
- Ionise H2O -> generate free radicals -> break DNA covalent bonds
This aims to exploit the fact that malignant cells are generally less able to repair DNA than normal cells, causing damage to accumulate over cycles and ultimatlely leading to cell death.

The aim of treatment is to deliver the lowest possible dose of radiation to the affected tissue as precisely as possible, with minimal damage to surrounding structures. The dose of radiation is measured in _Grays_ - where 1 Gray = 1 Joule of radiation enegy absorbed per kilogram of matter. Synchronous chemotherapy widens the therapeutic index of radiotherapy by reducing the ED50, allowing the radiotherapy to take greater effect at a lower dose, while minimising toxicity.

#### Radiotherapy Modalities
1. External Beam Radiotherapy:
   - 2D
   - 3D conformal
   - Intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) = 3D with more beams
   - Stereotactic radiosurgery = precisely targeted with higher doses - typically indicated for:
     - [[oncology/Lung Cancer|Lung cancer]] in patients unfit for surgery
     - Cranial metastases
     - Oligometastases
     - Some benign indications - e.g. cranial AVMs
   - Intra-operative radiotherapy
2. Brachytherapy = internal 
   - e.g. direct insertion of radioactive matter into the prostate
3. Systemic radionucleides
   - e.g. radioactive iodine in thyroid cancer
   - Can be ligated with peptide receptors to target specific tissues, e.g.:
     - PSMA-Lu177 in prostate cancer
     - Somatostatin-Lu177 in NETs
   - Or, can use radioisotopes with specificity for particular tissues, e.g. Sr89 in bony metastases from prostate cancer
