### Overview
A _genetic variant_ (or mutation) is a permanent alteration in the DNA sequence that makes up a gene. They are grouped into three broad categories:
- Benign/likely benign (class 1/2) - variants that do not alter the function of the gene 
- Variants of uncertain significance (class 3) -not to be used in management decisions
- Likely pathogenic/pathogenic (class 4/5) - variants that are known to alter the function of the gene. Variants that cause serious or early disability tend to be rare, due to their adverse effect on life expectancy and reproduction. In contrast, pathogenic variants that cause disease in later life or which are autosomal recessive tend to be relatively common*.
  - These tend to also encompass the “clinically actionable” variants, where they will inform management for cancer-affected patients, their non-cancer-affected relatives, or couples planning to conceive 

Note that in a genetic context, we often say that “common” = >1% [[prevalence]]. 

There are two(ish) types of mutations that can cause cancer:
1. **Germline** mutations are present in the germ cell (egg or sperm). If passed onto offspring, these mutations will be present in all cells, and result in a heritable condition.
   - There is also a rate of rare emergence of spontaneous germline mutations that occur.
2. **Somatic** mutations are present in non-germ cells, and while they can reproduce abnormally in clonal cells, they are generally non-heritable. 
3. **Mosaic** variants - these lead to a ~1% risk of cancer in siblings when supposedly ‘de novo’ mutations arise 

The **penetrance** of a genetic variant refers to the proportion of individuals carrying a (pathogenic) variant who will manifest the disease.

### Patterns of Inheritance
#### Autosomal Dominant
Autosomal dominant inheritance refers to a pattern of inheritance that involves the non-sex chromosomes, and which require only one allele to cause disease.

This is the most common mode of inheritance, and many of the ‘classic’ hereditary cancer syndromes are inherited this way:
- Breast - _BRCA, TP53, PALB2, CHEK2_
- Colon - _MMR_ (Lynch syndrome), _APC_, _STK11_ (Peutz-Jegher Syndrome)
- Ovarian - _BRCA_
- Thyroid - _MEN, RET, PTEN_

There is a chance that these genes will display incomplete penetrance. Moreover, many genes that display sex-specific syndromes may appear to ‘skip’ a generation. 

#### Autosomal Recessive
Autosomal recessive inheritance, on the other hand, requires both copies of an allele be affected in order to be manifested. This is much less common, and features syndromes/genes such as:
- Attenuated FAP/_MYH_
- Bloom syndrome/_BLM_
- Fanconi anaemia/_FANC_
- Ataxia telangiectasia/_ATM_

That said, note that there may be an increased cancer risk for individuals who are heterozygous for pathogenic variants. 

#### The Founder Effect 
Genetic bottlenecks can lead to marked increases in the prevalence of a mutation in a community - e.g. Ashkenazi jews.

### Effects of Variants
#### Gain of Function
Mutations in some genes are associated with _overexpression_ of protein, and often increase in activity. These genes are referred to as _proto-oncogenes_, and are almost always acquired. There are a few, rare exceptions such as _RET_ in MEN2 and _MET_, which causes hereditary paipllary renal cell carcinoma type 1. 

#### Loss of Function
Mutations in some genes that lead to reduced activity of proteins that are critical to cellular housekeeping, such as repair of damaged DNA, regulation of cell division, or apoptosis of damaged cells. These are referred to as _tumour suppressor genes_, and are frequently inherited. In these cases, the ‘two hit hypothesis’ often applies, with one inactivated TSG being inherited, and then the second hit being acquired. Tests for LOF genes can involve immunohistochemistry (looking for absence of a protein) and microsatellite instability studies. These are the most common causes of familial cancer syndromes, inherited in an autosomal dominant fashion.
