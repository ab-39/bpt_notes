### Overview 
HCC is a major cause of cancer burden and mortality worldwide - particularly in China and Mongolia. The vast majority of HCC arises due to [[cirrhosis]] or [[gastroenterology/Hepatitis B|Hepatitis B]], with some cases also being attributable to aflatoxin. As MASH/NAFLD continues to increase in incidence, however, it is also becoming responsible for causing HCC. 

> [!NOTE] 
> AFL-B1 is an Aspergillus-derived toxin that is metabolised by CYP450 in the liver and is hepatotoxic. 

Diagnosis is usually based on radiological findings, and symptoms are often limited. The gold-standard imaging test is a Quad-phase CT, as arterial phase hyperenhancement is more common in HCC (which is supplied arterially like all cancers), unlike the liver which typically enhances in the portal venous phase (due to receiving 70% of its supply from the portal vein). The LIRAD diagnostic criteria are used for liver cancer, but the diagnosis can usually be made with:
- Hypervacsular liver lesion, and
- Elevated AFP
Biopsy is typically only considered if a diagnosis is uncertain after imaging. 

Screening for HCC typically consists of 6-monthly ultrasound with concurrent ⍺-fetoprotein (which can also be elevated in testicular cancer and pregnancy). Screening has been shown to have good CBA, as early detection usually results in high cure rates. Current EASL guidelines suggest screening for:
- Cirrhosis - Child-Pugh A, B, and C (if awaiting transplant)
- Non-cirrhotic patients with active HBV or family history 
- Non-cirrhotic patients with chronic HCV and F3 fibrosis
- High-risk individuals (Asian, ATSI)

Metastatic deposits in the liver are _far_ more common than primary hepatocellular carcinoma. When HCC metastasises, it is typically to the bone or lung. 

Treatment of HCC is guided by stage and prognosis, as well as liver function:
- Very early stage
- Early stage -> resection or ablation, consider transplant
  - The Milan criteria suggest transplantation if:
    - Single lesion ≤5cm, or
    - ≤3 lesions of ≤3cm
- Intermediate stage -> transarterial chemoembolisation (possibly as a bridge to transplantation)
- Advanced stage -> systemic treatment or palliative care
  - Systemic therapy -> immunotherapies (atezolizumab + bevacizumab) vs small-molecule inhibitors (sorafenib/lenvatinib)
- End stage
