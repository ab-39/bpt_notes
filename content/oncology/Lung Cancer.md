### Epidemiology
Lung cancer has a _massive_ global burden of disease. It is the leading cause of cancer deaths worldwide as well as in Australia, where it represents 10% of new cancer diagnoses annually. 

Importantly, lung cancer is often so devastating because patients present with advanced disease.

> [!NOTE] Effect of Smoking Cessation
> After stopping smoking, lung cancer risk halves compared to continuing smokers after approximately a decade, although cardiovascular benefits emerge sooner.

### Clinical Presentation of Lung Cancer
Patients with lung cancer are almost always symptomatic at presentation, with symptoms relating to:
- the primary tumour -> cough, haemoptysis, etc.
  - massive haemoptysis and stridor are severe warning signs
- intrathoracic spread -> pleural effusion, chest wall invasion, Horner syndrome, SVC obstruction 
- distant metastases -> bone pain, headache, cord compression, Addison disease
  - Liver
  - Bone
  - Adrenals
  - Brain:
    - Single brain metastasis + good ECOG -> surgery + radiotherapy
    - Single brain metastasis + bad ECOG -> radiotherapy alone 
- paraneoplastic syndromes:
  - PTHrP -> hypercalcaemia 
  - SIADH -> hyponatraemia (in SCLC)
  - neurologic syndromes (e.g. LEMS), typically in SCLC
  - HPOA
  - VTE and other haematological complications

#### Why does lung cancer present so late?
Early lung cancer is often asymptomatic, and the initial progress of the disease is often insidious. Unfortunately, by the time the lung cancer has reached the point of detectability, it is also often at high risk of having metastasised. Additionally, symptoms can often be masked by the symptoms of smoking, and compounded by the social stigma attached to smoking. 
> Early detection of lung cancer improves chances of survival

### Lung Cancer for Screening
Current recommendation is for:
- Current/former smokers
- 1-2 years
- Low-dose CT chest 

Key studies in this area:
- *[US NLST](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1102873)* -> 20% reduction in mortality with just three annual CT scans
- *[NELSON](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1911793)* -> screening q2y lead to a 24% reduction in mortality in men and 33% in women

We are implementing a national screening program in Australia from 2025, with biennial scans for:
- age 50-70y
- current or former smoker
- ≥30 pack years

### Diagnostic Work Up for Lung Cancer
Best scan: contrast-enhanced CT thorax including neck and upper abdomen
-> if possibility of curative treatment: need to follow up with a PET scan to avoid missing CT-occult metastases and minimise futile treatment 

Once diagnosis is suspected on CT, progress to:
- bronchoscopy
- mediastinal nodal sampling ->
  - histology
  - cytology
  - immunohistochemitry
  - molecular genetic testing
  - PDL1 score

#### Approaches for tissue evaluation
Least invasive is best - e.g. if there’s an effusion, tap that

If there is a high suspicion of early disease, may not require a biopsy before proceeding straight to surgery.
- Important to have enough tissue for PDL1 testing 

Common procedures for tissue evaluation include:
- Bronchoscopy +/- EBUS
- CT guided lung biopsy
- Biopsy of metastatic site
- Less commonly: mediastinoscopy, sputum cytology 
- Pleural fluid cytology - most likely to be positive in adenocarcinoma due to tendency to exfoliate into the pleural space 

New approach - liquid biopsy: looking for circulating tumour DNA/microRNA/circulating tumour cells (can also be used to monitor progress on treatment). 

Staging is typically done using the TNM method:
- Stage I: T1-2a (i.e. tumour ≤4cm) **and** N0 and M0
- Stage II: T2b-3 (i.e. tumour ≤7cm and local invasion)  **or** ipsilateral hilar nodal metastases
- Stage III: T4 (i.e. tumour ≥7cm or significant local invasion) **or** mediastinal or contralateral nodal metastases
- Stage IV: any metastasis, malignant pleural effusion, or contralateral lung disease

### Treatment
#### Early Stage Cancer (I/II):
- Surgery if medically fit - lobectomy with regional lymph node resection recommended
- If not a surgial candidate, and proceed to stereotactic radiotherapy

After surgery, current practice recommends adjuvant immunotherapy (typically PDL1 blockade, with e.g. atezolizumab) or targeted therapy (if appropriate, e.g. osimertinib for _EGFR_ mutated NSCLC) for cancers stage I-IIIb. Neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy with nivolumab is also associated with high rates of complete pathological response (~25%) in patients with resectable lung cancer (stage Ib/4cm+).

#### Stage III Cancer
Stage III cancer remains complex and controversial to manage, and a multidisciplinary input is necessary. Stage III cancer without ipsilateral spread is typically amenable to resection after chemoimmunotherapy, while tumours that are bulky or which have spread contralaterally often present more difficulty. 

#### Stage IV Cancer
Stage IV cancer has a very poor median survival when untreated. All patients should receive a thorough workup including next-generation sequencing to identify:
- Driver mutations - e.g. _EGFR, ALK, ROS1, RET, KRAS, HER2, BRAF_, etc. 
- PDL-1 expression
- Squamous vs non-squamous histology
- Performance status (generally need to be ECOG ≤2 for most drugs on the PBS)

If none of the following actionable mutations are present, most patients will receive one or more immunotherapeutic agents. In general, a high level of PDL-1 expression (e.g. ≥50%) could indicate single agent immunotherapy (e.g. pembrolizumab), while patients with low PDL-1 expression should be offered platinum doublet chemotherapy with additional immunotherapy. For patients with a favourable performance status in whom immunotherapy is absolutely contraindicated (e.g. previous solid organ transplant) there may be a role for maintenance chemotherapy.

Patients who are ECOG 3+ with metastatic lung cancer should be offered best supportive care alone. 

#### [[oncology/Molecular Pathways in Targeted Chemotherapy|Molecular Pathways in Targeted Chemotherapy]] 
_EFGR_ mutant:
Generally common in Asian populations, less common otherwise. More heavily associated with adenocarcinoma, young women, and never smokers. TKIs such as **_erlotinib_** and gefitinib, first generation ERBB1 inhibitors, have been shown to improve progression free survival. Side-effects include acneiform rash, diarrhoea, alopecia, nail changes, and pulmonary toxicity. 

Third generation ERBB1 inhibitors such as _**osimertinib**_ are now first line, as they are able to treat cancers that are resistant to erlotinib via sensitisation or acquisition of a T790 mutation (and can be used post-progression on first generation TKIs if the mutation is confirmed). They provide excellent CNS penetration with less skin toxicity. 

_ALK_ fusion oncogene:
A relatively rare mutation (~5% of NSCLC) that is assocaited with Asian populations, men, never smokers, and large-cell cancer. Arises from an inversion in chromosome two that fuses EML4 awith ALK. First-generation ALK TKIs include **_crizotinib_**, while more recent drugs that comprise the current standard of care are _**alectinib**_ and ***lorlatinib***. Median survival in ALK positive cancers is greater than five years. Side effects include visual changes, neutropenia, altered bowel habit, pulmonary toxicity, hepatotoxicity, QT prolongation, hyperlipidaemia, and CYP450 interactions.

_ROS1_ rearrangement:
Another rare (<1%) oncogene arising from chromosomal rearrangements, also inhibited by crizotinib, which provided an impressive response rate of 72% with median PFS of 20 months. The current standard of care is **_entrectinib_**, which has similar outcomes to crizotinib with a slightly more favourable side effect profile. 

_MET_ exon 14 skipping mutation:
Typically occurs in older patients with more complex needs. Can be identified either on direct biopsy or on ‘liquid biopsy’ of circulating tumour DNA. Currently utilised TKI is **_tepotinib_**, which tends to be well tolerated with the main side effects being peripheral oedema and pneumonitis.

### [[oncology/Immune Checkpoint Inhibition|Immune Checkpoint Inhibition]] in Lung Cancer 
The principle underlying immunotherapy is the potentiation of T-cells. Cancer cells are able to use the PDL1 molecule to inhibit T-cell function through providing a negative immune regulatory signal. Immune checkpoint inhibitors block this interaction from occurring, and allow the immune system to carry out its role:
| Drug                   | Target       | Use                                                          |
|------------------------|--------------|--------------------------------------------------------------|
| Pembrolizumab          | PD1          | First-line treatment for NSCLC                               |
| Nivolumab              | PD1          | Second-line after disease progression on platinum-based chemotherapy<br>Emerging evidence (CHECKMATE) for use in neoadjuvant treatment |
| Nivolumab + ipilimumab | PD1R + CTLA4 | First-line treatment for stage IV NSCLC                      |
| Atezolizumab           | PDL1         | Initial treatment for nonsquamous NSCLC (in combination with platinum and bevacizumab); also used in extensive stage SCLC |
| Durvalumab             | PDL1         | Unresectable stage III NSCLC                                 |
