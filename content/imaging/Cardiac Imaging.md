### Overview
In 2025, there are now multiple modalities by which we can image the heart. Despite this, the most commonly used and arguably useful test remains the humble transthoracic echocardiogram. 

Ischaemic heart disease is generally evaluated through two paraemeters:
- Structural -> coronary anatomy
- Functional -> ischaemia
- - -
### Coronary Angiography
The gold standard test for the imaging of coronary arteries remains invasive coronary angiography. Despite this, there are multiple risks associated with angiography, and so it is seldom the first-line choice of _diagnostic_ test.
- - -
### Non-Invasive Cardiac Testing
Non-invasive cardiac testing can be carried out resting, or else under conditions of stress. 
- - -
### Cardiac CT Angiography
CTCA is generally indicated for the workup of chest pain, particularly when:
- The stress test/TTE has been equivocal
- The ECG is equivocal/uninterpretable (e.g. LBBB)
- The patient cannot exercise
- Troponin and ECG are normal in the acute setting

CTCA has a very high sensitivity (~95%) and specificity (~85%) for coronary artery disease, with an extremely high (>99%) negative predictive value. Note that this means in some cases, CTCA can ‘overrule’ changes on a stress ECG, etc.

The PPV of CTCA is relatively poor, as false positives are possible from ‘calcium blooming artefact’. 

CTCA is particularly useful for acute chest pain as it also has a reasonably good ability to detect PE and aortic dissection.

### Cardiac MRI
With normal MRI, the myocardium appears black. Gadolinium contrast can be used, then, to distinguish scarring, which appears bright. This is useful for predicting viability of tissue following revascularisation. As the extent of the scar progresses from subendocardial to transmural, the likelihood of recovery of function from revascularisation declines. 
- - -
### Functional Testing
The application of stress to the heart, typically through exercise but occasionally through pharmacological means such as dobutamine, can also allow for the detection of haemodynamically significant lesions in the coronary arteries. Typically, there are three modalities:
- ECG -> myocardial ischaemia = exercise-induced ST-segment changes
  - Reduced specificity in women 
- TTE:
  - Incorporates an ECG
  - Pre-stress TTE: assess LVEF, diastology, valvular function, and wall motion
  - Post-stress TTE: assess wall motion, diastology, and RVSP
  - Myocardial ischaemia = exercise-induced ST-segment changes or RWMAs
  - Sensitivity and specificity in the range of 85% each, superior to stress ECG, probably due to the so-called ‘ischaemic cascade’

What are RWMAs? Defined as the percentage of wall thickening during contraction:
- Normal = >50%
- Hypokinetic = <50%
- Akinetic = <10%
- Dyskinetic = moving in the opposite direction

The reason diastolic dysfunction _precedes_ systolic dysfunction is that relaxation is the most oxygen dependent part of a myocyte’s activity.
