# Pulsus Paradoxus
#bpt/respiratory #bpt/cardiology 
- - -
### What is pulsus paradoxus?
Pulsus paradoxus is defined as a drop in systemic blood pressure of ≥10mmHg (some sources say >12% or 10%) on inspiration (against a normal drop of ~5-6mmHg).
- - -
### Mechanism
The mechanism of pulsus paradoxus relates to ventricular interdependence. Normally, inspiration relies on the creation of negative Intrathoracic pressure, which increases venous return and thus RVEDV. This causes the interventricular septum to bulge into the LV, thereby decreasing LVEDV and consequently stroke volume and cardiac output. 

When there is some external factor affecting cardiac compliance, such as pericardial tamponade or hyperinflated lungs, this restricts the ability of the left ventricle to compensate for this intrusion of the septum, thereby leading to an increase in the drop in pressure. 

The reason it is called pulsus _paradoxus_ is that when Kussmaul identified it, he noted that the radial pulse can ‘disappear’ during inspiration.
- - -
### Utility as a clinical sign
Pulsus paradoxus is typically elicited through an arterial line trace, sphygmomanometry, palpation of the radial pulse, or pulse oximetry.

Pulsus paradoxus is most commonly seen in:
- Cardiac tamponade
- Constrictive pericarditis
- Acute asthma (marker of severity if ≥15mmHg)
- Acute COPD

It is less commonly observed in other conditions such as large PE, large pleural effusion, tension pneumothorax, OSA, heart failure, and pregnancy. 

Pulsus paradoxus can be ‘masked’ by AR, ASD, and chest wall immobility.
