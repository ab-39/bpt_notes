### What is obstructive sleep apnea?
OSA is defined by repetitive episodes of complete or partial upper airway obstruction occurring during sleep. 
- Obstructions typically result in desaturations which are terminated by arousal
- - -
### Epidemiology
Common.
The Wisconsin Cohort Study suggests a prevalence of 14% in men and 5% in women.
Australian data suggest a prevalence of 8% in Australian adults.

### Presentation of OSA
The main presenting feature of OSA is usually _daytime sleepiness_, which can often occur during the execution of activities including talking, eating, and driving, although symptom burden correlated.

Other key signs and symptoms include:
- Loud snoring
- Sleep fragmentation
- Daytime napping
- Choking arousals
- Microsleeps/driving accidents
- Witnessed apneas
- Neurocognitive impairment 
- - -
### Diagnostic Criteria
Either:
- AHI ≥5/h + symptoms
Or:
- AHI ≥15/h +/- symptoms
- - -
### Screening for OSA
Ideally used in primary care to identify individuals with a high pre-test probability of OSA, as not everyone can be screened with a PSG. These tools are typically high sensitivity but low specificity. 

#### STOPBANG Questionnaire:
- Snoring
- Tiredness
- Observed Apneas 
- Blood Pressure
- BMI
- Age 
- Neck Circumference
- Gender
Validated in preoperative clinics, with a score ≥3/8 suggesting a medium-high risk of OSA. 
This tool has a high sensitivity (93%) and NPV (90%), but poor specificity (30%)
An alternative test is the Berlin score. 

The most commonly used tool in Australia is the **OSA 50**, to identify patients who may benefit from a sleep study. It consists of four items:
- Obesity (3pts)
- Snoring (3pts)
- Apneas (2pts)
- Age >50y (2pts)
A score ≥5 predicts moderate to severe OSA with a high sensitivity. 

While the Epworth Sleepiness Score has been validated for measurement of sleepiness, it is not a tool for sleep disorders. A score of greater than 10 indicated

### Risk Factors for OSA
The key risk factor for OSA is a _narrow, collapsible airway_. Other contributing factors include:
- low arousal threshold
- ineffective upper airway muscles
- oversensitive ventilatory control system (‘loop gain’) 

Other important risk factors include:
- Increased BMI (OSA prevalence is 63% in BMI ≥35)
- Male sex (RR 2-3)
- Older age
- Neck circumference (M >43cm, F >37cm)
- Witnessed apnoeas 
- Post-menopause
- Craniofacial abnormalities

The Mallampati score (I-IV) can be used as an indicator of oropharyngeal crowding.

### Complications of OSA
- Increased risk of MVA (RR ~4) in moderate to severe OSA
- Cardiovascular disease (HTN, CAD, CCF, AF)
  - The relationship between OSA and CAD appears to be bidirectional, and leads to an increased risk of AMI as well as worsening OSA 
  - The relationship between OSA and HTN is strong and dose responsive (mild -> RR = 2, moderate -> RR = 3-4)
  - OSA makes AF difficult to control -> increased symptom burden, medication use, and risk of hospital admission. Also high (~25%) risk of recurrence post ablation
- Stroke
  - Increased risk (RR = 3) of ischaemic stroke in men with OSA at any severity, with most risk in the severe category 
  - OSA is additionally a risk factor for poor recovery post-stroke and of stroke recurrence 
  - There is a risk of exacerbation of OSA in the post-stroke period
- Mildly increased risk of [[pulmonary hypertension]], particularly if there is sleep hypoventilation
- Neurocognitive deficits - issues with concentration, memory issues
  - Emerging evidence for relationship between dementia and OSA
  - OSA five times more prevalent in elderly with Alzheimers compared to age-matched controls 
- Metabolic dysfunction - risk of diabetes 
- Increased all-cause mortality based on Wisconsin study findings

### Treatment of OSA
#1 = risk factor modification 
1. Reduction in weight 
2. Reduction in alcohol intake
3. Avoidance of respiratory depressants
4. Positional therapy

Upper airway splinting is an important aspect of therapy. Oral devices (e.g. mandibular advancement devices, tongue stabilising devies, etc.) can do this, but are typically expensive and less useful. [[Non-invasive ventilation]], specifically [[continuous positive airway pressure]], is the mainstay of upper airway splinting. 
- In general, start treatment if: ESS >16, AHI >30, significant symptoms
- CPAP - rule of thumb: roughly start at ~10% of body weight in mmHg for PEEP
- APAP - varies pressure until resistance to flow is overcome. No evidence of superiority. 

There is also a limited role for OSA surgery - e.g. removing large tonsils. Emerging treatments include tirzepatide and sulthiame, a new carbonic anhydrase inhibitor.

### Benefits of CPAP in OSA
CPAP _has not_ been found to reduce cardiovascular mortality in patients with OSA. It _has_ been associated with the following, however:
- Reduced risk of MVA
- Reduced respiratory events during sleep
- Decreased daytime sleepiness
- Small improvements in BP
- Decreased erectile dysfunction
- Improved quality of life
