# Pulmonary Hypertension
#bpt/respiratory #bpt/racp_cls

### What is pulmonary hypertension?
Pulmonary hypertension is defined as pulmonary arterial pressures ≥20mmHg. 
- - -
### Epidemiology
PH is common, with an approximately worldwide prevalence of 1%, although the breakdown differs by subtype. There is a familial form of PAH, associated with mutations in the _BMPR2_ gene, the _ACVRL1_ gene (Osler-Weber-Rendu syndrome), and the _EIF2AK4_ gene (PVOD). 

#### Associated Conditions
- Several connective tissue diseases:
  - [[Systemic sclerosis]]
  - [[rheumatology/Systemic Lupus Erythematosus|Lupus]]
  - MCTD
- HIV
- [[Respiratory Consequences of Liver Failure|Cirrhosis]]
- Congenital heart disease
- - -
### Aetiology
| Type                                                         | Prevalence  | Group | Haemodynamics                                                |
|--------------------------------------------------------------|-------------|-------|--------------------------------------------------------------|
| Pulmonary **Arterial** Hypertension<br>e.g. idiopathic, HIV, connective tissue disease, congenital, etc. | Rare        | 1     | PAP >20mmHg<br>PWP ≤15mmHg<br>PVR >3 WU                      |
| LV-associated pHTN                                           | Very common | 2     | PAP >20mmHg<br>PWP >15mmHg<br>PVR ≤3WU (or >3WU if combined pre- and post) |
| pHTN associated with lung disease                            | Common      | 3     |                                                              |
| pHTN associated with PA obstruction<br>e.g. CTEPH            | Rare        | 4     |                                                              |
| Obscure                                                      |             | 5     |                                                              |
Note that pulmonary vascular resistance is a derived unit:
> $PVR = (P_{PA} - P_{LA})/Q$
Where Q = cardiac output, and noting that the PWP is a proxy for LA pressure.

Note that both group 1 and 3 PH are ‘pre’-capillary, and so are indistinguishable on right heart catheterisation.

About 10% of cases of PAH involve the capillaries or pulmonary veins (_pulmonary veno-occulsive disesae_), and are typically characterised by significant hypoxia (particularly on exertion) and a low DLCO. These patients typically respond poorly to treatment (especially with prostacyclins, which may trigger pulmonary oedema).

### Clinical Presentation
In general, signs and symptoms of PH are insidious in onset and fairly vague:
- Exertional dyspnoea
- Exercise limitation
- Fatigue
- Chest pain
- Presyncope

Right-heart failure is a late sign of PH, and indicates advanced disease. 
- - -
### Echocardiographic Signs of PH
The main sign of PH on TTE is ‘peak tricuspid regurgitant velocity’:
- ≤2.8m/s -> low probability of PH (if no other signs)
- 2.9-3.4m/s -> intermediate probability of PH (if no other signs)
- ≥3.5m/s -> high probability of PH (irrespective of other signs)

The presence of other signs increases the probability of PH by one tranche:
| Ventricles                                | Pulmonary Artery                                        | IVC and RA                                             |
|-------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------|
| RV:LV >1.0                                | RV outflow Doppler acceleration time <105ms             | IVC diameter >21mm with decreased inspiratory collapse |
| Flattening of the interventricular septum | Early diastolic pulmonic regurgitation velocity >2.2m/s | RA end-systolic area >18sqcm                           |
|                                           | Pulmonary artery diameter >25mm                         |                                                        |

### Diagnosis of PH
The gold standard test for PH is [[Swann-Ganz catheterisation]].  In general, work-up for PH requires a V/Q scan too, as this is highly sensitive and specific for the detection of CTEPH. Additionally, patients may also undergo vasoreactivity testing to determine which individuals with PAH/type I PH will respond to calcium channel blockers. 
- - -
### Treatment Strategies for PH
#### PAH:
PAH patients are best to be managed at specialist centres. Treatment involves various pathways:
- Endothelin pathway -> reverse endothelin-mediated constriction of vessels
  - Endothelin receptor antagonists = ambriesentan, bosentan, macitentan
- Nitric oxide pathway -> promote vasodilatation
  - PDEV inhibitors = sildenafil, tadalafil
  - Soluble guanylate cyclase stimulator = riociguat 
- Prostacyclin pathway -> promote vasodilatation
  - Prostacyclin mimetics = epoprostenol (IV), iloprost (nebulised), selexipag (oral)
- Activin signalling inhibitors -> sotatercept ([Sotatercept for the Treatment of Pulmonary Arterial Hypertension](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2024277))
- Positive vasodilator test = inhaled nitrous oxide -> CCBs (e.g. amlodipine, nifedipine, diltiazem) at high doses 
- Consider immunosuppression in secondary cases (e.g. SLE, SSc)

![](Pulmonary%20Hypertension/image%202.png)
High risk patients typically warrant triple therapy. 

For patients with group 2 or 3 PH (i.e. secondary to LHF or lung disease) we tend to _avoid_ vasodilators, as they have the risk of precipitating pulmonary oedema in the first instance, or disrupt pulmonary autoregulation in the second instance. 

CTEPH is typically treated with endarterectomy or angiographic stenting, in addition to regular medical therapy if the clot is too distal. 

Note that PH is a _high-risk_ condition in pregnancy, and patients should receive appropriate counselling.
