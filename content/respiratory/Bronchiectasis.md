### What is bronchiectasis?
Bronchiectasis is a _permanent dilatation_ of bronchi and bronchioles due to destruction of airway muscles and elastic connective tissue characterised by chronic, recurrent wet/productive cough and lower airway infections, with radiographic evidence of bronchial dialtatoin secondary to structural injury.

- - -
### Aetiology 
The ‘classic’ cause is post infectious, but this is decreasing. Other causes include:
- ABPA (very common in patients with asthma and cystic fibrosis)
- COPD
- Traction secondary to fibrosis
- Aspiration
- Focal obstruction (e.g. malignancy)
- Immunological defects
- Congenital defects - Marfan, Kartagener, α1-antitrypsin deficiency
- Inflammatory conditions - RA, coeliac, SLE, DPB, IBD
- CF
- Young Syndrome
- Amyloidosis 

Key differentials:
| Cause                                       | Diagnostic Clues                                             | Testing                                                      | Specific Management        |
|---------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------|
| Immunoglobulin deficiency                   | Recurrent sinopulmomary infections                           | Immunoglobulins and subclasses                               | Immunoglobulin replacement |
| [[Allergic Bronchopulmonary Aspergillosis]] | Underlying asthma or CF, frequent wheeze, mucous plugs, **_central_** bronchiectasis | Elevated IgE + Aspergillus-specific IgE and IgG              | Steroids                   |
| Ciliary dysfunction                         | Situs inversus; recurrent sinopulmonary infections; childhood onset  | Nasal nitric oxide<br>Nasal mucosal brushing<br>High speed videomicroscopy<br>Genetic testing | Nil                        |
| Non-tuberculous mycobacterial infection     | Radiologic features (e.g. nodules, cavities)                 | Sputum mycobacterial culture                                 | Multidrug treatment        |

### Important Investigations 
CT scan of the chest is the reference standard diagnostic test, showing:
- Thick-walled bronchi
- Non-tapering bronchi
- Bronchi larger than the accompanying vessel - the ‘signet ring’ sign
- Sputum insipissation -> tree-in-bud changes air trapping
![](Bronchiectasis/image%202.png)

The TSANZ recommended tests to order in the evaluation of bronchiectasis include :
- FBE
- Sputum M/C/S, x3 early morning sputum AFBs
- IgE, Aspergillus precipitins
- Immunoglobulins and subclasses
- Functional assessment/spirometry +/- ABGs

Additionally, consider:
- CF genotyping
- TTE
- Bronchoscopy
- Immune tests (incl. HIV) 
- Nasal brushings (ciliary dyskinesia)
- Upper GI endoscopy

### Staging
With the _FACED_ score:
- FEV1 <50% -> 2 points
- Age >70y -> 2 points
- Remaining 1 point each:
  - Chronic pseudomonas colonisation
  - Multilobar involvement
  - mMRC 3/4 dyspnoea

Higher scores correlate to decreased survival. On average, there will be a 50-100mL decline in FEV1 annually in patients with bronchiectasis, which is worse in women. The predicted severity of this can be inferred from neutrophil elastase activity.

### Complications 
The main complication of bronchiectasis is _Pseudomonas_ colonisation and infection. A key problem is the formation of biofilm in the airways, which can make neutralisation and treatment of the colonisation very difficult. Moreover, they are intrinsically resistant to several antibiotics. Colonisation also accelerates the rate of decline in FEV1. 

Other bacteria that can be problematic are _Stenotrophomonas maltophilia_ and streptococcal and staphylococcal species, particularly _Staphylococcus aureus_.

### Management of Bronchectasis
#1 = Treat the cause

The pillars of bronchiectasis management centre around stopping the vicious cycle outlined above, This can be achieved by:
- Improving mucociliary clearance
  - Chest PT -> improves QOL
  - Pulmonary rehabilitation -> improves QOL and exercise tolerance
  - Hypertonic saline -> consensus opinion for clearance of secretions
  - Mucolytics -> mixed data for bromhexine and NAC; dornase alfa can be used in CF-related bronchiectasis
    - ==🔴Evidence of harm in patients with _non-CF bronchiectasis_ with DNAses (e.g. dornase alfa) - avoid==
- Prevention/early treatment of infection
- Minimising inflammation
  - Macrolides - evidence for reductions in exacerbations, sputum prodcution, and rate of lung function decline (_[BLESS](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/1672240)_) 
    - Caveat - there is an association with increased macrolide resistance 
  - DPP-1 inhibition with **_brensocatib_** -  reduces the activity of neutrophil elastase and serine proteases  -> associated with a lower rate of pulmonary exacerbations and rate of decline in FEV1 ([Phase 3 Trial of the DPP-1 Inhibitor Brensocatib in Bronchiectasis](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2411664))
  - Inhaled antibiotics (typically for _Pseudomonas_ colonisation) - modest effect on reducing exacerbations but risk of bronchospasm and antibiotic resistance

Improve airway clearance -> aim to improve sputum expectoration, techniques include:
- Inhaled hypertonic saline - evidence for improvement in QOL in frequent exacerbators
- Bubble PEP
- Oscillating PEP
- etc. 

Treat airway infection - guided by sputum culture 
- Treat with PO ABx where possible - e.g. ciprofloxacin for pseudomonas - or even inhaled aminoglycosides 

Treat airway obstruction
- No evidence for ICS - reasonable to still trial given overlap with asthma/COPD
- Evidence for SABAs and LABA/ICS

Treat chronic inflammation - typically macrolides (good evidence, make mechanistic sense as prevent neutrophil adhesion - recall risk of prolonged QTc) 
- No evidence for oral steroids or leukotriene agonists

Chronic disease management 
- Chronic infection - trial nebulised tobramycin

#### Pseudomonas Colonisation
Although the data for efficacy of eradication is mixed, eradication of Pseudomonas colonisation can be a useful adjunct to treatment. This is typically done with 2-3 weeks of IV/PO antibiotics, followed by 3-12 months of inhaled antibiotics. There is a reasonably high risk of relapse.
