# Prostate Cancer
#bpt/oncology #bpt/racp_cls
- - -
### Overview
- Common cancer with relatively low mortality
- Derives from the prostatic glandular epithelium
- Insufficient evidence for population based screening - suggest individual discussions with GP 
- Androgens drive progression of prostate cell growth and also prostate cancer
- The threshold of testosterone below which growth should theoretically be suppressed is 1.7nmol/L (= ‘castration’)
  - Prostate cancer that grows despite chemical castration is ‘castrate-resistant’
- Mainstay of treatment for metasatstic castrate-sensitive prostate cancer is _androgen deprivation therapy_, either with:
  - Bilateral orchiectomy (rare)
  - GnRH antagonists - e.g. degarelix - most common, but can cause an initial flare of testosterone (-> disease flare, urinary retention, pain, etc.) 
  - GnRH agonists - e.g. goserelin, leuprolide 
- Mainstay of treatment is now doublet therapy with ADT plus one of:
  - ARSI - androgen receptor signalling inhibitors
  - Docetaxel chemotherapy

### Epidemiology
Prostate cancer is the most commonly diagnosed cancer in Australia, and the third-leading cause of cancer-related mortality.

Screening for prostate cancer is controversial, with two major trials providing conflicting evidence (_ESRPC_ and _PLCO_). At present, there is insufficient evidence to support population-based screening with PSA +/- DRE, and it is recommended that men come to an individualised decision with their doctor.
- - -
### Pathobiology
Like breast and endometrial cancer, prostate cancer is _hugely_ hormone-dependent, albeit with the relevant hormones being _androgens_ rather than oestrogen. 

This key role of androgens gives rise to the fundamental division in prostate cancer phenotypes: castrate-sensitive vs castrate-resistant, where castrate levels of testosterone are defined as <1.7nmol/L.

### Treatment
All patients should undergo a prostate MRI pre-biopsy. Patients with a PSA >20 should undergo staging with a bone scan or PSMA PET. 

The three main modes by which testosterone suppression can be achieved are:
1. GnRH agonists - e.g. goserelin, leuprolide
   - Associated with an initial surge of testosterone, which can be associated with urinary retention (prostate size), exacerbations of bony metastases, and pain crises
   - Can be co-initiated with an anti-androgen (e.g. bicalutamide) to minimise risks 
2. GnRH antagonists - e.g. degarelix 
   - Rapid reduction in serum testosterone
   - Avoids flare phenomenon
3. Bilateral orchiectomy 

ADT is generally given as doublet treatment in combination with either taxane-based chemotherapy (almost always with _docetaxel_) or an androgen-receptor signalling agent. Triple therapy is typically reserved for high-volume (≥4 deposits) metastatic castrate-sensitive prostate cancer. 

#### Androgen-Receptor Signalling Agents
Drugs such as **_abiraterone_** inhibit biosynthesis of androgens by inhibiting 17α-hydroxylase (=CYP17) and c17,20-lyase to minimise both testicular and adrenal androgen synthesis. Due to the downstream effect on other hormones, this leads to side-effects including hypertension, hypokalaemia, peripheral oedema (i.e. symptoms of mineralocorticoid excess), and transaminitis. Generally co-administered with prednisolone to restore the balance between glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid hormones and prevent adrenal insufficiency. 

Other ARSIs include _**enzalutamide**_ and **_apalutamide_**, which are both androgen receptor antagonists as well as inhibiting subsequent nuclear translocation of the androgen receptor after binding. Side effects include hypertension, fatigue, cognitive changes, and falls. **_Darolutamide_** is a related drug with structural differences that lower CNS penetration, leading to less fatigue.

#### PARP Inhibitors
These have a role in patients with a demonstrated homologous recombination repair deficiency (i.e. BRCA positive) tumour. Benefit is limited with other HRR gene mutations, and appears to be minimal in patients who are not HRR deficient. Utilised in castrate-resistant disease. 

#### PSMA Radionucleotide 
Prostate-specific membrane antigen is _highly_ expressed in prostate cancer, but minimally so elsewhere. In particular, levels are highest in metastatic castrate-resistant prostate cancer. These can be targeted with radionuclides (lutetium-88)

#### Sipuleucel-T
Autologous APCs activated _ex vivo_ with a recombinant protein consisting of prostatic acid phosphatase fused to GM-CSF. Used in castrate resistant prostate cancer. The [*IMPACT* study](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1001294#sec-3) found that sipuleucel-T was associated with improved mOS but not mPFS when compared to placebo, likely due to the delayed onset of antitumour effect with such treatment.
