### Overview
Melanoma is a _very_ common cancer in Australia and New Zealand that arises when there is a mismatch between skin phenotype and ultraviolet exposure.

Phenotypes of melanoma include:
- Cutaneous melanoma - classic among those with fair skin and hair with UV exposure; increased risk with age and immunosuppression
- Acral melanoma - more common in the nails and palms of those with darker skin
- Mucosal melanoma 
- Uveal melanoma - arises from neuroectodermal melanocytes 

> [!TIP] Mnemonic
> > _Beware the man with a glass eye and a large liver_
> Uveal melanoma most frequently metastasises to the liver.
- - -
### Pathobiology
Key mutations involved in the pathogenesis of melanoma tend to trigger constitutional activation of the RAS/RAF/MEK/MAPK pathway, and incude:
- Cutaneous melanoma:
  - BRAF (V600E)
  - NRAS
  - NF1
- Mucosal melanoma:
  - KIT
  - SF3B1
- Uveal melanoma:
  - G proteins

> [!NOTE] Other Skin Cancers
> In contrast, the most common mutations in other kinds of skin cancers are:
> - BCC = _PTCH1_ (hedgehog signalling) or _TP53_
> - Cutaneous SCC = multiple rare syndrome, **but** strong risk from immunosuppression, particularly with AZA

### Staging Melanoma
_T stage_:
- Based on thickness of the melanoma
- A or B qualifiers to denote ulceration

_N stage_:
- Based on number of involved lymph nodes

_M stage_:
- Based on presence of metastases
- A, B, or C qualifiers to denote skin/nodes only, lung only, or visceral organs

The vast majority of melanomas (>75%) are detected at stage I.
- - -
### Prognosis
As a general rule, melanoma is bad, but if it’s anywhere other than the skin it’s _especially_ bad. 

> [!TIP] Historical Point
> Bob Marley died of an _acral lentiginous_ melanoma - a type of melanoma that appears on the nailbed and more commonly affects people of African descent than other types of skin cancer.

Several factors affect prognosis:
- Depth
- Ulceration 
- Mitotic rate
- Location - limb more favourable than trunk
- Sex - women do better than men

### Treatment
#### Stage I-III Melanoma
Surgical resection with sentinel lymph node biopsy is the treatment of choice for early stage melanoma. Stage IIIb/c disease warrants 12 months of adjuvant therapy:
- All patients -> PD1 inhibition (e.g. pembrolizumab)
- _BRAF_ mutated -> BRAF + MEK inhibition (e.g. dabrafenib + trametinib)

#### Stage IV Melanoma
##### BRAF Inhibition + downstream MEK Inhibition:
- Vemurafenib + cobimetinib
- Dabrafenib + trametinib
- Binimetinib + binimetinib

##### [[Immune Checkpoint Inhibition]]
- CTLA4 inhibition = ipilimumab
- PD1 inhibition = nivolumab, pembrolizumab
- LAG3 inhibition = relatlimab

Unlike cytotoxic chemotherapy, immunotherapy can still confer survival benefit even if there is an initial increase in tumour burden or overall shrinkage despite some new lesions. 

Combination therapy with IPI-NIVO has better overall survival, but increased rates of significant toxic side effects. Newer studies examining REL-NIVO appear to show slightly lower rates of adverse events. 

Brain metastases are _very_ common in melanoma:
- ≤3 metastases -> stereostatic radiotherapy or surgery
- 3+ metastases -> BRAF/MEK inhibition or ICI as above (noting that adjunctive steroids would need to be ceased to allow these drugs to work)

##### Obsolete Treatments
- [[Interleukin 2]] 

In general, the treatment algorithm is:
- BRAF+ -> CTLA4i + PD1i -> BRAFi + MEKi (order is flexible)
  - Combination BRAF/MEKi therapy is superior to monotherapy alone, but is more likely to cause fever (yet less likely to cause proliferative skin lesions)
- BRAF- -> either combination immune checkpoint or sequential
