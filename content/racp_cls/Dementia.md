### Overview
Defined in the ICD-11 as a syndrome, due to disease of the brain, that is chronic, progressive, and involves a decline in two or more cognitive domains (not necessarily memory) that significantly interferes with independence in the person’s performance in activities of daily living.

> [!NOTE] What is dementia?
> An _acquired_, _global_ cognitive impairment that leads to an _impairment_ in functional status.

A related condition in ICD-11 is _mild neurocognitive disorder_ that invovles a _subjective_ experience of decline in cognitive functioning accompanied by objective evidence of impairment in performance on one or more cognitive domains relative to age and level of intellectual functioning, but not sufficient to significantly interfere with independence in ADLs and not attributable to normal aging. 

> [!NOTE] Domains of Cognition 
> Learning and memory
> Social cognition
> Complex attention 
> Executive function
> Perceptual-motor function
> Language 

Of all risk factors, _hearing_ is the most significant contributor to dementia. 

> [!NOTE] Patterns of Memory Loss
> Memories need to be _registered_, _encoded_, and then _recalled_. 
> 1. Hippocampal - due to temporoparietal dysfunction
>    - Failure of encoding  -> even if prompted, patients cannot recall memories
>    - Characteristic of Alzheimer dementia
> 2. Dysexecutive
>    - Failure of registration and recall -> generally can recall things with prompting

### Epidemiology
The number one risk factor for dementia is age, and prevalence doubles every 5y after age 60y, corroborating this:
- 60-64y =1%
- 65-69y = 2%
- 70-74y = 4%
- 75-79y = 8%
- 80-84y = 16%
- 85y+ = 32%

Increased prevalence in the Aboriginal community, mostly due to rates of AD, vascular disease, and head injury, rather than alcohol.

In terms of prevalence:
- Alzheimer dementia (probably ~2/3rds of all dementia)
- Vascular dementia 
- Dementia with Lewy bodies

### Pathophysiology
Pathologically, the dementias can be grouped into three broad categories:
- Synucleinopathies -> abnormal aggregations of ⍺-synulcein within neurons
  - e.g. PD, DLB, MSA
- Tauopathies -> aggregations of tau protein
  - e.g. AD, PSP, CBD, FTD
  - Neurofibrillary tangles are the best correlate of neuronal loss 
- Amyloidopathies - e.g. AD, CAA

The most common cause of dementia is [[Alzheimer disease]] (~85%), but in almost all cases this is a mixed picture involving:
- Neuropathological changes characteristic of AD (=ADNC) - i.e. amyloid, tau
- Vascular disease 
- Lewy bodies = accumulations of ⍺-synuclein
- [[TDP-43]] 

“Pure” vascular dementia is actually quite rare - perhaps <10% of cases. Instead, vascular disease is often comorbid in patients with dementia, and associated with reduced life expectancy.
