# Rheumatoid Arthritis

### Overview
Rheumatoid arthtitis is a common, systemic, autoimmune disease that is characterised by autoantibody production and synovial inflammation and hyperplasia. 
- - -
### Epidemiology
0.5-1% prevalence.
Onset between 30-60y of age.
Female predominance (3:1).

Strong association with cardiovascular disease, leading to a 50% increased risk of mortality and reduced life expectancy of ~10y.

Late age of onset is generally associated with a milder disease course. 
- - -
### Pathophysiology
Genetic factors seem to convey ~60% of risk, with particular genes (e.g. HLA-DRB1) playing an important role. The remaining risk comes from environmental factors, such as smoking (the strongest environmental risk factor), occupational inhaled particles, microbiota, inflammatory diet, etc.
![](Rheumatoid%20Arthritis/image.png)
Mucosal sites also seem to play an important part in the development of autoimmunity in RA. In particular, exposure of the lung mucosa to tobacco smoke (esp. >20py) can increase the risk of RA as much as fortyfold. Similarly, gingivitis of the oral mucosa (which involves the citrullination of potential antigens in the mouth; and the microbiome in the GI mucosa can do similarly. 

The inflammatory changes in the synovium are characterised by frond-like villous structure, and the development of _panni_ and the bone-synovium interface, which is what ultimately causes bone erosion. On histology, we see hyperplasia and increased vascularity.

Like most immune pathologies, RA likely begins with APCs in the lymph nodes presenting citrullinated peptides to T-cells in the lymph nodes. The key cytokines implicated in the subsequent inflammatory process are TNFα, IL-6, IL-17, RANK-L, and the Janus kinases. These molecules act on white cells, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, osteoclasts, and cartilage to induce inflammation. 

Key genes:
- HLA-DRB1 - strongest genetic risk factor
- _PTPN22_ SNP -> abnormal T-cell signalling (RR ~1.8)
- _IL2RA_ 

Other important pathways include:
- BAFF/BLYS/APRIL -> activation of B-cells and production of autoantibodies
- GM-CSF, M-CSF -> enhancement of granulocyte differentiation
- RANKL/OPG/WNT -> maturation and activation of osteoclasts 

#### Autoantibodies in RA
Autoantibodies in RA are directed against widely-expressed antigens and not _just_ joint specific structures. They include:
- Rheumatoid Factor:
  - IgM, less commonly IgG
  - Moderate to high specificity
  - Directed at the Fc portion of IgG
  - Positive in 75% of patients with RA 
  - Tend to predict more severe clinical disease, extra-articular manifestations, and cardiac risk
  - Associated with several non-RA diseases: Sjögren, cryoglobulinaemia, SLE, hepatitis, cancer, and IE
- ACPA:
  - High specificity (>95%)
  - Directed at citrullinated proteins, the hallmark of the immune response in RA
  - ’Citrullination’ is the post-translational modification of peptidylarginine to peptidylcitrulline
  - ACPAs precede onset and are an important predictor of RA
  - Predict more severe and destructive disease, and are associated with ILD and cardiac disease
  - May directly contribute to bone disease 
- Anti-PAD4:
  - Moderate specificity
  - Directed at peptidyl arginine deiminase 4
- Anti-CARP:
  - Moderate specificity 
  - Directed at carbamylated proteins
- Anti-MDA/Anti-MMA:
  - Unknwon specificity
  - Directed at (anti-)malondialdehyde-acetate

### Clinical Features
#### Patterns of Disease Onset
This tends to be an insidious onset over weeks to months, typically with systemic or articular morning stiffness. Joint manifestations are typically in the small joints, and may be asymmetric.

About ~15-30% of patients have acute onset, and this is associated with a better prognosis, likely due to lead time bias. 

In more rare cases, patients can present with _palindromic rheumatism_, the waxing and waning od articular pain over the course of hours to days. About half of these patients go on to develop RA, typically if they are ACPA positive or have significantly elevated inflammatory markers. 

Elderly patients tend to experience more limb girdle pain, and swelling of the exremities, with stiffness being the more predominant feature as compared to synovitis.

One well characterised syndrome is ‘RS3PE’ - remitting, seronegative, symmetric synovitis with pitting oedema (of the hands and feet), which can often be paraneoplastic and which responds well to corticosteroids. 

#### The Cervical Spine
As there is synovial membrane in the atlantoaxial joint, it can be affected by RA. This can cause subluxation and anklyosis, as well as neurologic symptoms.

#### The Hands and Wrists
The ‘classic’ phenotype of RA in the hands is:
- Radial deviation at the wrist
- Ulnar deviation of the fingers
- Severe inflammation of the MCP and PIP joints, with DIP sparing
- Swan-neck and boutonniere deformities

#### The Feet
The signs and symptoms of RA in the feet include hindfoot synovitis, with pronation and eversion fo the foot, as well as forefoot synovitis, with subluxation of the metatarsal heads. It is not uncommon for the feet to be affected first, often with the 5th MTP being the first affected.

#### Dermatologic Manifestfations
- Rheumatoid nodules - commonly seen in seropositive patients, particularly those treatment with methotrexate alone; typically on extensor surfaces and pressure points, but can occur on any skin or visceral surface
- Vasculitis - tends to be SVV/MVV, portending a poor prognosis
- Ulcers
- Neutrophilic dermatoses
- Treatment related rashes

#### Ocular Manifestations
These are rare, and almost alawys only occur in seropositive patients
- Episcleritis
- Scleritis (with the complication of scleromalacia perforans)
- Perilimbic ischaemic ulcers -> anterior chamber perforation (associated with cryoglobulins)
- Secondary Sjögren syndrome/xerophthalmica 

#### Pulmonary Manifestations
- Pleuritis/pleural effusion - usually exudative with very low glucose
- Nodules - more common in males; may be cavitating
- Cricoarytenoid arthritis
- ILD - NSIP/UIP > organising pneumonia > others
- Bronchiectasis

#### Cardiovascular Manifestations
- Premature atherosclerosis and CAD
  - Driven by inflammation + high frequency of traditional risk factors
  - 2x risk compared to general population - comparable to diabetes
  - DMARDs may modify this risk
- Pericarditis/pericardial effusion
- Arrhythmias
- Myocarditis
- HFpEF

#### Haematologic Manifestations
- Felty Syndrome - RA + splenomegaly + neutropenia 
- Lymphoma (x3 risk)
- Amyloidosis
- Cryoglobulinaemia
- Large granular lymphocyte syndrome

#### Other Extraarticular Manifestations
- GI - xerostomia, gastritis, stomatitis
- Renal - glomerulonephritis (usually mesangioproliferative), proteinuria, ADRs
- Hepatic - hyperplasia, fibrosis, cirrhosis

### Diagnosis
#### Radiographic Features of RA
Typically, we see:
- Periarticular soft-tissue swelling
- Juxta-articular osteoporosis
- Marginal erosions
- Joint space narrowing
- Symmetric involvement
- Deformities in advanced disease 

![](Rheumatoid%20Arthritis/image%202.png)
USS and MRI tend to be more sensitive than XR and clinical examination alone. Ultrasonographic signs of RA include:
- Pannus formation
- Erosions
- Increased blood flow 
![](Rheumatoid%20Arthritis/image%204.png)

For patients with synovtis not better explained by another disease, diagnosis is made using the ACR/EULAR Criteria:
- Joints: more joints and small joints accrue more points, up to a maximum of **5**
- Serology: high titres of RF and ACPA accrue more points, up to a maximum of **3**
- Acute phase reactants: **1** point if positive 
- Duration of symptoms: **1** point if >6/52

Definite RA requires at least 6 points.  Note that ACPA positivity and arthralgia alone does not make the diagnosis of RA, and does not indicate treatment.

### Management
Key principles:
1. Early treatment
2. Remission is an achievable goal - measured by the DAS-28 score 
3. Treat-to-target
4. Escalate rapidly
5. Consider bDMARDs if ongoing disease after 2 or more cDMARDs for 6/12
6. Measuring disease activity leads to less disease activity (clinical disease activity/e.g. DAS28 > imaging.- i.e. imaging is not a treatment target)

#### Conventional Synthetic DMARDs
MTX:
- The most important and commonly used cDMARD
- Optimal dose ~25mg/w, consider SC if dose >15mg/w + needs to be given with folate
- Efficacy of 25-40%
- Adverse effects (nausea, mouth ulecers, cytopenia) usually preventable with folate
- Good if used in combination, particularly with prednisolone as first line therapy - the ‘anchor’ drug for DMARD regiments 
- Teratogenic
- _Not_ associated with testicular toxicity
- Hepatotoxic in addition with alcohol
- Can cause acute pneurmonitis _but not_ subacute ILD 

SSZ:
- MoA unknown
- Efficacy of ~8% at 2g/d (range 2-4g/d divided dose)
- ADRs: hypersensitivity, nausea, diarrhoea, agranulocytosis, DILE
- Monitor FBE and LFT

HCQ:
- Antimalarial, well-tolerated, MoA uncertain
- Poor response rate but seems to work in combination
- ADRs: hyperpigmentation, retinopathy (if dose >5mg/kg), myopathy/cardiomyopathy

LEF: 
- Dihydroorotate dehydrogenase inhibitor -> reduced pyrimidine synthesis -> reduced T-cell activation
- 10% response rate
- ADRs: diarrhoea, hypertension, allergies, LFTs, pneumonitis, leucocytopenia, peripheral neuropathy
- Monitor with quarterly FBE/LFT

AZA, CYC, and gold are rarely used in the 21st century.

#### Biologic and Targeted Synthetic DMARDs
Tend _not_ to be used in combination with one another, bur rather in combination with cDMARDs, due to the risk of infection (similarly, live vaccinations are inhibited). Reserved for patients who have failed multiple/combination cDMARDs.

TNF inhibition
- Infliximab, etanercept, adalimumab, golimumab certolizumab
- Tend to exert clinical effect within week
- Response rate of ~20% of those who fail methotrexate
- Benefit seems to be most when in combination with MTX/LEF
- Maintaining clinical effect needs ongoing treatment
- Certolizumab _may_ be the safest in pregnancy
- Contraindications: SLE, demyelination, seriou s infection, HBV infection, CHF, pregnancy
- ADRs: infections/reactivation, malignancy (esp. skin, lymphoma), demyelinating syndromes, autoantibodies, hepatotoxicity, lupus-like syndromes

T-cell costimulation (CD80/CD86) inhibition
- Abatacept (recombinant molecule binding to CTLA4)
- Response rate similar to TNFi
- Probably most effective in seropositive patients in combination with MTX
- ADRs: lower risk of infection, leucocytopenia, injection site reactions

Anti-CD20/B-cell depletor:
- Rituximab
- Good response rate, particularly in patients who have failed TNFi and are seropositive
- Less common now given variety of drugs available
- ADRs: infections, infusion reactions, PML, diminished response to vaccination, reactivation of HBV 

IL-6 inhibition:
- Tocilizumab, sarilumab
- Good efficacy, can be used in monotherapy
- Preferred option when intolerant of cDMARDs
- Note that as IL-6 drives inflammation, patients can fail to mount a CRP response if on this drug
- ADRs: infections/reactivations; deranged LFTs, hyperlipidaemia, cytopenias, GI/diverticular perforation; hypersensitivity

JAK inhibition:
- Tofacitinib, baricitinib, upadacitinib
- Clinical efficacy as monotherapy or in combination with MTX, similar rate to TNFi
- Quick onset of action
- ADRs: infections/reactivation, Herpes zoster, cytopenias, hyperlipidaemia, cardiac risk, cancer risk 
- [_ORAL-Surveillance_ study](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2109927) - signal re: increased risk of MACE with tofacitinib cf. TNF-inhibition, but appears to be mostly in patients >65y

PD1 Agonism:
- Peresolimab
- i.e. the _opposite_ of immune checkpoint inhibition

![](Rheumatoid%20Arthritis/image%203.png)

If the decision is made to taper a drug, this should be done slowly, with at least one DMARD remaining in therapeutic range. Dose reduction should only occur if there has been reduced disease activity/remission for >6/12.
- cDMARDs: cease in the order SSZ -> HCQ -> MTX
- bDMARDs: cease MTX first
