# Gastro-oesophageal Reflux Disease
#bpt/gastroenterology #bpt/racp_cls
- - -
### What is gastro-oesophageal reflux disease?
GORD is the result of excessive exposure of the distal oesophagus to the contents of the stomach, particularly acid, pepsin, and bile salts. This is compounded by reduced mucosal defences and increased sensitivity, leading to symptoms and complications of disease. 

Although most individuals will have some reflux, particularly postprandially, when this becomes excessive, recumbent, or symptomatic, it is said to be GORD.
- - -
### Risk Factors
- Age
- Obesity 
- Hiatus hernia
- Smoking
- Family history
- Connective tissue diseases 
- Pregnancy

Infection with *H. pylori* may be slightly protective. 

Some medications have the potential to damage the oesophageal mucosa:
- Aspirin/NSAIDs
- Doxycycline
- Quinidine
- Bisphosphonates

While others can impair sphincter function:
- β-agonists 
- Theophylline
- Anticholinergics
- TCAs
- Progesterone
- ⍺-antagonists 
- Diazepam
- CCBs
***
### Epidemiology
Common, particularly in the Western world. There appears to be a genetic component. 
- - -
### Pathophysiology
In normal individuals, the gastro-oesophageal junction comprises two portions:
- Intrinsic sphincter
  - Comprised of circular oesophageal muscles supplemented by sling fibres of the stomach
  - Normal tone is cholinergic 
- Diaphragmatic crux

Additional support is provided by the oblique entry of the oesophagus into the stomach. A number of mechanisms can lead to the development of GORD:
- Transient lower oesophageal sphincter relaxation - vagally mediated response to proximal gastric distension
- Weak lower oesophageal sphincter 
- Increased intra-abdominal pressure 
- - -
### Damage in GORD
The relationship between the presence and severity of symptoms with the degree of oesophagitis is not strong. Most individuals will have some degree of oesophageal acidification, particularly postprandially. Unchecked acidification will lead to:
- Ulceration and/or bleeding
- Stricture formation
- [[Barrett oesophagus]] - metaplasia of squamous to columnar epithelium
- Adenocarcinoma of the oesophagus 
- - -
### Clinical Presentation 
Cardinal symptoms:
- Heartburn
- Regurgitation

Nonspecific symptoms:
- Nausea
- Epigastric pain
- Respiratory - cough, worsening asthma
- ENT/dental complications
- Chest pain
- Dysphagia
- Sleep disturbance 

In addition to this, the complications of GORD can lead to specific symptoms that are ‘red flags’ for investigation:
- Dysphagia/odynophagia
- Haematemesis
- Weight loss
- - -
### Diagnosis
GORD is a clinical diagnosis based on the cardinal symptoms combined with a response to acid suppression. Endoscopy is not typically required, and is only carried out to investigate potential alternate diagnoses, atypical symptoms, or a failure of acid suppression. pH studies can be used to exclude reflux as a cause of symptoms.

Patients with atypical symptoms should typically undergo a long trial (~8w) of high dose PPI. 
- - -
### Management 
The first line of management is lifestyle measures, including weight reduction, avoidance of fatty/spicy foods, avoidance of late meals, sleeping with the head of the bed up, and smoking cessation. 

The mainstay of medical therapy is acid suppression with proton-pump inhibitors, which can moreover allow oesophagus to heal. These bind irreversible to H+/K+-ATPase pumps in the parietal cells to suppress basal and stimulated acid secretion. If PPIs are ceased there is a risk of rebound acid hypersecretion. There is a role of prokinetics, but this is minimal. 

Surgery is indicated if medical therapy fails, and is typically a laparoscopic fundoplication. Alternatives include long-term acid suppression, endoscopic fundoplication, and other surgeries. Side-effects of surgery typically include flatus, dysphagia, and long-term failure.
