### Overview
Acromegaly, the syndrome of growth hormone excess, is most often caused by pituitary adenoma (in rare cases, other malignancies can secrete GHRH). The clinical course is of insidious onset of soft tissue swelling and bony enlargement, with symptoms such as:
- Frontal bossing
- Macrognathia
- Splayed teeth
- Large tongue
- Skin tags (particularly on the thorax)
- Doughly hands
- Increased shoe size

If GH excess sets on before closure of the epiphyseal plates, gigantism can ensue. If untreated, complications of acromegaly include:
- Hypertension
- Diabetes
- OSA
- Cardiomyopathy
- Colonic polyps/CRC
- Carpal tunnel syndrome
- Arthritis 
- Increased mortality 

Diagnosis of acromegaly is through (elevated) serum levels of IGF-1 in the first instance. This is a   sensitive test that can safely rule out acromegaly. Alternatively, an OGTT should suppress GH to nearly undetectable levels (<0.4ng/mL). This should be followed by an MRI of the pituitary to evaluate for an adenoma, which is the most common underlying cause of acromegaly (~70%). 

Treatment is usually with transsphenoidal surgery, with a higher cure rate for microadenomas. Many patients require subsequent treatment with long-acting injectable somatostatin analogues, acting on the SST2 receptor:
- Octreotide
- Lanreotide

Third-line treatment depends on patient characteristics:
- Cabergoline -> DA agonist, greatest effect if cosecretion of prolactin
- Pasireotide -> SST5 antagonist (also present on pancreatic β-cells), can worsen hyperglycaemia (can treat with GLP1 agonists)
- Pegvisomant -> GHR antagonist, no effect on adenoma (note that GH levels will still remain elevated)
- Radiotherapy -> very effective, but significant risk of hypopituitarism
