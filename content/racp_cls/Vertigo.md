### What is vertigo?
Vertigo is an illusory sensation of movement that is usually aggravated by head movements. The range of causes for vertigo is broad. The vast majority of vertigo is due to peripheral or benign central (e.g. migraine) causes, although there are certain key critical differentials. 

#### Red Flag Signs:
- Other neurological signs
- Ataxia out of proportion to vertigo 
- Nystagmus out of proportion to vertigo
- Central nystagmus - vertical, gaze-evoked, dissociated, pendular
- Central eye movement abnormalities - broken pursuit, gaze palsy, dysmetric/slow saccades, skew deviation 
- - -
### Pathophysiology
Balance is regulated in the ears by the horizontal canals, which fire at a basal rate. When we move our heads to one side, it stimulates the horizontal canal on the ipsilateral side and inhibits the contralateral horizontal canal, leading to asymmetric firing rates. If there is damage to one horizontal canal, it leads to a dramatic asymmetry in the firing rates, creating the sensation of movement (vertigo) and abnormal eye movements (nystagmus).

#### Anatomy
Vertigo is largely due to dysfunction in or of the three semicircular canals that regulate rotational movement: 
- Posterior
- Anterior
- Horizontal
Each of these has an ampulla at its base, as well as two _otolith_ organs that regulate horizontal and vertical movements:
- Utricle
- Saccule 

Within the otolith organs, hair cells protude into the otolithic membrane where they fire at a constant rate:
![](Vertigo/image.png)
On movement, the force of the weight of the otoconia pulls the membrane and distorts the hair cells, increasing the rate of firing.

A similar process unfolds in the ampulla. The whole organ is filled with endolymph, and within the ampulla there is an organ called the cupola. Movements of the head move the fluid through the semicircular canals, which displace the cupola which is sensed as movement:
![](Vertigo/image%203.png)
Normally, the antagonistic relationship between the left and right organs leads to the system’s ability to recognise movement in space:
![](Vertigo/image%204.png)
This is also involved in the vestibuloocular reflex with the PPRF to maintain gaze.

### Classification
Vertigo can either be due to central or peripheral causes:
|                     | Peripheral     | Central                    |
|---------------------|----------------|----------------------------|
| Associated Symptom  | Auditory       | CNS                        |
| Nystagmus           | Unidirectional | Unidirectional and central |
| Other eye movements | Normal         | May be abnormal            |

Peripheral vertigo is typically accompanied by peripheral vestibular nystagmus, which is characterised by being:
- unidirectional
- directed away from the side of the lesion
- horizontal-torsional 
- rapidly adapting
- suppressed by visual fixation (due to there being an intact vestibulo-ocular reflex)

### Diagnostic Approach
- Temporal course -> acute, chronic, or episodic
- Triggers -> spontaneous or motion-induced 

This leads to three broad diagnostic groups:
- Acute vestibular syndrome:
  - Vestibular neuritis = acute viral (HSV) infection of superior vestibular nerve, associated with gait ataxia, and positive head impulse test; usually no hearing deficit; may be a role of steroids 
  - Posterior circulation stroke -> positive HINTS exam:
    - Normal head impulse test 
    - Direction changing nystagmus
    - Skew deviation (eyes separated vertically when one is closed) 
    - (+ hearing loss)
- Episodic vestibular syndrome:
  - Spontaneous vertigo:
    - Vestibular migraine - can result with a variety of symptoms, ‘classically’ recurrent episodic vertigo with or without headache 
    - Meniere disease - characterised by episodic vertigo (lasting 20m-12h), sensorineural hearing loss, and a sensation of fullness in the ear 
    - Vertebrobasilar ischaemia 
  - Motion-triggered vertigo:
    - BPPV - diagnosed by the Dix-Halpike manoeuvre and treated with the Epley manoeuvre; caused by otoliths in the semicircular canals (most commonly the posterior canal); characterised by brief episodes of motion-triggered vertigo; nystagmus direction is related to the canal involved:
      - Posterior -> upbeat nystagmus
      - Anterior canal -> downbeat nystagmus
      - Lateral canal -> geotropic/apogeotropic (essentially, horizontal) nystagmus
    - Central positional vertigo

> [!TIP] Mnemonic
> _Up the back, down the front, side to side_
> Posterior canal -> upbeat nystagmus
> Anterior canal -> downbeat nystagmus
> Lateral canal -> horizontal nystagmus

- Chronic vestibular syndrome:
  - CNS -> cerebellar disease, NPH, multi-infarct state
  - Proprioceptive loss -> spinal disease, peripheral neuropathy
  - Other -> aging, hypothyroidism, multisensory, etc.
  - Bilateral vestibulopathy -> often secondary to aminoglycoside toxicity
  - Persistent postural-perceptual dizziness -> [[functional neurological disorder]]
  - Mal de débarquement
