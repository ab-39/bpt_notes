### What is the anion gap?
Put simply, the anion gap is the difference between the concentrations of the most numerous anions and cations in the blood. It helps us to calculate what proportion of a change in acid-base balance is attributable to additional anions. 

Derivation:
> `[Na+ + K+ UC] = [HCO3- + Cl- + UA]`
> `∴`
> `[Na+ + K+] - [HCO3- + Cl-] = [UA - UC]`

This value, the difference between serum unmeasured anions and unmeasured cations ( [UA-] — [UC+]) is the anion gap. But, because we obviously don’t know what we can’t measure, we can see that the anion gap is equivalent to:
> `[Na+ + K+] - [HCO3- + Cl-]`

A normal value is in the vicinity of 11mmol/L, but may need to be corrected for (negatively charged) albumin. 

The main use of the anion gap is to separate the different causes of [[metabolic acidosis]]. All metabolic acidoses involve a decrease in [HCO3-], and the laws of electrochemical neutrality mandate that this must be balanced by an increase in an other anion. Either this will be chloride, in which case the derived anion gap will be normal, or it will be an unmeasured anion, in which case the derived anion gap will be high. 
- - -
### Normal Anion Gap Metabolic Acidosis 
Normal anion gap metabolic acidosis suggests that the loss of HCO3- from the ECF is being chemically compensated for by an increase in [Cl-]:
- Diarrhoea/fistulae  
- Renal wasting (e.g. renal tubular acidosis) 
- Addison’s disease
- Chloride administration

A handy acronym here is ABCD: Addison’s, bicarbonate wasting (GI or renal), chloride excess, diarrhoea.
- - -
### High Anion Gap Metabolic Acidosis 
A high anion gap metabolic acidosis suggests that the loss of HCO3- is being compensated for by some unmeasured anion:
- Lactate
- Toxic alcohols
- Ketones 
- Urate
- Isoniazid
- Salicylates

The ‘classic’ mnemonic here is MUDPILES - methanol, uraemia, DKA, pyroglutamic acidosis, iron/isoniazid, lactate, ethylene glycol, salicylates
