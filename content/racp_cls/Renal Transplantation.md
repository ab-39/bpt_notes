### Overview
Renal transplantation is an intervention that saves lives and improves their quality. It is associated with increased life expectancy and quality of life. It can be accessed in two ways in Australia:
- Deceased donor waiting list - _after_ the patient has commenced dialysis
- Living donor - either a direct donor or on the Kidney Exchange programme
  - Note that living donor transplants appear to have better outcomes
  - There is a risk of HLA-sensitisation which makes AKX a complex option
- - -
###  Criteria for Living Kidney Donors
- Low preoperative risks
- Acceptable lifetime ESRF risk with informed consent
  - Note that post-donation risk can be different from pre-donation risk
  - Factors to consider include age (risk is higher for younger donors!), hypertension, diabetes, BMI, eGFR, albuminuria, smoking, and others 
  - Also important to consider psychosocial situation - is there coercion?
  - Must consider infection and cancer status as this can be
  - A healthy living donoe has a <1% lifetime risk of developing ESRF

#### Autosomal Dominant Polycystic Kidney Disease
When screening FDRs as possible donors for ADPKD, the decision about the donor’s disease state can be made in one of two ways:
1. Donor candidate is >40y and has ≤1 cyst on USS kidney -> no ADPKD -> proceed with donation
2. Recipient is screened for _PKD1_ and _PKD2_ -> donor is screened for relevant mutation -> if no mutation, proceed with donation

### Deceased Donation
Deceased donation is typically the most common source of donor kidneys. Factors that can increase deceased donor rates include:
- Ensuring all potential deceased donor families are asked by specified transplan oriented personnel

### Criteria for Recipient Eligibility (Deceased Donor List)
- Kidney failure requiring dialysis
  - Although pre-emptive transplantation (i.e. transplant _before_ dialysis) is associated with the best long-term outcomes, this is typically not possible due to comorbidities and the scarcity of donor kidneys
- Low preoperative mortality risk
  - This is the goal of pre-transplant workup
- Reasonable post-transplant patient and graft survival with a high likelihood of significant benefit from transplantation
- Age is _not_ a contraindication, but comorbidities need to be considered 
- - -
### Matching
Matching needs to be carried out for:
- ABO antigens
  - ABO incompatible transplants are possible with pre-transplant immunodepletion
  - Rates of ABMR tend to be higher but overall graft survival tends to be similar
  - Although the A/B antibodies can recur, they tend not to cause graft rejection
- Human leucocyte antigens, particularly at the A, B, and DR loci
  - Although desensitisation is also possible for HLA antigens, the outcomes tend to be worse 

Previous exposure to non-self tissue can create problems with HLA-matching, as eplets (specific HLA epitopes) are often shared between multiple HLA proteins. This means that exposure to one HLA can result in the formation of antibodies against _several_ HLAs. 

The _luminex_ test is used to test for HLA antibodies, and involves antibody beads that fluoresce when exposed to the relevant antibodies. The greater the titre of antibody, the stronger the fluorescence (reported as the MFI - median fluorescent intensity).

### Immunosuppression
Induction is typically with _**basiliximab**_ - an anti-IL-2R/CD25 monoclonal antibody (SYMPHONY trial) to reduce the activity of activated T-cells. Treatment is generally well tolerated.   The alternative option is ***anti-thymocyte globulin*** (ATG), polyclonal antibodies which target T-cells and many other cell types. It has a lower incidence of acute rejection in patients at higher immunological risk, but long term benefit is less clear. There is an increased risk of infections and malignancies. 
 
This is followed by triple therapy for maintenance: 
1. Glucocorticoids - prednisolone
2. Calcineurin inhibitions - tacrolimus (TAC, common) or cyclosporine (CsA, rare)
   - Both are nephrotoxic both acutely and chronically, and are associated with hypertension and dyslipidaemia 
   - They are non-myelosuppressive, and safe in pregnancy
   - CsA is associated with hirsutism in females 
   - Compared to CsA, TAC is associated with less acute rejection/early graft loss and fewer drug interactions, but has a higher risk of post-transplant diabetes, electrolyte abnormalities, and neurotoxicity/tremor
3. Antimetabolite - typically mycophenolate (MMF/MPA) or else azathioprine (AZA)
   - MMF/MPA - inhibitors of purine synthesis (via IMPDH) - less acute rejection and graft loss than AZA - **but** fewer drug interactions (particularly with allopurinol), no problem with TPMT
   - AZA >> MMF/MPA in terms of pregnancy safety, and also myelosuppressive leading to pancytopenia and opportunistic infections
   - MMF/MPA levels lowered by CsA (but not TAC); associated with diarrhoea 

Alternate possibilities include the MTOR inhibitors sirolimus (rapamycin) and everolimus. These were previously used as calcineurin inhibitor-sparing agents, but this lead to _more_ rejection. More recently, they are used in combination with low-dose CNI and prednisolone to replace an antimetabolite. Key points:
- EVR is much more common than SRL due to favourable ADR profile 
- Benefits: less cancer (typically skin), lower risk of CMV infection
- Side-effects: wound complications, proteinuria, cytopenias (when used in combination with antimetabolites), dyslipidaemia, mouth ulcers, oedema, interstitial pneumonitis 
- All are contraindicated in pregnancy. 

In patients with ABO or HLA antibodies requiring desensitisation, the agent used is ***imlifidase***, a cysteine protease that cleaves IgG leading to a rapid decrease in atibody levels. 

Torque teno virus, a nonpathogenic anellovirus, can be used as a marker of the degree of immunosuppression.

### Infective Complications of Immunosuppression 
The likelihood of various infections varies with the time after transplant:
1. Early (<1m):
   - Donor-derived infections (uncommon) - e.g. HIV, rabies, etc.
   - Nosocomial infections - e.g. UTI, aspiration, wound infection, etc.
2. Medium (1-6m):
   - Reactivation - e.g. HBV, HCV, TB, etc. 
   - Opportunistic infections while on immunosuppression - e.g. CMV, PJP, etc.
   - CMV risk:
     - Highest in D+/R- transplants -> six months of CMV prophylaxis
     - Moderate in any R+ transplant -> three months of prophylaxis 
     - Lower in D-/R- transplant -> can monitor 
3. Long (>6m):
   - Community-acquired infections - e.g. CAP, UTI, etc.
   - Latent/opportunistic infections once prophylaxis ceased

### Graft Loss
The most common _overall_ cause of graft loss is _death_ with the transplant still functioning - at 48%!

Early graft loss, in the first year - is largely due to infective (~30%) and cardiovascular (~30%) causes, with cancer being relatively rare (~10%). Beyond one year, however, cancer is the most common cause (30%), with cardiovascular (~20%) and infective (~10%) causes becoming less common. 

When considering only death-censored graft loss, we see a slightly different pattern:
- Early -> graft thrombosis/technical (~40%), rejection (~20%), and glomerulonephritis (~5%)
- Late ->  ‘chronic allograft nephropathy’ (60%, but terminology is phased out), GN (~5%), acute rejection (~5%), nonadherence (~5%)
  - Chronic allograft nephropathy - ?due to CNI toxicity vs chronic-Ab mediated rejection combined with insufficient immunosuppression 

The best predictor of whether a graft will survive is if the patient is adherent to their immunosuppression, and this can be measured with therapeutic drug monitoring.

### Delayed Graft Function
In some cases, the graft can take time to work, with _delayed graft function_ being defined as the requirement for dialysis post-transplantation (although the exact time-definition can vary). The most common cause is post-ischaemic ATN, with important risk factors including:
- Deceased donor (~40%)
  - _Very_ uncommon with living donors, need to exclude other causes
- Donor AKI
- Donor age
- Donor circulatory death
- Cold ischaemia time

There are several other important and possibly critical causes to consider:
- Graft thrombosis - usually not salvageable
- Obstruction/urine leak - uncommon
- Rejection - biopsy after 1 week (or earlier if suspicious)
- Early recurrence - can treat empirically (consider FSGS, thrombotic microangiopathy, oxalises)
- - -
### Early Worsening Graft Function 
Causes to consider of worsening graft function within the first twelve months of transplant:
- Acute rejection - need to exclude this with a biopsy
- CNI toxicity - need TDM
- Renal artery stenosis - relatively rare
- Obstruction +/- leak +/- collection after ureteric stent removal 
- BK polyomavirus nephropathy - diagnosis is usually with detection of DNA in the urine _but_ renal biopsy is gold standard as this will also rule out rejection
- Recurrent disease:
  - Early: primary FSGS, atypical HUS, MPGN, ANCA vasculitis 
  - Late: IgA nephropathy, membranous; MPGN

### Acute Rejection 
Acute rejection can be T-cell mediated (TCMR), B-cell mediated (AbMR), or mixed. When there is rising creatinine or delayed graft function, acute rejection needs to be suspected until proven otherwise with a biopsy. In the setting of graft cell injury, donor-derived cell free DNA is released into the recipient’s bloodstream. Although this can happen with any injury, it appears to be higher in rejection, and can be used as a biomarker in patients who are unsafe for biopsy. 

Risk factors include:
- Previous HLA sensitisation - e.g. repeat transplant, previous transfusion, pregnancy
- Pre-transplant anti-donor antibodies - ABO, HLA, non-HLA
- Younger recipient + older donor
- Ischaemia time or delayed graft function 
- HLA mismatch

Treatment depends on the type of rejection:
- TCMR -> 3/7 pulsed IV methylprednisolone -> optimisation of maintenance immunosuppression (considering ATG if steroid resistant)
- ABMR -> PLEX/IVIG, many emerging therapies including rituximab
- Also need to consider infection prophylaxis for PJP and CMV

### BK Nephropathy
Infection with BK polyoma virus can lead to a progressive graft failure, typically occurring within twelve months of reactivation. The general progression is from viruria -> viraemia -> nephropathy, in a manner that correlates with serum viral load. 

Patients need to be screened for BK virus by serum PCR within the first twelve months. 

Graft dysfunction warrants a kidney biopsy not only to confirm nephropathy, but also exclude other diagnoses including rejection (note that biopsy findings may imitate rejection with interstitial nephritis). Biopsy findings suggestive of BK nephropathy include intranuclear viral inclusions and SV40 staining - need to have medullary tissue to diagnose!

Treatment involves a judicious reduction in immunosuppression, as overdoing it can precipitate rejection - the first line is to reduce dose of the antimetabolite and then the CNI. There is no specific proven antiviral therapy, but it is sometimes used nonetheless (cidofovir would be the drug of choice here, when other options have failed). IVIG can be used as ‘rejection prophylaxis’ during the immunosuppression wean.

### Cancer in the Transplant Patient 
Transplant patients are at almost one hundredfold the risk of cancer compared to the general population, particularly of non-Hodgkin lymphoma, renal cell carcinoma, and melanoma. There is no difference in levels of breast and prostate cancer. 

Cancer deaths in transplant patients are typically due to NHL, lung, or colorectal cancer (noting that the latter two are also common causes of death in the general population). 

Skin cancers are _very_ common in post-transplant patients, and metastatic SCC is a common cause of death. Strategies include AZA avoidance (use MTORi), sun protection, surveillance, and reduction of overall immunosuppression.
