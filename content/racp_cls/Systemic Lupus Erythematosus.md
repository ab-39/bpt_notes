# Systemic Lupus Erythematosus
#bpt/rheumatology #bpt/racp_cls #bpt/obstetrics
- - -
### Overview
Systemic lupus erythematosus, hereafter SLE or lupus, is a complex, chronic, multisystem autoimmune disease that is characterised by diverse clinical manifestations and a relapsing-remitting course. 
- - -
### Epidemiology
Uncommon - incidence of ~1-25/100,000; prevalence varies by race, least common in whites and more common in blacks. More common in urban settings. High rate of concordance in monozygotic twins.

There is a strong female predominance with the majority of cases setting on in the childbearing years. 

Ten-year survival rate of 90%, **but** increased standardised mortality ratio (~3x), typically due to infection/immunosuppression or disease activity early in the disease course, or cardiovascular disease later in the disease course.
- - -
### Pathophysiology
Complex and poorly understood. The key defect is a loss of self-tolerance to autoantigens, which form immune complexes with autoantibodies and trigger the inflammatory cascade. Of particular note are:
- Toll-like receptors
- B-cell survival factors: BAFF/BLYS/APRIL
- Microbiome

Like most conditions, there appears to be an interaction between genetic, environmental, immunological, and hormonal factors that ultimately lead to the formation of immune complexes and autoantibodies that trigger cytokine release and immune cell activation leading to organ damage and inflammation. Another theory is related to the formation of ‘neutrophil extracellular traps’ which are involved in the activation of neutrophils:

#### Genetics and Lupus
Most patients likely have a combination of common variations in multiple genes, and, in face, over sixty risk loci have been identified, both on sex and somatic chromosomes. Monogenic defects causing SLE are rare, but seem to confer significantly increased risk (HR ~25):
- Deficiencies of (early) [[complement]] components -> e.g. low C1q activity leads to defective phagocytosis of apoptotic material, a proposed antigenic target in lupus
- _TREX1_ gene mutations -> defective 3’-repair endonuclease 1 -> impaired degradation of DNA 

Other important susceptibility loci include:
- MHC - particularly HLA-DR2, DR3, and DRB1
- IFN-related genes - particularly IRF5, IRAK1, TLR7, and STAT4
- T/B cell signalling-related genes - e.g. PTPN22, PD-1, BLK
- Immune complex clearance - e.g. C1q, C4, FcγRIIa/IIIb
- Other - e.g. IL-10, TNF⍺, MBL, 

![](Systemic%20Lupus%20Erythematosus/image%204.png)

Equally important are epigenetic changes. Of note, drugs such as procainamide and hydralazine can cause histone hypomethylation, leading to drug-induced lupus. 

#### Environmental Factors
Environmental factors implicated in the development of lupus include:
- UV light
- Infection (esp. EBV)
- Smoking
- Environmental pollutants (esp. silica)
- Being exposed to other species with lupus

Drug-induced lupus can be triggered by several drugs, and typically results in the formation of anti-dsDNA and anti-histone antibodies. The syndrome tends to be less severe. Offending drugs include;
- ***Procainamide***
- ***Hydralazine*** (HLA-DR4)
- Minocycline
- Diltiazem
- Penicilliamine
- Tetracyclines - exercise caution with prescribing in patients with known lupus
- Isoniazid
- Quinide
- IFN-⍺
- Methyldopa
- Chlorpromazine

TNF inhibitors (e.g. infliximab) can trigger the formation of autoantibodies, but clinical lupus is rare.

#### Hormonal Factors
- Increased oestrogen
- Decreased DHEA/androgen
- Hyperprolactinaemia

### Clinical Features and Diagnosis
Lupus can present with a multitude of symptoms, ranging from mild to life-threatening.

Clinical diagnosis is made using the EULAR criteria, which gives an idea of what some of those symptoms are:
![](Systemic%20Lupus%20Erythematosus/AGV_vUchwHivKGGxQAoF2nFurT8aTnT-9T_SicEesC-t6ZLP0K1b8lkZIxVMl9wXfFTE0V9et2h_-QnfYuiqFzVQ-IUU7lzwplqrcuVCkti5NY0_U3W9SFWxQvNmtpwpZtKK84C-tmS4Ug=s2048.png)
Once diagnosed, we can broadly group lupus into one of four categories:
1. Discoid lupus
2. Subacute cutaneous lupus
3. Systemic lupus with a low risk of serious harm
4. Systemic lupus with risk of serious harm:
   - Vasculitis
   - Neurological or neuropsychiatric symptoms
   - Headache
   - Endocarditis
   - Lupus nephritis
   - Pregnancy-associated complications
   - Haemolytic anaemia 
#### Dermatologic Manifestations
Acute:
Photosensitive, malar rash sparing the nasolabial folds with associated genearlised erythema.
![](Systemic%20Lupus%20Erythematosus/image.png)

Subacute:
Photosensitive papulosquamous rash. May be associated systemic features, particularly:
- Arthritis
- Tenoxynovitis
- Pleurisy
- Oral ulceration
![](Systemic%20Lupus%20Erythematosus/image%202.png)

Chronic:
Discoid lupus - generally characterised by:
- Majority of lesions being above the neck
- Scarring
- Depigmentation
- Alopecia
- Mucosal lesions
Can be limited, but ~1/4 of patients will progress to systemic lupus. 
![](Systemic%20Lupus%20Erythematosus/image%203.png)

Other, nonspecific signs tend to be nail dystrophy, hair loss, and painless oral ulcers.

#### Renal
Renal disease, [[lupus nephritis]], is possibly the most significant manifestation of lupus. The pathological changes typically progress from minimal mesangial changes, to mesangial proliferation, through to membranous glomerulonephropathy and diffuse sclerosis. This is accompanied by a deterioration in renal function clinically ranging from minimal proteinuria, to full-blown nephrotic syndrome, often with additional haematuria.

Electron microscopy typically shows subendothelial immune complexes and tubuloreticular inclusions.

Biopsy-proven lupus nephritis makes the diagnosis of SLE. 

#### Musculoskeletal
Lupus is characterised by non-erosive _arthralgias_ rather than true arthritis (_Jaccoud arthropathy_). Unlike the deformities sustained in other autoimmune joint disesase, these tend to be reducible (although some unfortunate individuals can get an RA/SLE overlap syndrome with positive ACPA). 

#### Cardiovascular 
Pericarditis is the most comon manifestation of SLE in the heart, typically large enough to cause tamponade. The valves can be affected, sometimes with verrucous sterile lesions (_Libman-Sacks endocarditis_). 

Importantly, like in rheumatoid arthritis, the risk of coronary artery disease is significantly increased, and MACE tend to occur at an earlier age.

Vasculitis frequently presents with a rash, particularly of the hands, and is often a harbinger of impending deterioration. 
![](Systemic%20Lupus%20Erythematosus/image%206.png)

#### Pulmonary 
Commonly:
- Pleurisy
- Small pleural effusions
- ILD - typically an NSIP pattern but rarely severe
- Lupus pneumonitis

Less commonly:
- Pulmonary hypertension
- Shrinking lung syndrome - possibly due to diaphragmatic myopathy
- Alveolar haemorrhage 

#### Gastrointestinal 
Although SLE can cause deranged LFTs, actual clinical liver disease is typically rare, as are pancreatitis, enteritis (typically regional) and hepatitis. There may be an increased risk of perforation in active vasculitis.

#### Haematological
Anaemia is very common in lupus patients, and autoimmune haemolytic anaemia is an important cause. ITP can also occur, sometimes preceding SLE. ITP along with AIHA is termed _Evan syndrome_. TTP is also possible.

There is a strong association with the [[antiphospholipid syndrome]]. 

#### Neuropsychiatric
The neurological, cognitive, and psychiatric manifestations of SLE are varied, although cognitive dysfunction (so-called ‘lupus fog’) and headache are the most common, affecting almost 80% (~70% for headache) of patients. Almost any psychiatric or neurological syndrome can be caused by lupus. It is most important to exclude other causes with appropriate investigations.

Neuropsychiatric testing with a neuropsychologist is the most sensitive test for CNS involvement. Among imaging studies, the PET is typically the most useful scan for neurological involvement in lupus - MR less so. 

There are two clinically distinct subtypes of long-segment myelitis associated with lupus:
- Grey matter - hyporeflexic picture with a prodrome of fever andn urinary retention
- White matter - hyperreflexic picture associated with positive AQP4 and APL antibodies 

#### Autoantibodies
The autoantibodies of SLE tend to precede clinical disease by a mean of almost four years. 

An antinuclear antibody (ANA) is almost always positive in lupus, but can also be elevated in a range of autoimmune, rheumatic, and infective conditions, and even with increasing age. So, ANA is a sensitive but not specific test, unless the titres are _extremely_ high:
> **An ANA titre >1:640 is considered significant**
The ANA can be associated with a range of staining patterns:
- Dense/homogeneous - may indicate a low probability of autoimmune disease; may also indicate anti-histone antibodies in drug induced lupus
- Speckled - tends to indicate antibodies against the non-DNA nuclear components (i.e. most of the ENAs: Sm, Ro, La, etc.)
- Centromeric - tends to indicate anti-centromere antibodies in [[systemic sclerosis]]
- Nucleolar - tends to indicate anti-RNA antibodies in SSc
![](Systemic%20Lupus%20Erythematosus/AGV_vUeVUzgr7i0dPPGmGy0lRCGY1X54-634tWecqB4cpObumSzmMw4r2dL_5LZAsOVCZvrv9c8vJOV74fxM0axecCAMdPU16L-90OEb1MwxetXPjcsPNSb28IjYiuA6TBk1mx7YJc7aFQ=s2048.png)

The most specific antibody for SLE is the antibody against the Smith antigen (~98%), an ENA, followed closely by anti-ds-DNA (the latter being slightly less specific (~97%) as it can be induced by TNF inhibitors without clinical lupus). 

The antibodies against the various ENAs have specific associations:
- Ro/SSa -> neonatal lupus and congenital heart block, lymphopenia, photosensitivity, C2 deficiency, subacute cutaneous lupus
- La/SSb -> Sjögren syndrome, neonatal lupus
- Smith/Sm -> _highly specific_, renal and CNS disease
- U1RNP/RNP -> myositis, Raynaud phenomenon (note secondary RP associated with abnormal nailbed capillaries)
- Ribosomal p -> psychosis, depression
- Neuronal -> organic brain syndrome

Conversely, the _anti-dense fine speckled-70_ antibody (anti-DFS70) has a strongly ***negative*** association with autoimmune disease, and can be used as a rule-out test (positive in <1% of patients with an autoimmune condition). 

#### Other diagnostic markers
ESR > CRP
- ESR more elevated due to presence of immunoglobulin
- Elevated CRP can be suggestive of infection rather than disease flare
Low C3/C4
Complement functional assays:
- CH50 tends to be reduced

### Management
Treat to target a SLEDAI score of zero (remission) or <4 (low disease activity). 

#### Risk Factor Modification 
Trigger avoidance:
- Stress
- UV light
- Oestrogen
- Sulfonamides 

Steroids can be used at the lowest possible dose for the shortest possible duration to treat disease flares. 

#### HCQ
The antimalarial hydroxychloroquine has efficacy as an immunomodulator and should be background therapy in _all_ patients with SLE. It has been found to have efficacy in terms of reducing flares, increasing survival, and improving the response to MMF. There are additional cardioprotective benefits. The main risk is of retinotoxicity, for which annual funduscopy after 5y of use (when risk increases) is recommended. 

Dose: generally up to 4-5mg/kg

Also - MTX. CYC and AZA are typically reserved for patients with [[lupus nephritis]]. 

#### RTX
The anti-CD20, B-cell depleting agent rituximab has mixed evidence in, but is often used to treat lupus due to efficacy evident from registry data. The negative _EXPLORER_ and _LUNAR_ trials both found that rituximab had non-statistically significant benefits in SLE.

#### BEL
Belimumab is a human monoclonal antibody that binds to the soluble stimulatory factors BAFF and BLYS, preventing them from regulating B-cell differentiation and survival. _BLISS 52_ and _BLISS 76_ found that in SLE seropositive patients with a high SLEDAI score, BEL was associated with reduced serological activity, reduced disease activity, and improved quality of life. Belimumab is not indicated for CNS or renal disease. 

#### ANI
Anifrolumab, an antibody against the IFNα receptor, was found by the _TULIP-2_ study to have significantly fewer flares and significantly higher BICLA score when compared to placebo. There is an icnreased risk of infections and reactivation of latent pathogens. This is likely related to the fact that type I interferons are a key pathogenic cytokine in SLE, with increased levels in the serum of patients, and exogenous administration of IFN being known to induce SLE-like symptoms. 

#### Other treatments:
- JAK inhibition -> e.g. baricitinib
- Cytokine inhibition -> secukinumab (IL17), ustekinumab (IL12/23)
- E3 ligase inhibition -> lenalidomide
- Dendritic cell inhibition -> litifilimab

#### CAR-T Cells
Although evidence for CAR-T therapy is only emerging in SLE, it seems to be highly effective with one small study showing a 100% drug-free remission rate in seven patients.

### Lupus and Pregnancy
Lupus is associated with a massively increased rate of maternal mortality and obstetric complications (e.g. pre-term delivery, pre-eclampsia, PPH, etc.). Predictors of poor outcome include active disease, use of hormone therapy, prior lupus nephiritis, seropositivity for APL ABs, thrombocytopenia, and primigravid status. 

Contraindications to pregnancy include:
- Severe pHTN
- Severe restrictive lung disease
- ESRF
- Advanced heart failure
- Previous severe pre-eclampsia or HELLP syndrome
- Recent stroke
- Recent flare 

Womnen who are APL AB+ should receive:
- Asymptomatic -> low dose aspirin
- Previous obstetric morbidity -> ASA + LMWH 40mg
- Prior thrombosis -> therapeutic LMWH

Most neonatal lupus symptoms resolve within 6-8/12 when the maternal antibodies are cleared. Anti-Ro (and less commonly anti-La) antibodies are associated with complete heart block in the neonate, with the highest risk being in late T2/early T3 (18-24/40). Fluorinated steroids can be used to prevent progression. 

Medications in pregancy:
- Contraindicated -> MTX, LEF, MMF, CYC, VKA, ACEi/A2RB
- Continue unless contraindicated -> HCQ
- Potentially safe:
  - NSAIDs -> cease in T3 due to risk of premature PDA closure
  - Steroids -> use as low dose as possible, and non-fluorinated (e.g. prednisolone) to minimise placental passage
    - Signal of increased cleft palate with T1 use
  - AZA at <2mg/kg/d
  - TAC - limited evidence 
- - -
### Prognosis
Poor prognostic factors include:
- Onset at extremes of age: <30y or >50y
- Haemolytic anaemia
- Male sex
- Renal lupus within 1y of presentation (SMR = 7.8)
- Neuropsychiatric lupus (SMR = 2-4)
