### The History of Dialysis
Key figures:
- Thomas Graham - described diffusion across semipermeable membranes 
- John Abel et al. - practiced diffusion on animals 
- Georg Ganter and George Haas - first description and practice of dialysis in humans in 1923-4
- Wilhelm Kolff - successful haemodialysis in humans in 1945
- Morton Maxwell - modern era of peritoneal dialysis

### What is dialysis?
Dialysis is a common treatment that fundamentally revolves around the diffusion of solutes across semipremeable membranes. Although the incidence of dialysis is increasing every year in Australia and New Zealand, the demographic cohort remains largely the same, with people in their 40s and 50s being the most common. Economically, dialysis utilises ~1.2% of the annual health expenditure, typically in the order of ~A$40-100,000 per patient per year. 

The most common indications for the initiation of dialysis are:
- Diabetes - 40%
- Glomerular disease - 18%
- Hypertension - 11%
- Tubulointerstitial dosasse - 8%
- Hereditary disease - 7%

Dialysis utilises the principles of diffusion and convection to _passively_ achieve two things:
1. Removal of toxins
2. Removal of fluid 

| Diffusion                                                    | Convection                                                   | Absorption                                                   |
|--------------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------|
| The movement of solutes across a semipermeable membrane down a concentration gradient.  | The ‘drag’ of solutes with fluid across a semipermeable membrane.  | Movement of solutes and fluid from the peritoneal cavity into the circulation via the lymphatic system.  |
| The rate of diffusion relates to the concentration difference between the two sides of the membrane, and the surface area of the membrane, as well as the intrinsic resistance of the membrane and the molecular weight of the solute.  | Plays a smaller role than diffusion in dialysis, but is important for larger molecules.  | Unique to peritoneal dialysis.                               |

### Indications for Dialysis
Dialysis should ideally be initiated for _symptoms_ and not numbers.

#### Acute
‘AEIOU’
- Acidosis - metabolic, refractory
- Electrolytes - refractory hyperkalaemia
- Iatrogenic/toxins 
- Overload - refractory fluid overload
- Uraemic complications - e.g. encephalitis, pericarditis

#### Chronic
As for acute dialysis, plus:
- Loss of weight 
- Difficulty concentrating, weakness, etc.

In Australia, dialysis is typically considered or started when the eGFR reaches the range of 5-8mL/min/1.73sqm.

### Haemodialysis 
![](Renal%20Replacement%20Therapy/image.png)
The general schema of haemodialysis involves blood being taken from the patient via the arterial line, running past the dialysate in a counter current to allow for diffusion of solutes, then being returned to the patient via the venous line. 

#### Access for Dialysis
Access for haemodialysis can either be permanent or temporary, depending on the context of initiation of dialysis and plans for ongoing treatment:
- Temporary:
  - Non-tunnelled catheter (‘Vascath’)
  - Tunnelled catheter (‘Permacath”)
- Permanent:
  - Arteriovenous fistula
  - Ateriovenous graft
  - Tunnelled catheter 

The arteriovenous fistula is by far the preferred option for haemodialysis, with the best long-term patency rates of all options.
 ![](Renal%20Replacement%20Therapy/image%202.png)<!-- {"width":174} -->  ![](Renal%20Replacement%20Therapy/image%203.png)<!-- {"width":396} -->
An optimal AVF will:
- Have good blood flow
- Be easy to cannulate
- Be in the nondominant hand
- Not restrict blood supply

An AVF needs to be created in a surgical procedure, and often requires up to three months to mature. As such, it cannot be used for acute or urgent dialysis. AVFs can also fail after some time, requiring revision or re-do. Various options for AVFs include radial-cephalic (preferred), brachial-cephalic, and brachial-basilic. Complications of AVFs include distally interrupted blood supply and venous aneurysm formation.

In contrast, central catheters can be inserted quickly and allow for urgent dialysis. Common complications include CLABSIs, exit-site infections, malpositioning, central venous stenosis, and fibrin clot formation.

The ‘last resort’ when a patient’s native vasculature is unsuitable for an AVF is an arteriovenous graft, which involves prosthetic tissue being used to link the artery and vein. In addition to the possible complications of an AVF, AVGs are also prone to infection and failure. The lifespan is typically limited to a few years.

#### Prescribing Haemodialysis 
Key considerations:
- Frequency - often three times a week for logistical reasons, but as frequent as possible is best
- Duration - typically 4-5 hours per session
- Fluid removal (the ‘ultrafiltration rate’)
- Anticoagulation
- Blood flow rate
- Dialysate flow rate
- Dialyser 
- Dialysate composition and temperature 

#### Complications of Haemodialysis
- Intradialytic hypotension
  - ![](Renal%20Replacement%20Therapy/image%204.png)
- Intradialytic cramps
- Dialysis disequilibrium syndrome
  - Often occurs in new patients or patients who have missed dialysis for a period of time
  - Secondary to cerebral oedema from fluid shifts in the setting of changes in osmolality 
  - Start slowly!
- Calciphylaxis -> sodium thiosulfate 
  
Hypertension is a common finding in patients on dialysis, particularly at initiation. Volume expansion is the major cause of hypertension in patients on dialysis, although sympathetic overactivity, activation of the renin-angiotensin system, and arteriosclerosis also contribute.

#### Causes of Death
The number one cause of death for patients on dialysis is cardiovascular death, although in the elderly cohort withdrawal of dialysis is a close second. Another important cause in all age groups is infection.

### Peritoneal Dialysis 
Peritoneal dialysis revolves around using the patient’s own peritoneum as the semipermeable membrane across which dialysis occurs. The dialysate is slowly infused into the patient’s peritoneal cavity, allowing dialysis, and then drained off. The two main modalities of PD are continuous ambulatory PD and automated PD. The characteristics of the patient’s peritoneum will also factor into prescribing PD, and can be quantified through the peritoneal equilibration test. 

Compared to haemodialysis, peritoneal dialysis is:
- As effective, with potentially better mortality in the first few years
- More flexible, as it is a home based therapy
- Able to preserve residual renal function for longer
- Typically only able to be carried out for 3-5y 

The Tenckhoff catheter, a flexible catheter used for PD, needs to enter into the abdominal wall, and then descend below the pelvic brim. The Tenckhoff catheter typically has two Dacron cuffs, unlike the catheters used in HD. 

The choice of fluid used for PD varies between a few physiologic solutions containing Na+, Ca2+, Cl-, and Mg2+, with lactate acting as a buffer, and glucose acting as the main osmotic agent. The alternative to glucose is icodextrin, a poorly absorbed carbohydrate polymer. 

The main complications of PD include peritonitis, exit site infections, and tunnel infections, as well as mechanical complications associated with the line. Catheter malfunctions are strongly associated with constipation, but other causes include omental wrapping (typically early), catheter kinking (typically early), and fibrin clot occlusion (can happen at any time):
![](Renal%20Replacement%20Therapy/image%205.png)

PD is typically unable to be carried out in patients who do not want to do it (typically, most patients prefer PD > HD), if the patient has no home, or if the patient has insufficient peritoneal membrane.

### Dialysis Adequacy
The goal of dialysis is the removal of small solutes, and this is how we benchmark dialysis. Typically this is done by collecting all effluent (dialysate and urine) over a 24h period from a patient, and measuring the amount of urea to calculate:
- Creatinine clearance and fluid removal (peritoneal dialysis)
  - The generally accepted target for weekly creatinine clearance in PD is at least 60 L/week/1.73m²
- Body-indexed water clearance-time product (Kt/V)
- Urea reduction ratio (haemodialysis)

It is also important to consider the patient’s psychosocial setting and long term complications of renal disease and dialysis when considering adequacy. 

Kt/V is a dimensionless number that quantifies the efficacy of dialysis by comparing the amount of urea removed to the total body water.
- K stands for the dialyzer clearance of urea (rate at which urea is removed).
- t represents the time over which dialysis occurs.
- V is the volume of distribution of urea, which approximates the patient’s total body water.

The formula for weekly Kt/V in PD can be simplified as:
> Weekly Kt/V = Total urea removed per week/Total body water

Target Values: The recommended target for adequate peritoneal dialysis is a weekly Kt/V of at least 1.7.

### Mortality in Dialysis
Mortality in dialysis patients remains very high, far exceeding the general population. The most common cause of death in patients on dialysis is cardiovascular disease, with infections also being a common cause.

### Approach to the Dialysis Long Case
Need to maintain a systematic approach:
1. Identify the cause of the patient’s ESRF:
   - What has the time course of the disease been?
   - What are the potential implications for transplantation?
2. Identify the kidney replacement therapy (KRT) history:
   - What modalities has the patient been on?
   - How long were they on them for?
   - Why change modalities?
3. Review the progress of the current KRT modality:
   - What is the current modality?
   - What is the current prescription?
   - What is their ideal body weight/dry weight?
   - How much fluid is removed per dialysis?
   - Assess adherence - how well are their potassium, fluid, and phosphate controlled?
     - **This is important for transplantation**
   - Have there been any complications?
4. Review their medications, particularly those significant to dialysis:
   - Phosphate binders 
   - Erythropoietin
   - Immunosuppression
5. Review their suitability for transplantation:
   - Living vs cadaveric donor?
6. Review their psychosocial situation:
   - Finances
   - Supports
   - Mental state
   - Insight
