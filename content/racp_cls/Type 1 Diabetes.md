# Type 1 Diabetes
#bpt/endocrinology #bpt/racp_cls

### Overview
Type 1 diabetes is caused by an autoimmune destruction of the β-cells leading to absolute insulin deficiency. 
- - -
### Epidemiology
Account for ~5-10% of diabetes. Increasing in incidence and prevalence. Although onset is _most common_ in the young, this is not always the case (i.e. latent autoimmune diabetes of adulthood, which is defined as onset after the age of 30y). Life expectancy is decreased by around 13y. 

Although there is clearly a genetic component, with a strong association with the HLA-DR3/DR4-DQ8 haplotypes, around 90% of cases are in someone with no family history. 

Most prevalent in Finland (interestingly three times as many in neighbouring Estonia). Being born in the springtime increases risk.

There is a frequent association with other [[autoimmune endocrine disease]], and screening is recommended:
- Autoimmune thyroid disease - as many as 1/5 have antibodies, and 5% develop clinical hypothyroidism
- Addison disease - <1% of patients with T1DM 
- Coeliac disease
- Vitiligo
- Autoimmune hepatitis
- Myasthenia gravis
- Autoimmune gastritis

Frequently, this is in the context of the _[[Defects of Immune Dysregulation/Autoimmune Polyendocrinopathy/Candidiasis/Ectodermal Dysplasia Syndrome|autoimmune polyendocrine syndrome type 2]]_, which is a polygenic condition associated with Addison disease, T1DM, and autoimmune thyroiditis with risk being conferred by HLA-DQ2/DQ8/DRB1.

Note that the lifetime risk of developing diabetes in a child born to a type 1 diabetic mother at age 16y is approximately 5%.

### Diagnosis
T1DM is often diagnosed in the setting of [[diabetic ketoacidosis]], where patients present with a triad of:
- Acute onset symptoms
- Random plasma BSL >11.1mmol/L
- Ketoacidosis

Unlike T2DM, HbA1c is not typically used to _diagnose_ T1DM, although it is used to monitor progression. A low or undetectable plasma C-peptide level supports the diagnosis.

Although diabetes is a cell-mediated autoimmune disorder, the presence of diabetes autoantibodies also suggests T1DM:
- Anti-GAD65 (=glutamic acid decarboxylase) - the one and the same implicated in Stiff Person Syndrome
- Anti-IAA (=insulin) - most specific for β-cells
- Anti-IA2/B (=tyrosine phosphatase)
- Anti-Zn transporter 8
- Anti-tetraspanin 7

### Pathophysiology
Cell-mediated autoimmune destruction of pancreatic β cells results in an absolute insulin deficiency. This is probably related to a combination of:
- Autoimmune factors
- Environmental factors - cow’s milk sensitivity, breastfeeding (protective), hygiene hypothesis
- Genetic factors, particularly the human leucocycte antigen complex on chromosome 6:
  - Increased risk: HLA-DR3, HLA-DR4
  - Reduced risk = HLA-DR2

This is compounded by a dysregulation of the normal Treg system, that should keep a check on autoimmunity. 

On histology, the main characteristics are:
- CD8+ infiltration of the pancreas
- Pseudoatrophic appearance of the pancreas
- Absence of insulin in ~70% of islets (at diagnosis)

A minority of patients have no evidence of B-cell autoimmunity, and are said to have _type 1b diabetes_. This is inherited albeit without HLA-association. 
- - -
### Fulminant T1DM
A form of acute-onset T1DM that is rare in the West, but comprises ~20% of acute-onset T1DM in Japan. Characterised by:
- Abrupt onset
- Flu-like and GI symptoms before onset
- Ketoacidosis at diagnosis
- Virtually no C-peptide secretion
- Generally negative for islet autoantibodies
- Increased serum levels of panreatic enzymes
- - -
### Latent Autoimmune Diabetes in Adults
More properly, slowly evolving immune-mediated diabetes of adults. Clinical presentation similar to T2DM, but in young adults with positive autoantibodies (usually anti-GAD). Insulin is often not required at diagnosis, but time-to-insulin requirement is usually shorter than in adults with typical T2DM. Usual criteria for diagnosis include:
- Age >35y at diagnosis
- Presence of GAD autoantibodies
- Lack of insulin treatment in the first 6-12m after diagnosis

### Prevention
Recall that bovine serum albumin in cow’s milk _may_ increase the risk of T1DM. [A Finnish study](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1004809) found that weaning babies onto hydrolysed casein rather than whole cow’s milk formula was associated with lower rates of subsequent development of β-cell antibodies, but did not point at prevention of T1DM itself.

In patients with newly diagnosed T1DM, or detected asymptomatic dysglycaemia, there are also interventions that aim to slow the progression of the disease to overt clinical diabetes:
- Immunotherapy in at risk individuals - _**teplizumab**_ (anti-CD3 monoclonal antibody). Shown to delay progression of T1DM from asymptomatic dysglycaemia to overt symptomatic hyperglycaemia in adults and children aged ≥8y. Oral insulin may have a similar effect.
- Verapamil - seems to reduce β-cell apoptosis and preserve production of insulin/C-peptide, based on studies of adolescents with newly diagnsoed T1DM. Baricitinib may have a similar effect.

### Complications
The relationship betwen HbA1c and complications of diabetes were best characterised by the _[DCCT trial](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJM199309303291401)_, which randomised patients to intensive vs. conventional control. 

Intensive control (aiming HbA1c <7.0%) was associated with significant reductions in the rates of the microvascular complications:
- Retinopathy
- Nephropathy
- Neuropathy

Building on this, the _[EDIC study](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/22/1/99/19691/Epidemiology-of-Diabetes-Interventions-and)_ found that a period of intensive treatment _continued_ to have an effect on retinopathy, nephropathy, and the macrovascular complications of stroke and MI. 
- - -
### Glycaemic Target
Based on the DCCT study ==🟢a target of HbA1c <7% (53mmol/mol)== appears to be optimal for minimising the risk of diabetes complications. This should be accompanied by:
- Pre-prandial sugars: 4.4-7.2mmol/L
- Post-prandial sugars: <10mmol/L
- CGM time in range (4-10mmol/L): >70%
- CGM time below range (<4mmol/L): <5%
- Variability: <36%

### Management
Insulin analogues form the cornerstone of T1DM management, and should be administered either as:
- Multiple daily subcutaneous injections in a basal/bolus format
- Continuous subcutaneous insulin infusion:
  - Less hypoglycaemia
  - Less weight gain
  - Lower HbA1c

Compared to regular human insulin, insulin analogues:
- Do not aggregate as hexamers (i.e. rapid acting insulin is able to absorbed rapidly), meaning they:
  - Control postprandial hyperglycaemia better
  - Reduce late and nocturnal hyoglycaemia 
- Reduce HbA1c (small effect size)
- Do not seem to reduce complications 

On the other hand, long-acting insulins are engineered to form _multihexamers_, enabling them to be released slowly into the circulation.

Of note, there are some differences among the three longest-acting insulins:
- Degludec - half-life of 25 hours (x2 glargine) -> forms long hexameric chains after injection to promote slow release
- U-300 glargine - more stable formulation with more predictable PK/PD that is associated with a lower hypoglycaemic risk than U-100 glargine 

Dosing typically begins at 0.5iu/kg/d, with half being given as a bolus and have as a basal dose. Patients should be empowered to dose their own insulin, and taught to carbohydrate count:
- Insulin carbohydrate ratio -> how many grams of carbohydrates are covered by 1 unit of (rapid-acting) insulin
  - The ICR is roughly calculated by dividing 500 by the total daily dose of insulin 
  - This is used to calculate how much rapid-acting insulin is needed to ‘maintain’ the current BSL 
- Insulin sensitivity factor -> how much 1 unit of rapid-acting insulin will lower blood glucose over 2-4 hours to correct for hyperglycaemia
  - The ISF is roughly calculated by dividing 100 by the total daily fose of insulin 
  - This is used to calculate how much insulin is required to ‘reduce’ the current BSL to target (e.g. 8mmol/L) 

e.g. if a patient’s pre-prandial BSL is 14mmol/L with an ISF of 3mmol/L and they are eating a meal with 20g of carbs with an ICR of 5, an appropriate dose of insulin might be 6iu.

Correct injection technique is important to optimise glycaemic control and to ensure insulin is used safely. Important to rotate sites to avoid lipohypertrophy and lipoatrophy. 

Insulin can also be administered via a continuous subcutaneous infusion whic his designed to mimic the normal phsyiological secretion of insulin in a basal/bolus fashion:
- Pros: mimics physiology, fewer injections, less variable absorption, better control and less hypoglycaemia, patient satisfaction
  - Help to overcome the ‘dawn phenomenon’ - the rise in BSL in the morning due to cortisol/GH spike
  - Help to attenuate the Somogyi effect - morning hyperglycaemia due to nocturnal hypoglycaemia (typically due to excessive nocturnal insulin)
- Cons: cost, SSTI, pump failure/needle dislodgement -> DKA, inconvenience 
Can be augmented with a continuous glucose monitor to precisely deliver insulin (so-called [_closed loop_ pumping](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1907863)) , guarding against hyper and hypoglycaemia. This is associated with a roughly 10% increase in time in range, predominantly due to tighter control of nighttime BSLs. 

#### Non-Insulin Treatments in T1DM
- SGLT2 inhibitors - associated with reductions in HbA1c, body weight, insulin requirements and SBP; **but** dose-related risk of ketoacidosis which requires close monitoring.
- GLP-1 agonists - associated with reductions in HbA1c, body weight, and prandial insulin requirements.
- Metformin - small reductions in weight and lipid levels, but no effect on HbA1c.
- Pramlintide (β-cell peptide amylin analogue) - small reduction in HbA1c and body weight. 
- Whole pancreas transplantation - curative treatment, usually simultaneously with a renal transplant. Graft half-life up to 14y in SPKT. Requires lifelong immunosuppression.
- Islet transplantation - generally indicated if severe hypoglycaemia or hypoglycaemic unawareness despite intensive treatment. Good rates of improved glycaemic control and in some cases insulin independence. Requires immunosuppression.
- [Stem cell](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2506549) and gene therapy - using induced pluripotent stem cells which are then commenced on a differentiation protocol to form insulin-producing cells that are glucose responsive
- [CRISPR](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMe2507578)

### Assessing Glycaemic Control
HbA1c:
- General target ≤7.0%
- Pregnant/trying ≤7.0%
- Recurrent severe hypoglycaemia ≤8.0%

The concept of “_metabolic memory_” is important here, as the _EDIC_ study found that a finite period of intensive control yields benefits that extent for potentially decades after that control ends. 

Self-monitoring of blood glucose is a critical part of effective therapy, as it allows the patient to both guide treatment and monitor for hypoglycaemia. At least, the individual should check their BSLs before food and prior to bed time, and often at other times of the day such as important tasks (e.g. driving) or if there is suspicion of hypoglyceamia. 

Continuous glucose monitoring tests interstitial glucose levels at 1-5min intervals, which map well to plasma glucose levels albeit with a lag of ~10 mins. Can either report data in real time, or intermittently. Trial evidence that CGM:
- Reduces time in hypoglycaemia without compromising HbA1c
- Improves time in target range
- Improves HbA1c 

General targets for T1 and T2DM:
- ≥70% time in range
- <5% hypoglycaemic time
- <25% time above range 
- Narrow control

That said, this is only really useful if there is ≥90% data capture.
