# Delirium
#bpt/geriatrics #bpt/racp_cls
- - -
### Overview
Delirium is the clinical manifestation of encephalopathy, and is characterised by:
- Disturbances in attention and awareness
- Acute onset
- Tendency to fluctuate in severity
- Additional disturbances in cognition

> [!TIP] A fun mnemonic is ‘AFRAID DASH’:
> Acute onset
> Fluctuation
> Rambling thought and speech
> Arousal disturbances
> Inattention
> Disorientation
> Delusions
> Agitation/apathy
> Sleep-wake cycle disturbance
> Hallucinations often visual

Delirium can come in three subtypes:
- Hyperactive = agitated, wandering, aggressive
- Hypoactive = drowsy, withdrawn
- Mixed

Irrespective of the phenotype, delirium is associated with poorer outcomes, accelerated cognitive decline, increased length of stay, increased rate of admission to RACF, and increased mortality.

### Epidemiology
_Very_ common.
- - -
### Risk Factors
The chief risk factor for delirium is aging and neurodegeneration. Neurodegeneration results in microglia and astrocytes that induce a pro-inflammatory response when challenged, and consequently this can cause impaired cerebral perfusion, endothelial dysfunction in the blood-brain barrier, and impaired function of the microcirculation.

On a chemical basis, it appears that acetylcholine is the major neurotransmitter, as anticholinergic drugs cause delirium-like symptoms _and_ EEG changes. Histamine also appears to play an important role in arousal.

Important situational risk factors include:
- Severe illness
- Polypharmacy (RR up to 4.4)
- Infection (RR up to 3.1)
- Electrolyte and acid-base disturbance (RR up to 3.4)
- Restraint use (RR up to 4.4)
- IDC (RR up to 2.4)
- Heart failure
- Hypoxia or hypercapnia
- Change of environment
- Pain
- Constipation
- Urinary Retention
- Organ failure
- Hypoglyacemia
- Stroke
- Immobilisation
- Alcohol withdrawal
- Benzodiazepine withdrawal

### Diagnosis
Can utilise tools suchas :
- 4AT
- CAM
- etc. 

Investigations are typically adjunctive to a clinical diagnosis, and encompasses the suite of causative factors for delirium. Although highly sensitive and specific, an EEG is not usually required. If performed, the EEG may show:
- Occipital slowing
- Excess delta waves
- Θ waves

### Management
An ounce of prevention is worth a pound of treatment. 
The usual things - attend to unmet need, reverse possible causes, reassurance, reorientation, etc. 
In terms of medications, it is worth considering:
- Reducing:
  - Overall number of medications
  - Anticholinergics
  - Tricyclic antidepressants
  - Antihistamines
  - Benzodiazepines
  - Tramadol
- Use judiciously:
  - Opioids
- Avoid where possible:
  - Antipsychotics - don’t prevent deliriuma and do not decrease the severity of delirium either 
  - Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors - don’t prevent delirium
- Consider:
  - Melatonin
  - Suvorexant 

If patients have distress that cannot be relieved, particularly with psychoatic features, medications that can be considered include:
- Haloperidol - 0.25-0.5mg PO/IM
- Risperidone - 0.25-0.5mg PO
- Olanzapine 1.25-2.5mg PO/IM
Quetiapine can be effective in those with Parkinsonism.
