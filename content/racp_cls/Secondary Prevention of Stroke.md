### Carotid Endarterectomy
Simply put, this procedure involves the removal of plaque from within an offending artery. There is the best evidence for this procedure _within two weeks_ of an offending event:
- [[Ischaemic stroke]]
- [[Transient ischaemic attack]]
- Retinal ischaemia - i.e. amaurosis fugax

**And** if there is carotid stenosis between 70-99% that we are confident is the cause of the patient’s presentation. 
- There is modest benefit for blockages between 50-69%, but these also offer room for medical optimisation
- There is _no benefit_ if the patient is asymptomatic from the carotid disease 

**Summary**:
- Within two weeks
- Stroke/TIA/amaurosis fugax
- Stenosis 70-99%

### Anticoagulation
This is relevant to patients whose strokes arise from cardioembolic phenomena in the setting of atrial fibrillation. Today, DOACs are the mainstay of anticoagulant therapy, with some exceptions if the patient has a mechanical heart valve or renal failure (CrCl <25mL/min). 

It is important to consider if anticoagulants can be reversed - warfarin is easy, and dabigatran is now possible with idarucizimab, but there are no reversal agents for the direct factor Xa inhibitors (there _is_ an agent - andexanet - but it is not approved in Australia).

### [[Atrial Fibrillation]]
Standard of care for patients with stroke is a 24h Holter monitor to look for atrial fibrillation. Increasingly, however, devices like implantable loop recorders or Heart Bugs are being used to investigate paroxysmal AF in more and more cases (up to 30%).

### Antiplatelet Therapy 
There is strong empirical evidence (the _CHANCE_ trial) for _dual antiplatelet therapy_ after stroke or high-risk TIA. The primary benefit of DAPT is in the first 21-30 days, with DAPT after 30 days carrying an additional bleeding risk compared to aspirin alone. The typical choice of second agent is _clopidogrel_ (a P2Y12-R antagonist), although the recent _THALES_ study suggests that ticagrelor is a viable alternative, although with its own high bleeding risk.
- - -
### Blood Pressure Lowering
All stroke and TIA patients with BP ≥140/90mmHg should have long term blood pressure lowering. The most well-known trial in this setting is _PROGRESS_, which found a reduction in the number of of people with recurrent stroke using perindopril, with an NNT of only 25. It appears that ACEi/ARB/CCB/thiazides all work well, but that β-blockade should be avoided unless a patient has concurrent ischaemic heart disease. 
- - -
### Lipid Lowering
High dose statin therapy is established as standard post-stroke by the _SPARCL_ trial. Most patients should get atorvastatin 80mg to a target of LDL-c <1.8mmol/L, with care being taken for elderly and/or frail patients. Statins are _not_ recommended post-haemorrhagic stroke as there is a small increase in the risk of bleeding. 
- - -
### PFO Closure
In patients <60y with embolic stroke with no other clear aetiology, percutaneous PFO closure reduces the risk of stroke recurrence (_RESPECT_, _GORE-REDUCE_, _CLOSE_). Diagnosis should ideally be confirmed beforehand with a TTE with saline bubble study + Valsalva.

### Lifestyle
The usual suspects - smoking, exercise, diet, alcohol, sedentariness, adherence to pharmacotherapy, etc. 
- - -
### Stroke Risk in Diabetes
The GLP-1 agonists have superior evidence (LEADER, SUSTAIN, SELECT) for reducing MACE in patients with diabetes.
