### Overview 
Achalasia refers to a heterogeneous form of [[dysphagia]] that is characterised by loss of the intrinsic innervation of the oesophagus, leading to abnormal peristalsis and incomplete relaxation of the lower oesophageal sphincter:
![](Achalasia/image.png) The exact cause remains unknown. There is an association with an increased risk of SCC. Weight loss is a late sign.

Achalasia is common worldwide in adults, and has no predominance in age or sex. The regurgitated food is non-acidic. Oesophageal manometry is the most sensitive test. The ‘classic’ diagnostic sign is the ‘bird’s beak’ on barium swallow:
![](Achalasia/image%202.png)
Gastroscopy is also important to exclude strictures and cancer, and ofter shows food in the oesophagus. Manometry gives distinct patterns of achalasia:
- Type 1 = aperistalsis, often results in a dilated oesophagus
- Type 2 = residual contractions
- Type 3 = premature and disordered contractions
![](Achalasia/image%203.png)
(Normal manometry is demonstrated below)

Management is centred around symptom control, and is typically low-efficacy. More effective therapies include endoscopic LOS botox injection and balloon dilation, but these typically need to be repeated and carry the risk of causing fibrosis that will contraindicate subsequent surgery. The main surgical option is a laparoscopic cardiomyotomy, which is highly effective and typically well tolerated. It can also be carried out endoscopically (particularly in type 3 achalasia) with high efficacy but an increased rate of reflux.
