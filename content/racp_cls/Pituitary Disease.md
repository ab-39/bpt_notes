# Pituitary Disease
#bpt/endocrinology #bpt/racp_cls

### Overview
Pituitary disease is typically caused either by a pituitary mass, which can cause increased or reduced secretion of one of more pituitary hormones, or [[hypopituitarism]], which can arise from a variety of causes. 
- - -
### Pituitary Tumours
The most ‘classic’ finding associated with pituitary tumours is _bitemporal hemianopia_ due to compression of the optic chiasm, although headache, gaze palsies, and disordered hypothalamic function are also possible. 

The common pituitary tumours are associated with excess of specific hormones (often in combination), or hypopituitarism due to mass effect:
| Tumour/Condition                                 | Size                                                         | Clinical Presentation                                        | Histopathology                                 |
|--------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------|
| Prolactinoma* (most common, ~50%)                | Micro (<10mm, majority) or macro (>10mm, more mass effect symptoms) | Galactorrhoea, amenorrhoea, hypogonadism, erectile dysfunction | Chromophobe                                    |
| Acromegaly                                       | Variable                                                     | Change in appearance, hypopituitarism                        | Acidophil, may be specific G-protein mutations |
| Cushing’s disease                                | Small (few mm)                                               | Central obesity, cushingoid appearance                       | Basophil                                       |
| Nelson’s syndrome                                | Usually large (>10mm)                                        | Typically post-adrenalectomy, pigmentation (due to POMC excess) | Basophil                                       |
| [[Non-functioning adenoma]] ** (2nd most common) | Large (>10mm)                                                | Local symptoms described above, hypopituitarism              |                                                |

Note that TSH secreting tumours are rare. If a TFT pattern consistent with TSH-excess is found, it is best to ask the laboratory to repeat the test with a different assay, and then to check for thyroid hormone resistance. Treatment is as below. 

Management for pituitary tumours is typically surgical, although radiotherapy and non-curative medical therapy is also an option:
- Surgery - transsphenoidal (minor) or transcranial (major) route
- Radiotherapy - reduces recurrence rate after surgery
- Medical:
  - Dopamine agonists - e.g. bromocriptine (anti-prolactin)
  - Somatostatin analogues - e.g. octreotide (anti-GH), lanreotide
  - Growth-hormone receptor antagonists - e.g. pegvisomant 

Besides the above tumours, the most common masses of the region are craniopharygiomas, particularly in children.

### [[Hypopituitarism]]
Hypopituitarism can arise from a number of causes, and be associated with deficiencies of some or all of the anterior pituitary hormones. Posterior pituitary involvement is less common. Panhypopituitarism is commonly caused by pituitary tumours, surgery, or radiotherapy, and leads to a ‘classic’ appearance of pallor (“alabaster skin”) and hairlessness. The clinical signs and symptoms of the syndrome depend on the patter of hormones affected:
- [[Hypothyroidism]] - weight gain, slowness of thought and action, cold intolerance, dry skin, constipation, bradycardia, hypothermia
- [[Hypoadrenalism]] - mild hypotension, hyponatraemia, possibility of cardiovascular collapse when unwell
- [[Hypogonadism]] - loss of libido, loss of secondary sexual hair, amenorrhoea/erectile dysfunction, osteoporosis
- [[Growth Hormone Deficiency]] - growth failure in children, loss of energy and impaired wellbeing in adults with reduced lean body and bone mass

Hypophysitis is typically lymphocytic in aetiology, and associated with other autoimmune conditions. It often presents late in pregnancy. Other differentials include IgG4 related, granulomatous, and xanthomatous, which are all rare. Increasingly, immune checkpoint inhibitor hypophysitis is becoming a more common cause of secondary hypophysitis, and hypophysitis is typically the second most common endocrine ADR after thyroiditis. 

In addition to this, there are several syndromes associated with hypopituitarism:
- Kallmann syndrome - X-linked GnRH deficiency caused by mutations in the *KAL1* gene, classically associated with anosmia
- Septo-optic dysplasia - rare congenital syndrome, rarely associated with the *HESX1* gene, presents in childhood with a triad of midline forebrain abnormalities, optic nerve hypoplasia, and hypopituitarism
- Sheehan’s syndrome - pituitary infarction following postpartum haemorrhage 
- Pituitary apoplexy - rapid enlargement of a pituitary tumour due to infarction or haemorrhage, associated with headache, diplopia, and possible visual loss
- Empty sella syndrome - a radiological finding that typically truly indicates an eccentrically located, underfunctioning pituitary gland 

Diagnosis and management of these conditions depends on the specific hormonal axis affected.

### Diagnosing Hypopituitarism
#### ACTH Deficiency
Low morning cortisol and low/normal ACTH are suggestive of a diagnosis of central/secondary hypocortisolism. This can be confirmed using the _short Synacthen test_, with the caveat that it takes some weeks of hypopituitarism for the adrenal cortex to atrophy. 

The short Synacthen test involves the administration of tetracosactide, a synthetic form of ACTH, that is used to attempt to induce cortisol release from the adrenals. A cortisol level of <390nmol/L is suggestive of adrenal failure.

Note that this is a test of _adrenal_, and not pituitary function. The gold standard test for pituitary function would be an insulin toleracne test, with the adminsitration of enough rapid-acting insulin to reduce the serum glucose to <2.2mmol/L, which should induce the pituitary gland to release enough ACTH to bring serum cortisol to >500nmol/L.

#### TSH Deficiency
TSH can be measured directly in the serum, along with fT4.

#### GnRH Deficiency 
Pattern varies according to age and sex:
- Male, any age: low testosterone + low FH/LSH -> GnRH deficiency
- Female, premenopausal: low oestradiol + low FH/LSH -> GnRH deficiency
- Female, postmenopausal: low/normal FH/LSH -> GnRH deficiency (expect this to be high post-menopause)

#### GH Deficiency 
IGF-1 lacks sensitivity. Diagnosis is usually based on an insulin tolerance test or a glucagon stimulation test.
- - -
### Treating Hypopituitarism
#### ACTH Deficiency
Usually with divided dose hydrocortisone. Prednisolone _may_ be used but tend to avoid dexamethasone due to long half life.

#### Others
Replace the target hormone.
