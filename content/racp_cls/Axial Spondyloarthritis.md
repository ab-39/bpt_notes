# Axial Spondyloarthritis
#bpt/rheumatology #bpt/racp_cls
- - -
### Overview
The axial spondyloarthritides (SpA) are inflammatory disorders involving the axial skeleton predominantly, with possible peripheral joint involvement and charcteristic extra-articular features.

There are three key groups to be aware of:
- Axial spondyloarthritis with radiographic sacro-iliitis on XR  (raxSpA) = ankylosing spondylitis
  - Characterised by eventual progression to squaring of vertebrae, syndesmophyte formation, and Romanus lesions (shiny corners of vertebrae)
- Axial spondlyoarthritis without radiographic sacro-iliitis (nraxSpA)
  - Typically, ~80% of patients progress over a 20-year period to ankylosing spondylitis
  - Main predictor of progression is raised inflammatory markers 
- Peripheral spondyloarthritis 

Moreover, the spondyloarthritides are associated with the seronegative arthritides, such as reactive arthritis, psoriatic arthritis, and arthritis associated with IBD.
- - -
### Diagnosis
In patients with **greater than three months** with **onset before the age of 45y**, there are two pathways:
- Radiographic = sacroillitis on imaging (MRI or XR) **and** one or more SpA feature
  - Sacroiliitis progresses (NY criteria): erosions/sclerosis at margins -> pseudo-widening -> joint space narrowing -> ankylosis 
  - Although XR is pathognomic, MRI is more useful for early disease (bone marrow oedema -> fatty repair lesions -> syndesmophytes)
- Clinical = HLA-B27 positive **and** two or more SpA features

The SpA features considered diagnostic include joint features:
- Inflammatory back pain
- Arthritis
- Enthesitis 
- Family history of SpA
- Dactylitis 
And non-joint features:
- Uveitis
- Psoriasis
- IBD/colitis
- Response to NSAIDs
- Family history of SpA - about an 8% chance of developing SpA with a first-degree relative who has SpA
- HLA-B27 positivity (_but_ this is _not_ a screening or diagnostic test - it is an association! Only about 5% of B27 positive people develop SpA)
- Elevated CRP - can be normal in up to 25% of patients

Peripheral SpA is defined by ASAS as peripheral arthritis +/- enthesitis +/- dactylitis with:
- One or more non-joint SpA feature
- Two or more joint SpA features

### Epidemiology
Common - ~1% of the population.
Ankylosing spondylitis has a male predominance, but among all SpA there is no gender predilection.
Average age of onset is 3rd decade of life. 
Smoking is associated with a worse prognosis, quicker radoigraphic progression, and higher disease activity. 
- - - -
### Pathophysiology
The inflammatory pathophysiology of SpA is intimately tied to _HLA-B27_, an allele that encodes MHC class I in humans. In addition to this, over 100 non-MHC loci have been implicated in the development of AxSpA, including:
- _ERAP1_
- _IL23R_ 
Usually, these are used to generate a polygenic risk score. 

This genetic predisposition is associated with environmental factors that can precipitate inflammation:
- Biomechanical stress at entheses
- Infection of the skin
- Alterations in gut microbiome

The key cytokines implicated in this process are [[interleukins 12 and 23]], particularly the latter. IL-23R are expressed on multiple cells, but the most relevant is the Th17 cell, that produces [[interleukin 17]]. 
![](Axial%20Spondyloarthritis/image%202.png)
IL-17R tends to be expressed on epithelial cells, endothelial cells, and fibroblasts. Importantly for SpA, synovial cells are fibroblasts, and when they are exposed to IL-17, this induced inflammation and synovitis. 

This process of inflammation can cause both bone formation and erosion, which leads to characteristics imaging findings such as pencil-in-cup deformities and syndesmophyte formation in the spine.

### Clinical Features
#### Axial Features
- [[Inflammatory back pain]] - often alternating back/buttock pain
  - Morning stiffness is one of the most specific signs 
- Restriction in spinal movement - characteristic posture of ankylosing spondylitis
  - Tends to lead to a loss of lumbar and cervical lordosis, with accompanying severe kyphosis of the thoracic spine

#### Extra-axial MSK Features:
- Peripheral arthritis - commonly an asymmetric oligoarthritis of the lower limbs
- Enthesitis

#### Extra-articular Features:
- Anterior uveitis - common (~50%) and limited, usually unilateral but can alternate eyes
- IBD
- Psoriasis
- ILD - apical fibrosis
- Aortic regurgitation

> Think of the ‘5 A’s’:
> - Axial disease

> - Aortic regurgitation
> - Anterior uveitis

### Management
First line = NSAIDs, in conjunction with PT, exercise, etc.
- Generally trial at least 2 NSAIDs over a 12-week period prior to progressing to bDMARDs
- Can consider local steroids or conventional DMARDs if primarily peripheral presentation

> [!NOTE] Conventional DMARDs
> Conventional DMARDs can be used in peripheral but not axial disease.  
> Usual options are MTX, SSZ, and LEF.

Second line - typically after failure of twelve weeks of NSAIDs and exercise in both AS and nr-axSpA:
- TNF⍺ inhibition - e.g. adalimumab, infliximab, etanercept, etc.
  - Note that TNF blockers are best for uveitis, with the exception of etanercept 
  - Evidence appears to be that these drugs (and secukinumab) reduce radiographic progression after 2y
  - If comorbid IBD or uveitis -> inflximab or adalimumab
- JAK inhibition = e.g. tofacitinib, upadacitinib
- IL-17 inhibition = e.g. secukinumab, ixekizumab
  - First-line if comorbid psoriasis
  - Note that IL-17 blockers are not to be used in IBD

Biologics or small molecule inhibitors should be considered if there is failure of standard treatment and high disease activity, as well as the recommendation of a rheumatologist. 

Predictors of response to biologics include:
- Short disease duration
- Young age
- High inflammatory markers
- Worse inflammation on MRI
- Less functional impairment at baseline
- HLA-B27 positive 

IL-12 and IL-23 inhibitors (e.g. ustekinumab) have not been found to be affected for axial spondyloarthopathies, but can be used in cases of enthesitis or psoriasis.
