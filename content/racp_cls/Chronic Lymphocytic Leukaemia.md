# Chronic Lymphocytic Leukaemia
#bpt/haematology #bpt/racp_cls
- - -
### Overview
Chronic lymphocytic leukaemia is a monoclonal proliferation of _mature_ B-cells defined by an absolute number of malignant cells in the blood. Recall that in the non-diseased state, somatic rearrangement, somatic hypermutation, and class switching drive the incredible diversity of B-cell antigen receptors. In disease, this diversity is restricted, yielding a monoclonal phenotype.

If CLL arises in the lymph nodes without peripheral blood involvement, it is called **small lymphocytic lymphoma**. 
- - -
### Epidemiology
CL is a disease of older adults, with median age at diagnosis of about 70y. It is the most common leukaemia in the Western world.
- - -
### Diagnosis
The definition of CLL is of the presence of >5x10,000,000,000/L small, mature, monoclonal lymphocytes in the peripheral blood. A common finding in the peripheral blood is the so-called ‘smudge’ cell.

The precursor state to CLL is ‘monoclonal B-cell lymphocytosis’ or MBL. 
- - -
### Genetics and Immunophenotype
Key mutations in CLL that carry an unfavourable prognosis include del17p12/other TP53 mutations, trisomy 12, del11q22 and the presence of unmutated IGHV genes (i.e. <2% distance from germline). The del13q14 mutation carries a favourable prognosis. 

CLL typically express CD5 and CD19 strongly, and CD20 and CD23 less so. 
- - -
### Staging
The older staging systems for CLL were the Rai and Binet systems, but they have been superseded by the CLL IPI.
- - -
### Clinical Features
About 70% of cases of CLL are asymptomatic at diagnosis. Symptomatic patients typically present with lymphadenopathy (50%), hepatosplenomegaly (20-50%), B-symptoms, anaemia/thrombocytopenia, and immunosuppression. Around 10% of patients experience autoimmune complications such as AIHA or ITP (Evans’ syndrome), or less commonly things like Bullous pemphigoid. 

#### Richter Transformation
CLL will transform to diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (or another high-grade lymphoma) in about 5% of cases, at a rate of <1% a year. As patients live longer, this is obviously becoming more prevalent. This portends a poor prognosis, as DLBCL is typically less responsive to conventional chemotherapy.
- - -
### Treatment 
Most patients with CLL can be approached with watchful waiting, with the aim being to initiate treatment (just) before patients become symptomatic. 

Red flags to begin treatment include:
- Worsening anaemia (<100g/L) or thrombocytopenia (<100/nL) - i.e. Binet C
- Massive, progressive or symptomatic hepatosplenomegaly or lymphadenopathy 
- Progressive lymphocytosis (e.g. >50% increase in two months) or lymphocyte doubling time <6 months
- AIHA/ITP unresponsive to steroids 
- Symptomatic extranodal involvement
- Constitutional/B-type symptoms

#### Supportive and Preventive Care
Patents with CLL _cannot_ receive live vaccinations, but should be kept up to date with vaccinations as far as possible - e.g. flu, VZV, Pneumovax, COVID. Recently, the Shingrix vaccine has opened up the possibility of vaccinating for shingles as well.

Patients with hypogammaglobulinaemia and recurrent sinopulmonary infections should be treated with IV/SCIG. 

#### Chemoimmunotherapy 
The ‘classic’ chemotherapy for CLL has been FCR:
- Fludarabine
- Cyclophosphamide
- Rituximab

This is still given to young, healthy patients with aggressive mutations and has been found to work, but is fading away as targeted therapies become more and more available.

#### Bruton’s Tyrosine Kinase Inhibitors
BTK is critical in the signalling and activation of B-cells that promotes cell survival, growth, and migration. Blocking BTK with agents such as _ibrutinib_ or _zanubrutinib_/_acalabrutinib_. 

These are daily medications that require continuous treatment to be effective. When treatment is initiated, this disrupts B-cell adherence and may actually lead to a period of initial lymphocytosis. It is important to remember that BTK inhibitors are _not_ curative, and as such cannot be stopped once treatment is initiated. Side-effects include AF, bleeding (due to a reduction in collagen mediated platelet aggregation; needs to be withheld prior to surgery), and skin changes.  

Key studies: RESONATE 2

#### BCL2 Inhibitors
BCL2 is a potent cellular inhibitor of apoptosis that is normally inhibited by BH3. BH3 analogues, such as _venetoclax_ are potent inducers of apoptosis. Due to how effective venetoclax is at triggering apoptosis, its dose needs to be carefully titrated upwards. Common side effects include tumour lysis syndrome, neutropenia (treatable with GCSF), nausea and interactions with CYP3A4. Venetoclax is often given in combination with obinutuzumab (an anti-CD20-mab). 

Key studies: MURANO, CLL14 

Monitoring of treatment is with ‘measured residual disease’. The G101V mutation can confer venetoclax resistance.
