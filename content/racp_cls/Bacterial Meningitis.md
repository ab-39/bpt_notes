# Bacterial Meningitis

### Microbiology
In adults, bacterial meningitis is most commonly caused by:
1. _Streptococcus pneumoniae_
   - Suspect if there are predispositing conditions (usu. ENT infections), CSF leak, cochlear implants, asplenia, HIV/AIDS, or other reaosns for immunosuppression
2. _Neisseria meningitidis_ - decreasing prevalence with age
   - Suspect in young adults without comorbidities, particularly in the context of an epidemic
   - Meningococcaemia is relatively uncommon, and so the absence of the rash has a poor negative predictive value
   - Key risk factor is deficiencies of C5-C9, which can either be congenital in the context of terminal complement deficiencies, or acquired with eculizumab treatment
   
> [!IMPORTANT] Complications of Meningococcaemia
> A key complication of systemic meningococcal infection is _Waterhouse-Friedrichsen syndrome_ - infarction or haemorrhage of the adrenals leading to acute adrenal insufficiency. 

3. _Listeria monocytogenes_ - increasing prevalence with age 
   - Consider in those aged over fifty, on long term glucocorticoids or other immunosuppressants, diabetes, alcoholism, cirrhosis, CKD, malignancy, HIV/AIDS, or transplant recipients 
4. Group B streptococci
5. _Haemophilus influenzae_ 

Gram-negative meningitis is rare, but can be associated with:
- Head trauma
- Neurosurgery
- Strongyloides hyperinfection

A key bacterial differential is tuberculous meningitis, which typicaly presents with a subacute course. Unlike other bacterial causes of meningitis, the leucocytosis tends to be lymphocyte predominant with some neutrophils.

### Investigations
The lumbar puncture is the diagnostic test _par excellence_ for meningitis. Some points:
- CT brain is _not_ required for many patients prior to LP, and is associated with increased mortality 
- Treatment prior to LP reduces yield of CSF cultures, although PCR is able to partially overcome this 
- In general, CT before lumbar puncture is only required if there is/are:
  - GCS <15 (<12 in UK/European guidelines)
  - Focal deficits including cranial nerve palsies
  - Papilloedema
  - New onset seizures within past week
  - Symptoms >4/7 duration
  - Immunosuppression
  - Known CNS disease 
- Note that guidelines vary significantly

Lumbar puncture evidence for bacterial meningitis includes:
- Opening pressure >20cmH2O
- Positive Gram stain
- Protein >1g/L
- CSF:blood glucose ratio ≤0.5
- WCC >1,000
  - Predominantly neutrophils 
  - The exception is _Listeria_ meningitis which can can be lymphocytic 
  - Viral meningitis may be neutrophilic in the first 48h
- Positive pneumococcal antigen test (more sensitive and specific than culture and Gram stain)
- PCR panel retains fair sensitivity for the first week of antibiotic therapy, and can be used for:
  - _N. meningitidis_
  - _S. pneumoniae_
  - _L. monocytogenes_ 
  - Enteroviruses
  - HSV
  - VZV

### Treatment
#### Adjunctive Dexamethasone
Dexamethasone (IV 10mg QID) should be given with or before the first dose of antibacterials, as it:
- Reduces mortality (RR 0.84)
- Reduces hearing loss (RR 0.74)
- Reduces other neurologic sequelae (RR 0.83)
- Reduces neuronal damage due to inflammation and oedema
- Reduces the inflammatory response from antibiotic-mediated bacteriolysis

Note that the best evidence is for steroids in pneumococcal meningitis, where the usual treatment course is four days. They are not harmful in meningococcal meningitis, and can be stopped after diagnosis. This evidence for benefit appears to be diminished in low-income settings, although this could be associated with late presentation and/or high rates of HIV coinfection. Steroids are associated with increased mortality in neurolisteriosis and cryptococcal meningitis. 

> [!TIP] Mnemonic
> **S**teroids for **S**trep

#### Antibiotic Choice
The antibiotic of choice is high-dose ceftriaxone (IV 2g BD), with potential additions:
- Pneumococcus suspected -> vancomycin
- Listeria suspected -> benzylpenicillin
- Penicillin hypersensitivity -> can consider:
  - Vancomycin + ciprofloxacin
  - Moxifloxacin
  - Meropenem
  - Chloramphenicol

#### Precautions
Meningococcal meningitis requires droplet precautions until 24h of effective antibiotics have been administered. Post-exposure prophylactic antibiotics are recommended for household contacts and hospital staff who have performed airway management without a mask. Options include:
- Rifampicin 600mg PO BD for four doses
- Ciprofloxacin 500mg PO once
- Ceftriaxone 250mg IM once (best option in pregnancy)

#### Vaccination
Vaccination for pneumococcal or meningoccal disease is recommended for adult patients for secondary prevention, or for primary prevention in patients at high risk.
