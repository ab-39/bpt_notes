### Basic Neural Anatomy
The central nervous system comprises the brain and spinal cord, as well as the optic nerve. The remainder of the cranial nerves, the spinal nerve roots, and the remainder of the peripheral and autonomic nerves together make up the peripheral nervous system.

In the spinal cord, there is a division of sensory and motor nerve bodies:
- Dorsal root ganglia -> sensory nerves
- Anterior horn -> motor nerves 

There is a numerical scheme grades neurones based on their size, velocity of conduction, and presence of myelin, ranging from the largest, heavily myelinated, predominantly motor neurones (Ia/Ib) to the finest, thinly myelinated, predominantly autonomic fibres (IV). These different nerves are typically _differentially_ involved in peripheral neuropathies. 

Additionally, nerves are also grouped into three broad classes:
1. A = myelinated fibres:
   - A-⍺ = the largest fibres = muscle afferents
   - A-δ = smaller myelinated fibres = vibration and proprioception
2. B = thinly myelinated fibres = preganglionic autonomic efferents, visceral afferents
3. C = unmyelinated fibres = pain and temperature sensation

### Epidemiology
Peripheral neuropathies are _very_ common, affecting 2-8% of the population, with incidence increasing with age. 
- - -
### Aetiology
Although there are over a hundred possible causes of neuropathy, the most common causes are diabetes, nutritional deficiency, alcohol intake, vasculitis, systemic disease, and toxin ingestion. That said, ~20% of patients will never find a definitive cause. 
- - -
### Diagnostic Approach 
As there is such a broad range of differentials, it is important to have a systematic approach to narrow these down. Consider: 
1. Is the neuropathy motor, sensory or both?
   - Does it involve large or small fibres?
2. Is a single nerve affected (mononeuropathy), multiple single nerves (mononeuritis multiplex), or multiple nerves (polyneuropathy)?
3. Is there a primary axonal process at play, or demyelination?
   - Axonal degeneration -> Wallerian degeneration of both axon and sheath = length-dependent pattern -> as the nerves supplying the feet are the longest, they are the first affected 
   - Acquired (usually inflammatory) condition -> myelin loss = patchy pattern -> rapid recovery (if acquired, can be slower if genetic)
*** 
### Patterns of Neuropathy
1. Length dependent 
   * Predominantly sensory, but can progress to motor problems (typically muscle wasting rather than weakness) and absent/reduced reflexes later in the disease course 
   * Small fibre neuropathy -> sensory loss and pain
   * The longer the nerve, the more likely it is to be affected 
   * Tends to be a problem of transport proteins 
   * Vulnerable to toxic or metabolic insult 
2. Multifocal neuropathy -> multiple nerves affected (e.g. vasculitis, mononeuritis multiplex)
   - Multifocal motor neuropathy -> ‘conduction block’ is pathognomic, anti-GM1 antibodies
3. Poly(radiculo)neuropathy -> often the most proximal and symmetric; motor predominant 
   - Often sensory sparing as the sensory nerve roots are outside the spinal cord, and therefore immune to Wallerian degeneration from this source 
4. Neuronopathy -> a “pure” affliction of a nerve at its origin in either the dorsal root ganglion (sensory) or anterior horn cell (motor)

### Clinical Signs and Symptoms
| Class                                               | Signs and Symptoms                                           |
|-----------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------|
| Negative - reduced nerve activity                   | Motor: weakness<br>Sensory: numbness, sensory ataxia, temperature insensitivity |
| Positive - inappropriate spontaneous nerve activity | Motor: cramps, twitching<br>Sensory: pain, paraesthesiae, allodynia, hyperaesthesia<br>Often early symptoms |
| Autonomic dysfunction                               | GI: early satiety, bloating, constipation<br>GU: impotence, urinary incontinence<br>Other: orthostatic hypotension, cold extremities, cutaneous vasomotor changes, hyper- or hypohidrosis  |

It is important when discussing these symptoms with a patient to consider the effect on execution of their activities of daily living. 

Signs evident on examination: 
- Skin - vasculitic rashes (livedo reticularis, purpura), etc.; hyperpigmentation
- Toxins - Mees lines (lines in nails perpendicular to finger = arsenic)
- Hair - alopecia, calf hair loss
- Thickened nerves (ulnar, superficial radial)
- Eyes - optic pallor (vit. B12)
- Trophic changes (2’ to nerve damage rather than underlying disease process)

By nerve fibre:
- Large -> vibration, proprioception, light touch, Romberg sign
- Small -> pinprick, temperature, reflexes (typically normal)

### Aetiology 
The temporal profile of the neuropathy gives clues as to the underlying aetiology:
- Acute -> infection, vasculitis, GBS, porphyria, toxins
- Subacute -> toxins, nutritional, malignancy/paraneoplastic syndromes, metabolic
- Relapsing -> demyelination (e.g. CIDP, HNPP), repeated toxic exposures, vasculitis 

Length-independent neuropathies tend to point demyelinating causes, often immune or infectious. 

Similarly, motor vs. sensory pattern can point to different causes:
- Motor -> GBS, CIDP, toxins (Pb, amiodarone, botulism), porphyria, hereditary neuropathies
- Sensory -> diabetes, nutritional deficiencies, uraemia, HIV, leprosy, amyloidosis, Sjögren, sarcoidosis, paraneoplastic syndromes

### Basic Investigations
In addition to ‘routine’ bloods, patients should get:
- HbA1c/fasting glucose/oGTT
- Vitamin B12, homocysteine
- Vasculitic screen - ANCA, ANA, anti-dsDNA, RF, etc.
- Sensory neuropathy -> anti-Hu,  coeliac screen

### Advanced Investigation 
#### Nerve Conduction Studies
Involve stimulation of a nerve, and then electrical recordings of the nerve and the associated muscle. These are standard in the evaluation of large fibre neuropathies, and help to exclude muscle, radicular, and motoneuron pathology.

#### Nerve Biopsy
Rare today. 

#### Associations with paraproteinaemia 
- IgM has the strongest association 
- Anti-MAG ->  distal demyelinating sensory neuropathy
- Anti-Gd1b -> cryoglobulinaemia 
- IgG -> myeloma 

Immunofixation is the best option here.
