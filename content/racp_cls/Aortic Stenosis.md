### What is aortic stenosis?
Aortic stenosis refers to a form of [[valvular heart disease]] in which the aortic valve surface is narrowed to <2sqcm. If untreated, severe, symptomatic AS has a _very_ poor five-year mortality. 
- - -
### Aetiology and Epidemiology 
Aortic stenosis is most typically caused by long term degeneration and associated dystrophic calcification of the valve, particularly in elderly patients, which occurs in approximately 12% of people above 75y. 

The most common congenital valvular lesion leading to aortic stenosis is a congenital bicuspid valve, which was found to account for 53% of patients who ultimately required valve replacement in one study, although the population prevalence is quite low at ~1%. 

Other important causes to consider are rheumatic heart disease and radiation therapy.

### Clinical Presentations
Symptoms:
- Dyspnoea
- Fatigue
- **Chest pain**
- **Syncope**
- **Heart Failure**

These later three symptoms are signs of severity. 

Clinical examination findings:
- Murmur -> aortic stenosis is typified by a _crescendo-decrescendo ejection systolic murmur_, that peaks later and later in systole depending on the severity
- Pulse -> a slow-rising (‘parvus et tardus’) pulse is a sign of severity 

Clinical signs of severity include:
- Pulsus parvus et tardus
- Late peaking/harshness of the murmur 
- Soft S2 with paradoxical splitting 
- Presence of S4
- Heart failure 
- Palpable aortic thrill

### Echocardiography
Aortic stenosis is determined on echocardiography on the basis of the _continuity equation_. This assumes that the product of the cross-sectional area of the LVOT and the velocity of blood flow in that area should be the same as the product of those two parameters across the aortic valve. As LVOT area and the two velocities can be measured, this allows for the aortic valve area to be calculated on that basis. 

Echocardiography allows for us to make an objective assessment of AS severity, based on:
- Velocity: severe = >4m/s
- Mean gradient: severe = >40mmHg
- Area: severe = <1sqcm

### Treatment
There is no medical treatment for AS - a diseased valve must be replaced. The current ESC guidelines recommend intervention for:
- Asymptomatic patients with severe AS **and** LVEF <50% (without another cause)
- Asymptomatic patients with severe AS **and** demonstrable symptoms on exercise testing
- Intervention can be considered in asyptomatic patients with severe AS with any of:
  - LVEF <55%
  - Drop in BP on exercise testing
  - Very severe AS (gradient >60mmHg or Vmax >5m/s)
  - Severe valve calcification with progressive disease
  - Markedly elevated BNP levels
  - Prior to high-risk non-cardiac surgery 
- Symptomatic patients 

In terms of long case buzzwords - we need to mention a “heart team discussion” and “shared decision-making” with the patient. 

#### SAVR vs TAVI
- Young, fit patients, or those at low surgical risk -> surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR)
  - Young, healthy patients -> Ross procedure (transplantation of the native pulmonary valve into the aortic position, with implantation of a prosthesis into the pulmonary position) = most durable option
  - Older, but healthy patients -> mechanical valve (main drawback is anticoagulation with warfarin)
  - Other factors in favour of SAVR include: 
    - Bicuspid AV
    - Risk of coronary obstruction 
    - LVOT calcification
    - Unfavourable anatomy
    - Aortic root aneurysm 
    - Septal hypertrophy 
- Older patients (>70y), or those at prohibitive surgical risk (EuroSCORE >8%) -> transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI). Other factors in favour of TAVI include:
  - Poor transfemoral access 
  - Porcelain aorta 
  - Intact CAGS in situ
  - Severe chest wall deformity 
  - Comorbidity/surgical risk

Patients at elevated/high risk should have the decision made following a multidisciplinary team discussion.
