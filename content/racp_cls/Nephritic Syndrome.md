### What is nephritic syndrome?
Nephritic syndrome is a state characterised by inflammation of the glomeruli, and defined by:
- Haematuria with RBC casts and/or dysmorphic RBCs
  - Asymptomatic haematauria +/- proteinuria is often the canary in the coalmine for glomerulonephritis 
  - Note that if the nephritic syndrome is sufficiently severe, heavy proteinuria _can_ occur 
- Oliguria and uraemia 
- Hypertension
- Progressive reduction in eGFR 

Additionally, oedema and/or proteinuria is not uncommon, as sufficient glomerular damage will also damage the podocytes. 

Additionally, nephritis can occur aggressively, with glomerular haematuria and kidney failure developing over the course of weeks to months, in which case it is termed **rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis** (RPGN), and typically characterised by the development of _crescents_ or macrophages and epithelial cells in Bowman’s space. In the event that there is no necrosis, the term nephritic syndrome or acute nephritis is typically used instead. 
- - -
### Clinical Presentation
Generally with a rapidly progressive AKI with hypertension, with or without oedema depending on the degree of proteinuria. This is a medical emergency where prompt diagnosis and treatment can reduce morbidity and mortality. Systemic symptoms may give clues as to the aetiology.

### Causes of the Nephritic Syndrome/RPGN
#### Post-Streptococcal Glomerulonephritis
PSGN is a common cause of acute nephritis in children, typically 1-3 weeks after a streptococcal infection, classically Lancefield type A (i.e. _Streptococcus pyogenes_). The pathogenetic process is to do with immune complex formation and deposition in the kidneys, leading to florid, diffuse inflammation on biopsy. This is driven by activation of the complement alternative pathway, and so C3 is typically reduced while C4 is normal. The clinical presentation can range from asymptomatic to florid nephritic syndrome with haematuria, proteinuria, hypertension, and AKI. Management is typically supportive, with a small role for corticosteroids. A small percentage of patients will progress to CKD and hypertension later in life. 

#### [[IgA Nephropathy]]
The most common cause of the nephritic syndrome, probably accounting for around _half_ of the biopsied cases of the disease. Classically presents with synpharyngitic haematuria in young men.  The pathogenesis related to abnormal circulating IgA and mucosal abnormalities. Treatment is generally with RAAS blockade and SGLT2 inhibition if there is proteinuria. In some high risk patients, steroids (e.g. targeted release budesonide to Peyer patches) or mycophenolate.

#### Alport Syndrome
Alport syndrome is a rare, X-linked disorder of the COL4⍺5 collaged chain, a component of the GBM. It is characterised by haematuria, sub-nephrotic range proteinuria, progressive kidney disease, and high-frequency sensorineural hearing loss. The loss of COL4⍺5 leads to eventual proteolysis, thickening, and deterioration of the GBM. The relative preservation of the slit diaphragm is the reason for the relatively low-range proteinuria. Interestingly, anti-GBM disease never occurs in patients with Alport syndrome _unless_ the patient receives a transplant with a different type of COL4. 

#### Anti-GBM Glomerulonephritis 
Anti-GBM GN is part of the spectrum of disorders in which there is autoantibody mediated destruction of the glomerular basement membrane, specifically the non-collagenous (NCI) component of type IV collagen. One third of patients have a renal-restricted RPGN, while the majority undergo concurrent lung haemorrhage - Goodpasture syndrome. The pathophysiological mechanism is to do with the breakdown peripheral regulation of T-cells, leading to eventual stimulation of auto-reactive CD4+ T-cells and subsequent production of autoantibodies. Management involves PLEX, steroids, and immunosuppression (usu. CYC). The prognosis is related to the extent of the glomerular damage. Unlike many autoimmune diseases, anti-GBM disease tends _not_ to follow a relapsing-remitting course, instead remitting spontaneously within three years if untreated, which correlates to the re-establishment of peripheral tolerance by CD25+ cells. De novo anti-GBM disease can occur post-transplant if the patient is exposed to abnormal COL4 (e.g. in Alport syndrome)

#### [[ANCA Vasculitis]]
Small vessel vasculitis that can affect any organ, but most commonly the glomeruli or airways. Rare (10-50/1,000,000/y) but appears to be increasing. c/PR3-ACNA tends to be more acute/aggressive than p/MPO-ANCA. In terms of a tissue diagnosis, kidney biopsy tends to have the highest yield, particularly if there is sediment in the urine. Treatment consists of induction immunosuppression with cyclophosphamide or rituximab (or PLEX in extreme cases - e.g. ANCA+GBM, advaced AKI, pulmonary haemorrhage), followed by 12-24 months of maintenance with rituximab, AZA, MTX, or MMF, plus TMP-SMX prophylaxis (which also appears to have an independent protective effect against relapse). Emerging therapies include avacopan, a complement 5a receptor antagonist. Risk of relapse as high as 60%, particularly if c/PR3-ANCA positive. Note that although ANCA titres are monitored, they are not diagnostic and as many as 60% of patients with rising titres will not have a relapse. Treatment for relapse is repeat induction. Transplant is often successful.

### Chronic Nephritic Syndrome
This is a very common state, generally attributable to a low-grade IgA nephropathy. Around 5% of individuals in large cohort studies have had low-grade nephritic syndrome. The most common issue is isolated microscopic haematuria, and patients often present with hypertension, microscopic haematuria, or premature gout. Detection is most often incidental.
