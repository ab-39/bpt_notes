### What is epilepsy?
Epilepsy, ό ιερός νούσος, is an illness as old as mankind and charged with alternating associations of the divine and demonic. It is attested in the historic record as far back as ancient Akkad, and is listed in the Code of Hammurabi as a valid reason to get a refund on a slave. 

Simply, epilepsy is the tendency to recurrent, spontaneous [[seizures]].
- - -
### Epidemiology
Epilepsy is the most common serious disorder of the brain, comprising ~0.5% of the global burden of disease, at a prevalence of about 650/100,000. In Australia, at least 250,000 people suffer from epilepsy (~6/1000), with an incidence of ~50/100,000/y

### Diagnostic Criteria
The definition of epilepsy means that a patient needs to have:
- At least 2 unprovoked seizures
  - In general: 
    - One seizure -> 40% chance of recurrence
    - Two seizures -> 80% chance of recurrence 
- More than 24h apart 

**Or**, one seizure with high-risk (>60% in 10y) clinical (i.e. abnormal examination or seizure in sleep), electroencephalographic, or neuroradiological findings. 

The most important diagnostic tool is the history:
- The _objective history_ - what actually happened
- The _patient’s history_ - what they experienced, any aura
- The _observer’s history_ - what witnesses noticed 

Important points on history for epilepsy include:
- Risk factors - febrile seizures, head trauma, CNS infection, family history, history of pregnancy complications, learning/physical disabilities
- Prodrome/aura
- Event
- Postictal symptoms
- ’Tell-tale’ features 

The differential diagnoses for epileptic seizures is usually quite narrow:
- Non-epileptic seizures
  - Organic
  - Psychogenic - typically last longer than a seizure (~5-10min), and have few if any postictal symptoms
- Syncope - variable postictal symptoms
- TIA - negative symptoms predominate
- Migraine - prolonged ‘sensory march’ 

The electroencephalogram is useful for diagnosing specific epilepsy syndromes, but is rarely applied to someone during a seizure - as such, a normal EEG does not rule out epilepsy (i.e. it is a poorly sensitive test with a low NPV). 
- General measures:
  - Alpha = wakeful = 9-12Hz
  - Beta = benzodiazepines = 13-15Hz
  - Theta = drowsy/encephalopathic = 5-8Hz
  - Delta = asleep/encephalopathic = 1-4Hz 
- ’Classic’ epileptiform activity on an EEG -> spikes (50-70ms) and sharp waves (70-200ms)
  - Confer a risk of recurrence of 30-70% in 1y

- 3Hz spike and wave discharges -> idiopathic generlaised epilepsy

### Epilepsy Syndromes
There are _dozens_ of epilepsy syndromes. Some of the most common (and commonly tested) are:
- Juvenile Absence Epilepsy
  - Affects adolescents, but tends to persist into adulthood
  - Clasically absence or GTC seizures; occasionally myoclonic seizures
    - Absence -> 3Hz spike-wave discharges on EEG
  - Responds well to valproate 
- Juvenile Myoclonic Epilepsy
  - Second most common type of epilepsy, associated with _ICK_ gene mutations
  - Affects adolescents
  - Classic seizure is **myoclonic seizures soon after waking**; GTCS very frequent, absence seizures occur in 1/3rd
    - 3Hx spike-wave discharges on EEG
  - Exacerbated by sleep depirvation and EtOH
  - Valproate is first line although prognosis for remission is poor 
- Mesial Temporal Lobe Epilepsy with Hippocampal Sclerosis
  - Most common form of epilepsy in adults, most frequent indication for epilepsy surgery
  - Risk factors: prolonged febrile seizures, CNS infections, head trauma, etc.
  - Clinical features:
    - Auda: déjà vu, jamais vu, dreamlike state, rising epigastric sensation, etc.
    - Seizures: focal impaired awareness seizures +/- automatisms tend to be common, frequently progress to bilateral tonic-clonic seizures 
  - Tend to see hippocampal atrophy on MRI
  - Frequently refractory to medications -> surgery has good remission rates

### Treatment
[[Antiepileptic drugs]] are _prophylactic_ medications, and not seizure-terminating drugs. Some key considerations:
- Long term prophylaxis is only warranted for epilepsy
- Repeated provoked seizures (e.g. alcohol withdrawal) are _not_ epilepsy
- Some syndromes (e.g. Rolandic syndrome, tumours without seizures) do not warrant treatment

In general:
- Valproic acid -> first line for GTCS, myoclonic, and typical absence seizures 
  - Pure absence in childhood -> ethosuximide
  - Idiopathic generalised epilepsy -> valproate 
  - Unknown semiology -> valproate 
- Lamotrigine -> first line for focal seizures, second line for other types 
  - Carbamazepine has three key caveats:
    - Unacceptably high risk of SJS/TEN in (SE) Asians with HLA-B58*01
    - Significantly elevated risk of SJS in Europeans with HLA-A31*01
    - If there is a rash on carbamazepine, the risk of ADRs to other AEDs is also elevated
  - Levetiracetam is _not_ non-inferior to lamotrigine
- Women of childbearing age -> lamotrigine 

There is some evidence that the ketogenic diet (pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6517043), and fasting more broadly (Mark 9:28) may be effective in treatment resistant epilepsy.

### Complications
#### SUDEP
Sudden unexplained death in epilepsy (SUDEP) affects 1/1000 patients with epilepsy, and the exact aetiology is unknown. The number one predictor of SUDEP is frequency of tonic-clonic seizures. Other risk factors include:
- Nocturnal seizures
- Treatment resistant seizures
- Long duration of epilepsy
- Dravet syndrome

#### Psychosis
Can be divided into interictal, intraictal (rare), and postictal:
- Interictal - ~5% of patients develop a chronic, schizophrenia-like psychosis
  - RFs: temporal love epilepsy, age of onset of epilepsy, refractory course
  - Tend to be responsive to antipsychotics
- Postictal - ~10% of patiets
  - Follow exacerbations or clusters of FIA seizures, tend to return to baseline after seizures for 12-72h before onset of psychosis
  - Can last for weeks
  - i.e. natural history: cluster of FIA seizures -> 1-2 normal days -> weeks of psychosis 

#### Status Epilepticus 
_At least 5 minutes of continuous seizure or two or more seizures between which there is incomplete recovery of consciousness_. **High** mortality - one in five. Can be a vicious cycle:
>Prolonged seizures -> brain injury -> new epileptogenic focus 

Treatment is with benzodiazepines, either:
- Lorazepam
- Midazolam

Add a second-line agent, one of:
- Phenytoin
- Levetiracetam
- Phenobarbital 

If there are ongoing seizures at this point, the patient typically needs an ETT and a mdiazolam and propofol infusion. 

_Non-Convulsive Status Epilepticus_
Tricky! Can occur after a convulsive seizure (e.g. prolonged postictal phase that is actually an ongoing focal seizure) or independently of one. Tend to manifest as changes in behaviour and cognition _without_ motor manifestations - the only way to diagnose is EEG. Increased risk if:
- Known history of epilepsy
- Structural brain abnormality 
- Benzodiazepine withdrawal
Generally less ‘life-threatening’ than convulsive status, but prolonged NCSE can lead to neuronal injury and poor outcomes. Treatment is the same as convulsive status epilepticus.
