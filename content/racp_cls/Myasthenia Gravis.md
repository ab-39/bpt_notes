# Myasthenia Gravis
#bpt/neurology #bpt/racp_cls

### What is myasthenia gravis?
MG is an autoimmune [[neuromuscular junction disorder]] in which autoantibodies against skeletal muscle acetylcholine receptors lead to a deficiency of those receptors at the NMJ. This leads to a classical picture of weakness and fatiguability of the skeletal muscle. 

#### Pathophysiology
MG is characterised by an antibody-mediated, T-cell dependent attack on the postsynaptic membrane, leading to the ‘flattening’ of that surface, with destruction of the AChRs. The thymus is thought to play a role in this process, with hyperplasia found in 65% of patients and a thymoma in 15%.
- - -
### Epidemiology
Common: 20-200/100,000, with a female predominance, particularly when younger 
- - -
### Clinical Presentation
The main clinical characteristic of MG is _fluctuating and fatigable_ muscle weakness that worsens with exercise and improves with rest. The most common clinical presentations include:
- **Ptosis - often asymmetrical** 
- **Binocular diplopia** 
- Bulbar weakness - flaccid dysarthria, dysphagia, jaw-closure weakness
- Facial weakness - weak smile
- Neck weakness - flexion << extension
- Limb weakness - usually proximal and symmetric

Note that some patients will never develop more than ocular signs, and are said to have _ocular MG_. 

#### Common Triggers
- Surgery
- Pregnancy and puerperium
- Heat (hence the famous ‘ice-pack test’ of ptosis)
- Stress
- Infection
- BMT
- Medications (several antibiotics including azithromycin, magnesium, botox, IFN-⍺, d-penicillamine, several cardiac medications, anaesthetics, neuromuscular blockers, checkpoint inhibitors)

#### Myasthenic Crisis
In approximately 15% of patients, the respiratory muscles will weaken to the point that will require ventilatory support in ICU. Rapid identification and treatment with steroids/IVIG/PLEX is also critical.
- - -
### Diagnosis
Myasthenia Graves = autoantibody + weakness
When there is no autoantibody positive, other investigations can also be used:
- EMG/NCS - look for CMAP decrement >10%, single-fibre EMG is the most sensitive test
- Ice-pack test = application of ice-pack to ptotic eyelid for five minutes, expecting to see improvement - highly sensitive and specific for MG
- IV edrophonium test - acetylcholinesterase inhibitor, should improve symptoms
- CT chest - ?thymoma
- TFTs - autoimmune thyroid disease often associated with MG
- - -
### Investigations
#### Antibodies
Antibodies are used in the diagnosis but not monitoring of myasthenia gravis:
- Anti-AChR IgG1 - highly specific, may take six months to seroconvert, present in 80% of patients with generalised MG and 50% with ocular MG
- Anti-MuSK - generally tested if anti-AChR is negative in which case present in 1/3rd of cases (generally female), overall prevalence of about 10%
  - Tend to be associated with abnormal patterns of weakness - e.g. neck extensor << flexor, severe oculobulbar weakness
  - Tend to respond more favourable to PLEX rather than IVIG 
  - Tend to respond poorly with worse fasciculations to cholinesterase inhibitors 
- Anti-LRP4 - present in 20% of double-seronegative MG cases
- Anti-striational antibodies - related to thymoma

### Treatment
The goal of treatment is to minimise symptoms while also minimising treatment side effects.

#### IVIG/PLEX
These tend to be rapid-onset treatments used for exacerbations or to facilitate surgery. Chronic use is generally limited to those who cannot tolerate immunosuppressants
|                 | IVIG                                                         | PLEX                                                        |
|-----------------|--------------------------------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------|
| Modality        | 1-2g/kg in divided doses over 2-5 days                       | 5-6 exchanges of 2-3L on alternating days                   |
| Efficacy        | Effective at improving strength in the majority of patients  | Effective at improving strength in the majority of patients |
| Precautions     | Risk of volume overload, risk of thrombus and aseptic meningitis | Unable to use if sepsis or hypotension                      |
| Clinical Points | Improvement in few days-weeks, with effect lasting up to months | Fast onset in myasthenic crisis (after 2-3 exchanges)       |

#### Acetylcholinesterase Inhibitors 
Pyridostigmine increases the bioavailability of acetylcholine at the synaptic cleft. It is typically used at specific times in the day to target symptoms, e.g. 30-60mins before eating. There are no long term side effects, and the short-term side effects are muscarinic in nature. There is a poor response in anti-MuSK+ patients. 

#### Immunosuppression 
Corticosteroids are needed in most cases, and are usually the first choice for symptom control if pyridostigmine is insufficient. Usually the medication is effective within 2-3 weeks. Note that short-term exacerbations can occur with use, particularly if there is bulbar weakness. Prednisolone is generally started at a low dose, and up-titrated to 60-80mg/day, with a slow taper once there is clinical improvement. If symptoms recur at a high-dose of prednisolone, or with tapering, [[steroid-sparing immunosuppressants]] should be considered:
- Azathioprine 
- Mycophenolate mofetil 
- Rituximab - particularly in anti-MuSK+ cases, can be trialled in refractory anti-AChR cases
- Inebilizumab - anti-CD19 therapy

#### Thymectomy 
Indicated if patient has a thymoma. Best if done early. Needs consideration of safe surgery. 

#### Emerging Therapies
Given that anti-AChR antibodies trigger complement, terminal complement inhibitors such as eculizumab and zilucoplan have an emerging role in the treatment of MG. All patients receiving treatment with these agents require vaccination against the meningococcus.

Other treatments include neonatal Fcγ receptor inhibition with:
- Efgartigimod - a recombinant neonatal Fc receptor blocker that prevents the recycling of IgG back into the blood stream, thus blunting the immune response. 
- Rozanolixizumab

### Refractory MG
MG is termed refractory if symptoms are unchanged or worse after:
1. Steroid treatment, and
2. Trial of at least two other immunosuppressants at adequate doses for adequate durations (or stopped because of intolerable side effects)
- - -
### MG and Pregnancy
- ~33% of patients will have an exacerbation - usually T1 or peripartum
- ~5% of babies can be affected by transplacental spread but this is usually self-limiting
