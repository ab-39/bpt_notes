### What is asthma?
Asthma is a disease of the airways characterised by:
1. Variable airflow obstruction
   - This can be difficult to observe!
2. Bronchial hyper-responsiveness 
   - Laboratory confirmation only needed where clinical diagnosis is unclear or exact diagnosis has important implications 
3. Airway inflammation 
   - Exhaled NO or serum eosinophils suggest steroid-responsive disease

[[Pulsus paradoxus]], along with cyanosis, tracheal tug/intercostal recession, a silent chest, and agitation, are key signs of severe asthma. 

The Australian guidelines are available at: https://www.asthmahandbook.org.au.
- - -
### Diagnosis of asthma
Asthma is typically diagnosed on spirometry by demonstrating an obstructive pattern (i.e. FEV1/FVC < 0.7) that is responsive to bronchodilators (>10% or 12%/200mL in old system). 

Re: bronchoprovocation testing:
- _Direct_ chemical bronchoprovocation testing (e.g. with methacholine) is a sensitive test that is useful for ruling _out_ asthma
- _Indirect_ (e.g. exercise, mannitol) bronchoprovocation testing is a specific test that is useful for confirming a diagnosis of asthma 

> [!TIP] Mnemonic 
> If you *direct*ly smoke a *meth* pipe, you can’t have asthma - the *direct* *meth*acholine test rules out asthma
> 
> _SPIN_ - the *sp*ecific test is the *in*direct test - it rules *in* asthma

### Epidemiology
Very common - affecting ~1 in 9 persons in Australia, of whom 3-10% have severe disease.
There are ~400 deaths/yr and ~40,000 admissions per year due to asthma.

Asthma is most prevalent in boys up until the age of 14, but women thereafter.

### Reasons for Poor Asthma Control
- Wrong diagnosis
- Poor inhaler technique
- Poor adherence to ICS preventer
- Undertreatment 
- Smoking
- Comorbidities
- Exposure to trigger factors
- - -
### Risk Factors for Asthma Exacerbations 
Similar to reasons for poor control, and:
- History of being intubated for asthma
- Uncontrolled symptoms
- Exacerbations in the past 12 months
- Elevated FeNO
- Obesity
- Pregnancy

Additionally, risk factors for a fixed airflow obstruction include:
- No ICS treatment
- Smoking/occupational exposure
- Mucus hypersecretion
- Blood eosinophilia
- - -
### Grading Severity
Need to assess the patient’s symptoms in the past month:
- Have they needed a reliever more than twice a week?
- Have they had daytime symptoms more than twice a week?
- Do they wake at night due to asthma?
- Is there activity limitation due to asthma?

None of the above factors indicates good control, while three or four indicate poor control.

### Asthma Variants
- Samter’s triad = asthma + aspirin intolerance + nasal polyps
- [[Allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis]] = chronic asthma + recurrent pulmonary infiltrates + bronchiectasis
  - _Very_ high IgE
  - Evidence of _Aspergillus_ sensitivity
- Churg-Strauss Syndrome (EGPA) = asthma + vasculitis/granulomas + tissue eosinophilia
  - Positive for ANCA ~50% of the time 
  - Severe renal involvement uncommon
  - Cardiac disease most common cause of death

### Management
As per [GINA guidelines](https://ginasthma.org/2024-report/):
1. PRN low-dose ICS-formoterol
   - Few patients (typically with symptoms once-twice a month) can have a SABA reliever alone
2. Low-dose maintenance ICS-formoterol
3. Medium-dose maintenance ICS-formoterol
4. Add-on LAMA 
   - Refer for assessment of phenotype
   - Consider: high-dose maintenance ICS-LABA +/- anti-IgE, anti-IL5/5R, anti-IL4R⍺, anti-TSLP

Add-ons:
| Treatment                            | Situation                                         |
|--------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------|
| Tiotropium                           | Best if long-term airflow obstruction             |
| Macrolides                           | Best if frequent exacerbations, or cough + sputum |
| Montelukast (leukotriene antagonist) | Best if Samter’s triad, or children               |
| Monoclonal ABs                       | See below                                         |

Biologics:
| Molecule                | Target                       | Type                                 | Effect/Notes                                                 |
|-------------------------|------------------------------|--------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------|
| Omalizumab              | IgE                          | Allergic asthma                      | Needs baseline IgE <1300iu/mL (otherwise IgE _too_ high for effect) |
| Mepolizumab, reslizumab | IL-5                         | Severe eosinophilic (type II) asthma | Reduce exacerbations, steroid-sparing                        |
| Benralizumab            | IL-5 receptor                | As above                             | As above                                                     |
| Dupilumab               | IL-4/IL-13 receptor          | Asthma + eczema                      |                                                              |
| Tezepelumab             | Thymic stromal lymphopoietin | Severe asthma                        |                                                              |

> [!WARNING] Anaphylactoid Reaction
> Omalizumab has a ~1/1,000 risk of triggering an anaphylactoid reaction (particularly with the first few doses). Patients should carry an adrenaline autoinjector.

See: [Biologic Therapies for Severe Asthma](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMra2032506)

Long-term oral steroids are largely falling out of fashion. It is critical to assess adherence in patients who fail to improve on standard of care therapy. 

#### Acute Exacerbations
In addition to inhaled salbutamol, additional therapies during an acute exacerbation include:
- Inhaled ipratropium
- IV magnesium sulfate
- IV aminophyllines 
- IV adrenaline (proceed with caution)
