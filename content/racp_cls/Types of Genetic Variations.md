# Types of Genetic Variations
#bpt/genetics #bpt/racp_cls
- - -
### Single Nucleotide Variants
A substitution of a single nucleotide within a gene, typically in exons. They are annotated based on the effect of the variant (e.g. missense, synonymous, etc.), and may effect the normal activity of the gene, increasing the change of developing a genetic disease. 

#### Missense Variants
An SNV that alters the genetic code, resulting in the production of a different amino acid. This does not necessarily affect the function of the protein.

#### Nonsense Variants
An alteration that causes the premature termination of a protein, which can lead to partial or complete inactivation and an attendant change or loss in function. Typically, this process is arrested by _nonsense-mediated mRNA decay_, and so the output from the cell is _no_ protein rather than truncated protein.

#### Frameshift Variants
An insertion or deletion involving a number of base pairs that is not modular 3, leading to a disruption in the triplet reading frame of the DNA sequence, typically leading to creation of a stop codon and premature termination.

#### Splicing Variants
An alteration in the DNA sequence occurring at the boundary between an exon and an intron, resulting in disrupted RNA splicing.
- - -
### Trinucleotide Repeats 
Sequences of three nucleotides repeated in tandem on the same contiguous section of a gene. Within bounds, a certain number of copies is normal and has no clinical significance. After a point, however, they can be pathogenic.
- - -
### Copy Number Variants
Deletions or duplications that change the number of copies of a particular DNA segment within a genome, which may increase/alter protein output and the chances of developing a genetic disease. 
- - -
### Mitochondrial Variants
Generally either single nucleotide variants or copy number variants. Can exist in two state:
- Heteroplasmy - some normal and some mutated mtDNA copies
- Homoplasmy - all mutated mtDNA sopies

Cells will only be affected once the percentage of its mutated mtDNA copies exceeds a certain limit, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction.
- - -
### Imprinting
_Imprinting_ refers to the differential expression of a gene according to the parent of origin (NB. _x_ imprinting means that the chromosome deriving from parent _x_ has been silenced). Imprinting occurs via methylation, which essentially inactivates a section of DNA. The most common is _uniparental disomy_, in which both copies of a given chromosome are inherited from the same parent, and both are silenced (through a process known as trisomy rescue). 

Some loci, however, require an equal genetic contribution from _both_ parents, and if imprinting has occurred here then disease states can arise.

The most well known imprinting syndromes occur due to imprinting at chromosome 15 on the q-arm:
- Paternal imprinting -> Prader-Willi syndrome
  - 75% of cases are caused by deletions of 15q11-13 on the paternal chromosome
  - 25% of cases are caused by uniparental disomy (maternal)
- Maternal imprinting -> Angelman syndrome

On a pedigree, parents can only pass on the _phenotype_ to their children if they (the parent) are of the correct sex (i.e. a father with the allele for Angelman syndrome cannot pass it onto a child, as the condition is caused by maternal imprinting):

### Mosaicism
Variations that arise _after_ the formation of a zygote causes mosaicism - where two of more genetically different cell lines occur in a given individual but derive from a single zygote. This can affect both somatic or germline (gonadal) tissues.
