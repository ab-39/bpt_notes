### Overview
Obesity is a heterogeneous state of metabolic dysfunction that more or less can be defined as having excess fat - often with a BMI of 30.0 being used as a cutoff.

This situation fundamentally arises from an excess of energy. Humans tend to have a fairly predictable breakdown of their _total daily energy expenditure_ (TDEE):
- Resting energy expenditure = basal metabolic rate = ~50-70% of TDEE
- Thermic effect of food = ~10% of TDEE
- Physical activity = ~20-30% of TDEE

A few key things tend to change in obesity:
- There is an increased thermic effect of food
- More energy is required for physical activity as this is proportional to weight
- Energy expended at rest is greater due to more fat and lean mass

### Aetiopathogenesis 
Mice studies would suggest that a) there is a metabolic set point for weight and b) high-fat diets have a persistent effect on weight gain. 

Obesity tends to be polygenic, with _several_ genes implicated in obesity. The ones that confer the greatest risk appear to be:
- _FTO_
- _IRX3_ 

In contrast, monogenic causes of diabetes are often associated with known syndromes (e.g. Prader-Willi syndrome) that cause onset of obesity in childhood, with other associated symptoms (e.g. characteristic facies, intellectual disability, etc.). These tend to be driven by hyperphagia. Other genes that are not associated with syndromes include:
- _MC4R_ (most common)
- _LEP_
- _LEPR_
- _POMC_
- _PCSK1_ 

#### Hormones in Obesity
Several hormones are implicated in weight gain, often as a result of adaptation to weight loss:
- Increased ghrelin
  - An orexigenic hormone released from the oxyntic glands in the stomach with fasting
  - Increases gastric emptying
- Reduced PYY
  - An anorexigenic hormone released by enteroendocrine L-cells in response to food
  - Slows gastric emptying
- Reduced GLP-1
  - An anorexigenic hormone released by enteroendocrine L-cells in response to nutrients in the small intestine
  - Slows gastric emptying and stimulates insulin secretion
- Reduced amylin
- Reduced CCK
  - An anorexigenic hormone released by enteroendocrine L-cells in response to fats and proteins in the duodenum
  - Slows gastric emptying and promotes release of enzymes and bile from the pancreas and gallbladder
- Resistance to leptin
  - Note that leptin is not a satiety signal, but rather its absence is a stimulus of food intake. Supplementing leptin does not appear to treat obesity except in children with leptin deficiency driving their phenotype.

Note that the three main hormones that _stimulate_ appetite are:
- Ghrelin - released from the stomach
- Neuropeptide Y - released from the hypothalamus
- Agouty-related peptide - released from the hypothalamus

> [!TIP] Mnemonic
> Ghrelin, NPY, and ARP stimulate appetite:
> If your stomach starts **growling** (ghrelin) **Y**ou **N**eed (NPY) **A** **R**eal **P**roper **F**eed (ARP)

The _remaining_ hormones - PYY, oxyntomodulin, PP, CCK, GLP-1, insulin, leptin, CART, and ⍺MSH - are all anorexigenic/satiety hormones.
![](Obesity/image%202.png)

In contrast, weight _loss_ is associated with different hormonal changes:
- Ghrelin increases -> stimulates appetite 
  - Note that this increase is attenuated with low-carbohydrate diets
- Satiety hormones decrease
- Resting energy expenditure drops due to loss of fat mass

-> this all leads to _regaining_ weight to try and reach the ‘metabolic set point’ of the body.

Control of intake is through three pathways:
- The homoeostatic -> mediated by the hypothalamus
- The hedonic -> involving the amygdala, nucleus accumbens, and PFC
- The executive -> involving the PFC

Additionally, food and oral intake seem to be intimately related to the circadian rhythms. Sleep restriction additionally exacerbates dysglycaemia.

### Complications 
![](Obesity/image.png)

Particularly significant among these comorbidities are:
- OSA
- LUTS
- Depression
- Hypogonadism
- Pulmonary dysfunction
- Suboptimal cardiac function

#### Benefits of Weight Loss
Even mild reeducction in weight can have significant positive effects on:
- Prevention of diabetes
- Prevention of AF
- Sleep disorders
- LUTS
- Incontinence
- Erectile dysfunciton
- Low testosterone
- Infertility
- Osteoarthritis
- Many cancers

### Management
Lifestyle - avoid processed foods, no sugary drinks, no alcohol, time restricted eating, normalise sleep/wake cycle and sleep hygiene, exercise

Medication - withdraw inappropriate medications that can lead to weight gain where possible:
> Psychotropics
> Antiepileptics (except topiramate)
> Glucocorticoids
> β-Blockers
> Insulin, sulfonylureas, thiazolidinediones
> Depo-provera 

Add helpful medications such as: metformin, GLP-1RAs, phentermine, naltrexone/bupropion, and topiramate
> Remember to reduce the dose of antihyperglycaemics if 	the patient is put on a very restrictive diet

GLP-1RAs (as well as GIP-RAs) such as **_semaglutide_** and **_dulaglutide_** fall in the class of _incretin_ analogues/antagonists. **_Tirzepatide_**, a drug targeting both of these pathways, is the newest medication in this category, with emerging evidence for weight loss and OSA. The risk they run is of reducing lean mass. **_Retatrutide_** additionally blocks glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide, leading to further reductions in weight.

Surgery - Roux-en-Y is the best. Compared to a sleeve gastrectomy, it is associated with improved reductions in morbid obesity, mortality, cancer, diabetes, and coronary artery disease. Only ~10% of the weightloss post bariatric surgery appears to be due to malabsorption, the remainder appears to be neurohormonally-mediated due to the reduction of ghrelin secretion from the (bypassed) stomach, and the early release of satiety hormones due to early entry of foodstuffs into the duodenum.
