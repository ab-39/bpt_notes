# Pre-Eclampsia
#bpt/obstetrics #bpt/racp_cls
- - -
### Overview
Pre-eclampsia is a complex entity which is characterised by reduced placental perfusion, leading to systemic vascular dysfunction with the hallmark feature of hypertension. There is a risk of progression of end-organ damage, affecting the kidneys (proteinuria), brain (cerebral oedema/eclampsia), blood/liver (HELLP), and fetus. By definition, this occurs **after** 20/40 weeks gestation (and thus is most common in the third trimester).
- - -
### Epidemiology and Pathogenesis
Occurs in 2-8% of pregnancies.
Risk factors include:
- Maternal obstetric factors - nulliparity, multi-gestation pregnancy, previous pre-eclampsia, gestational diabetes
- Maternal comorbidities - metabolic syndrome/diabetes, CKD, obesity, [[antiphospholipid syndrome]], [[systemic lupus erythematosus]] 
- Other maternal factors - geriatric pregnancy, IVF

Pathophysiolgically, it would appear that _anti-angiogenic_ factors - particularly sFLT1 (an soluble VEGF receptor -> functional inhibition of VEGF) - play a key role in pre-eclampsia. sFLT1 is secreted by trophoblasts following placental hypoxia, and contributes to endothelial dysfunction. This mechanism is supported by the fact that the administration of bevacizumab can lead to a pre-eclampsia like syndrome. Importantly, sFLT1 levels _predate_ the onset of sympdoms, which means that the sFLT/PIGF ratio can be used to determine the patient’s risk of pre-eclampsia. 

The most important clinical variable in determining outcome is _gestational age_, leading to a twofold classification:
- Late onset = >34/40 -> most common (>80%)
  - Probably a demand ischaemia picture combined with a maternal predisposition to inflammation
- Early onset = <34/40 -> higher mortality and morbidity, greater association with FGR
  - Probably related to impaired placentation

### Severe Features of PE
- Refractory severe HTN
- Platelet count <100 **or** DIC
- Transaminitis ≥2x ULN **or** severe persistent RUQ pain
- AKI (def. Cr >90µmol/L or oliguria)
- Pulmonary oedema
- Headache unrelieved by medication or persistent visual disturbance
- Eclampsia or PRES 

One key severe manifestation of pre-eclampsia is _HELLP_ syndrome:
- _HE_ molysis
- _L_ FT derangement
- _L_ ow _P_ latelet count 
- - -
### Renal Manifestations of Pre-Eclampsia
The pathognomic renal lesion in pre-eclampsia is _glomeruloendotheliosis_ - acute atherosis of the kidney. This is the lesion that leads to the characteristic proteinuria of pre-eclampsia, defined as:
> Urinary protein >300mg/d
> or
> uPCR >30

**But** gestational proteinuria alone with normotension does not necessarily herald a poor outcome. It is important to follow these patients up and ensure that there is no other underlying renal disease.

This is associated with tubular dysfunction and hyperuricaemia. 

It is important to remember that among the [[physiological changes of pregnancy]] is a reduction in the serum creatinine, so a lower threshold of creatinine (usu. 70µmol/L) is taken as the threshold for an AKI. 
- - -
 ### Haematological Manifestations of Pre-Eclampsia
Pre-eclampsia is associated with _thrombocytopenia_ and microangiopathic _haemolysis_ (although the latter is relatively rare). Although a reduced platelet count is physiological in pregancy, a reduction below 100 is suspicious for pre-eclampsia. 
- - -
### Hepatic Manifestations of Pre-Eclampsia
Liver damage in pre-eclampsia is characterised by ischaemia and microhaemorrhages, associated with transaminitis.
- - -
### Cerebral Manifestations
The cerebral signs of pre-eclampsia are caused by oedema, and are often heralded by headaches and visual disturbance. Without treatment, this can progress to ischaemia, systemic UMN signs (hyperreflexia and clonus), and ultimately eclampsia and stroke.
- - -
### Fetal Manifestations
- IUGR
- Placental abruption
- Stillbirth

### Prophylaxis
Predictive factors for pre-eclampsia include:
- Anti-angiogenic factors
- Serum levels of PlGF and PAPP-A
  - This has been implemented as a screening tool as the ratio of sFLT1/PlGF ratio, which precedes the onset of PET by ~6/52  with a _very_ high NPV 
  - This should _not_ be used to diagnose
  - The cut off for the sFLT1/PlGF ratio is 38
- Maternal risk factors - e.g. metabolic syndrome, T2DM, etc.
  - These can be combined with the above biomarkers to form the FMF score for predicting pre-eclampsia at 11-13/40 gestation
- Mean arterial pressire
- Mean uterine artery pulsatility index

Patients with multiple risk factors or previous pre-eclampsia can be treated with aspirin as prophylaxis, whcih reduces their risk of progression to pre-eclampsia. Treatment is generally initiated between 12 and 16/40. Exercise and calcium are also protective. There is emerging evidence, also, for pravastatin, metformin, and PPIs. Risk factors include:
- History of pre-eclamspia
- T1 or T2 DM 
- Chronic hypertension
- Multifetal gestation
- Kidney disease
- Autoimmune disease with potential vascular complications

([Aspirin versus Placebo in Pregnancies at High Risk for Preterm Preeclampsia](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1704559) - in women with singleton pregnancies at high risk for preterm preeclampsia, the intake of low-dose aspirin at a dose of 150 mg per day, started from 11 to 14 weeks of gestation until 36 weeks of gestation, significantly reduced the incidence of preterm preeclampsia.)

Preventive measures:
- Aspirin
- Calcium
- Vitamin D (if deficient)

### Management 
The only definitive treatment for pre-eclampsia is to deliver the placenta., whcih is indicated if there is:
- Progressive evidence of maternal organ dysfunction, e.g.: 
  - Neurological features
  - Thrombocytopenia (plts <50)
  - Abnormal/worsening liver enzymes 
  - Abnormal/worsening sCr
  - Pulmonary oedema
- Inability to control BP despite use of ≥3 antihypertensives
- Inadequate fetal growth or non-reassuring fetal status
- Reversed end-diastolic flow in uterine aretery Doppler velocimetry
- Gestational age beyond 37/40

Note that antenatal corticosteroids for fetal lung maturation should be given to all women at risk of delivery <34/40.

Where safe, to delay this to an optimal time for the fetus, we can administer judicious treatment to stabliise the maternal condition:
- Control BP:
  - ⍺-Methyldopa
  - Labetalol - PO or IV 
  - Nifedipine
  - Hydralazine
  - **Avoid** diuretics and ACEi 
- Stabilise fluid status - small amounts of fluid in tandem with 
- Correct coagulopathy and thrombocytopenia 

Ergometrine (used for PPH) contraindicated due to HTN, increase risk of bleed which is the major cause of M/M in PE. 

Novel therapies under research include:
- Monoclonal antibodies targeting the complement system
- Pravastatin and its role affecting angiogenesis
- Metformin
- PPIs
- Plasmapheresis
- Gene silencing targeting sFLT1 or angiotensinogen

### Long Term Consequences
- 2-3x risk of IHD/stroke
- 4x risk of CKD
- HTN
- Metabolic dysfunction
