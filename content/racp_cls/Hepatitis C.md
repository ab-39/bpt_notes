### What is hepatitis C?
Hepatitis C is a blood-borne RNA virus that commonly causes chronic liver infection. The infection can remain clinically asymptomatic for decades until severe complications, such as cirrhosis or HCC, emerge. 
- - -
### Virology
The hepatitis C virus is a single-strand, positive sense RNA Flavivirus comprising 9.4kbp that encodes a large polyprotein of about 3000 amino acids. HCV is characterised by a diversity due to copying errors that makes vaccine development difficult. Hepatitis C produces a core antigen, against which the body produces an antibody (anti-HCV). There are seven distinct genotypes of HCV, of which genotypes 1 and 3 are most common in Australia. 
- - -
### Natural History 
Of every 100 people who contract HCV, typically 25 are able to clear the virus within 2-6 months. Of the remainder, 20 will not develop hepatic complications or symptoms, while the remaining 55 will go on to develop long-term complications. Typically, 20% of patients with untreated HCV will go on to develop [[cirrhosis]], and 5% will go on to develop [[hepatocellular carcinoma]]. 

#### Extrahepatic Manifestations
Extrahepatic manifestations of HCV include:
- Kidney - membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis
- Skin - porphyria cutanea tarda 
  - Decreased actvitiy of uroporphyrinogen decarboxylase leading to skin changes in sun-exposed areas and elevated urine uroporphyrin levels
  - Typical signs of scaling, erythema, atrophy
  - ![](Hepatitis%20C/image.png)
- Vascular - cryoglobulinaemia 
  - Cryoglobulins = immunoglobulins in the serum that precipitate at temperatures <37ºC
  - Sx = arthralgia, purpura, skin ulcers (>90% of patients), glomerulonephritis, and peripheral neuropathy
  - RFs = HCV, autoimmune disease, MGUS/MM
  - Types:
    1. = monoclonal IgG = IgM > IgA, strongly associated with MM/CLL, often present as a hyperviscosity syndrome
    2. = monoclonal IgM + RF + polyclonal Ig
    3. = polyclonal (with RF)

### Epidemiology
An estimated 120,000 Australians live with HCV.
HCV is particularly common in Mongolia. 

Patients at particular risk of HCV include:
- People who inject drugs
- ATSI peoples
- Migrants from high prevalence regions - Pakistan, Egypt, Africa, etc. 
- Prisoners 
- Prostitutes
- People who received blood transfusions or organ transplants prior to 1990
- Babies born to infected mothers 
- People who have had needlestick injuries

### Diagnosis 
Diagnosis of HCV is with serology:
- Anti-HCV -> indicates prior exposure to the virus, even if it has been cured
- HCV RNA viral load -> indicates current infection (test if anti-HCV positive)

Note that patients may present with a false-negative anti-HCV if particularly immunosuppressed. Patients at high risk can be screened annually with HCV RNA. 

On diagnosis, it is important to then go on to test:
- HCV genotype - particularly in complex patients 
- Other blood-borne viruses - HBV, HIV, HAV
- Routine bloods - FBE, UEC, LFT, INR
- Cirrhosis/HCC screening - ARFI/TLE, ultrasound, ⍺FP

HCV is a notable illness in Australia.

### Management
In 2025, hepatitis C is a curable disease. Importantly, this is a _pangenotypic_ cure. 

The typical regimen in Australia is with oral combination drugs for a duration of 8-12 weeks. This treatment has a high success rate, with a typical sustained virological response >95% in patients without cirrhosis. The new generation direct acting antivirals are also very well tolerated, with ADRs typically being limited to nausea, headache, and fatigue (with the exception of ribovarin which anaemia, rash, and insomnia). There is also a risk of drug interactions and viral resistance. IF there is ongoing risk-taking behaviour after cure, it is worthwhile screening annually with HCV PCR.  

#### Who gets treatment?
Almost all patients with HCV should be considered for treatment, with the exception of those whose lifespans are limited by other comorbidities. Most non-cirrhotic patients can be managed in the primary care setting, while patients with cirrhosis should be referred to a specialist. 

Therapy for HCV aims to completely eradicate the virus leading to a permanent cure. This is defined by a sustained virological response - a negative PCR for HCV RNA 12 weeks after completion of therapy. 

#### Side Effects of Treatment
There is a small risk of reactivation of hepatitis B during treatment for hepatitis C:
- HBsAg + -> check PCR at baseline and treat if indicated
- HBcAb + -> check LFT in weeks 4-8 and then check PCR if elevated

Other key counselling points include:
- Avoidance of pregnancy - limited safety data
- Drug interactions - seem to be mediated through CyP450
  - PPIs
  - Statins
  - Amiodarone
  - [[Antiepileptic drugs]] 
- Vaccination opportunities
- Screening for cirrhosis 

#### Resistance-associated substitutions 
Some polymorphisms that naturally arise during the HCV lifecycle can confer resistance to treatment. Typically, this determines if a patient will fail NS3 or NS5A based therapy. The most common and clinically significant of these mutations is _Y93H_, which is associated with a decreased response to most regimens. The combination drug Vosevi does tend to work in these patients. 

#### Specific populations
- Injecting drug users
- Patients with CKD
  - New combinations are relatively safe 
- Patients with decompensated cirrhosis 
  - Protease inhibitors are relatively contraindicated (i.e. ‘-previr’ drugs) due to risk of liver failure 
  - Most challenging genotype of HCV to treat is genotype 3
  - Need to consider for referral for transplant
