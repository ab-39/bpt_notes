### Macroanatomy
The kidney is broadly composed of two things:
- The cortex, in which the glomeruli are located
- The medulla, through which the tubules traverse 
![](Renal%20Physiology/image%202.png)

### The Glomerulus 
The glomerulus essentially consists of the structures in the renal cortex through which the capillary beds of the renal arterioles course. This means that between the blood and the renal extracellular matrix, there are essentially three key layers:
1. The capillary endothelial cells
   - Large gaps - 50-100nm
2. The glomerular basement membrane
   - Type IV collagen 
   - Highly electronegative
3. The foot processes of the renal podocytes  
   - 30-40nm gaps that are crossed by the slit diaphragm to ~8nm

As such, the glomerulus poses a formidable filtration barrier to large or negatively charged proteins (e.g. such as albumin, which is 7.2nm in size, but highly negatively charged).  
![](Renal%20Physiology/image.png)

### Renal Function 
The glomeruli filter approximately 125mL/min in a healthy adult, equating to almost 180L daily!  Of this, almost 99% is reabsorbed in the proximal and distal convoluted tubules in a passive process that follows the movement of solutes (primarily sodium - cells are electronegative, and thus have a favourable gradient for the reabsorption of sodium). Only ~5% of water is reabsorbed in a water-independent fashion through aquaporin 2 receptors (AQP2) in the collecting duct, after stimulation by vasopressin/antidiuretic hormone (AVP/ADH).

### The Movement of Sodium
Sodium is reabsorbed easily by tubular cells due to the inherent electronegativity of cells.   This is preserved by the activity of the basolateral **Na+/K+-ATPase** which extrudes 3 Na+ to absorb 2 K+ from the blood. This leads to a relative intracellular deficiency of Na+ (~10mmol/L) and K+ excess (~150mmol/L). 

This leads to a propensity of the sodium-deficient tubular epithelial cells to reclaim sodium from the tubular lumen, with water following as a consequence.  

In the collecting duct, there is additional reabsorption of sodium under the influence of aldosterone, although this accounts for only 1-2% of sodium movement in total.

### The Proximal Convoluted Tubule
In the proximal convoluted tubule, sodium is taken into cells from the urine in exchange for hydrogen ions:
![](Renal%20Physiology/image%207.png)
Homeostasis of the hydrogen ions is largely maintained through the action of carbonic anhydrase:
- H+ and HCO3- in the urine combine to form carbonic acid
- Carbonic anhydrase catalyses the conversion of carbonic acid into water and CO2
- CO2 is able to diffuse across the membrane into the tubular cells
- Within the tubular cells, carbonic anhydrase again catalyses the conversion of water and CO2 into carbonic acid
- Carbonic acid then dissociates into HCO3- and H+
  - HCO3- enters the bloodstream
  - H+ returns to step one 

Failure of carbonic anhydrase, consequently, leads to wasting of HCO3- in the urine, and a _proximal or type II [[renal tubular acidosis]]_. HCO3- loss “takes” potassium with it, leading to a hypokalaemia (which is also seen with acetazolamide use). This is also the mechanism behind hypokalaemia with vomiting (where loss of HCl from the GIT leads to increased urinary losses of HCO3-).

### The Loop of Henle
While some water is reabsorbed in the thin descending limb of the Loop of Henle, the thick ascending limb is largely impermeable to fluid shifts. 
![](Renal%20Physiology/image%206.png)
Instead, the thick ascending limb is a major site of solute reabsorption, largely thanks to the action of the Na+/K+/2Cl- co-transporter (NKCC2) on the luminal side, and the sodium-potassium ATPase on the interstitial side.  In addition to this, the electrochemical gradient between the interstitial fluid and the tubular effluent allows for the paracellular diffusion of cations (Na+, K+, Ca2+, Mg2+) into the interstitial fluid. This creates the concentration gradient critical to the functioning of the countercurrent.

What happens to these ions in the tubular cells?
- Na+: Na+ is quickly shifted into the interstitial fluid under the action of the Na+/K+ ATPase
- K+: the cell is replete with potassium, so it quickly diffuses out via specific channels (ROMK), largely into the urine but also partially into the interstitium
  - Note that this also maintains the electrochemical gradient required for paracellular diffusion 
  - Note that this process is _inhibited_ by magnesium, so hypomagnesaemia will lead to increased urinary potassium loss and hypokalaemia
- Cl-: Cl- is able to diffuse into the interstitium via specific channels (ClCkb and Barttin)

Congenital deficiencies in NKCC2 leads to _type I Bartter syndrome_, which displays the same biochemical derangements as being on a loop diuretic: hypokalaemia and a contraction alkalosis, with a low to normal blood pressure.
> Note that there are four _other_ types of Bartter syndrome depending on the channelopathy involved:
> - ROMK -> type II
> - ClCkb -> type III
> - Barttin -> type IV (with sensorineural deafness)
> - CaSR (increased) -> type V/Bartter like syndrome with autosomal dominant hypocalcaemia

 ### The Calcium Sensing Receptor 
The basolateral (i.e. interstitial) side of the thick ascending limb of the loop of Henle and the parathyroid glands are the only areas of the body in which the calcium-sensing receptor (CaSR) is found. 

In the loop of Henle, the CaSR acts to:
- Inhibit K+ channels in the luminal membrane and Na+/K+ ATPase in the basolateral membrane to decrease urinary K+ -> thus, reducing the electrochemical gradient favourable for Ca2+ absorption
- Stimulate claudrin 14 -> inhibit claudrin 16 and 19, key transport proteins in the movement of Ca2+ through the paracellular area
![](Renal%20Physiology/image%208.png)

So, we can see that the overall effect of CaSR stimulation by serum Ca2+ is the inhibition of paracellular Ca2+ and Mg2+ reabsorption (with concomitant increased urinary losses). 

Genetic deficiencies of CaSR lead to reduced loss of Ca2+ in the urine and an increase in serum Ca2+, a benign,   autosomal dominant condition known as _familial hypocalciuric hypercalcaemia_ (urinary Ca:Cr ratio <0.01) . Note that as CaSR is also expressed in the parathyroid glands, this condition is a form of hypercalcaemia associated with an _increased PTH_, unlike most non-parathyroid-related causes of hypercalcaemia. Acquired deficiency of this receptor can occur with prolonged lithium exposure.

### The Juxtaglomerular Apparatus
In the glomerulus, just as the loop of Henle is becoming the early part of the DCT, there is a region of the tubule that is located close to both the afferent and efferent arterioles. These cells are, due to their closely packed nature, referred to as the _macula densa_, which together with the juxtaglomerular cells (also: granular cells) make up the juxtaglomerular apparatus:
![](Renal%20Physiology/image%2012.png)<!-- {"width":278} -->
When there is low effective circulating volume, the macula densa cells absorb relatively less sodium. In this situation, the juxtaglomerular apparatus increase the production of renin through cleavage of pro-renin, thereby activating [[the renin-angiotensin system]]. The juxtaglomerular apparatus is also home to the cells that secrete erythropoietin.

### The Distal Convoluted Tubule 
In the distal convoluted tubule, sodium is reabsorbed through the action of the apical sodium-chloride co-transporter (NCCT):
![](Renal%20Physiology/image%209.png)

Genetic deficiencies of NCCT lead to _Gitelman syndrome_, which displays the same biochemical derangements as being on thiazide diuretics: hypokalaemia, metabolic alkalosis, low blood pressure, and (due to overactivation of the thick ascending limb of the loop of Henle) hypocalciuric hypercalcaemia. 

![](Renal%20Physiology/image%2010.png)
Additionally, we also observe dysfunction of the neighbouring _TRPM6_ channel, leading to urinary losses of magnesium and subsequent hypomagnesaemia, contrary to what we would expect given there is hypercalcaemia.

### The Collecting Duct
Principal cells in the collecting duct express aquaporin 2 (AQP2), a channel that is responsible for the reabsorption of water through the otherwise impermeable luminal membrane. Translocation of AQP2 channels from the cytosol to the apical membrane occurs due to AVP/ADH acting at [[vasopressin receptor 2]].
![](Renal%20Physiology/image%2011.png)<!-- {"width":278} -->
Genetic deficiencies in vasopressin, V2 receptors, or AQP2 genes lead to the group of conditions known as _diabetes insipidus_. Deficiencies of vasopressin production lead to central DI, while deficiencies in V2 or AQP2 lead to nephrogenic DI. This can occur in an acquired fashion due to lithium. 

The other key hormone acting at the collecting duct is _aldosterone_, the final player in the RAAS. As a steroid hormone, aldosterone acts on intracellular receptors, with several key effects:
- Increased action of the 3Na+/2K+-ATPase on the basolateral membrane -> sodium transfer into the blood + potassium absorption into the cell
- Increased action of the apical epithelial Na+ channels (ENaC) -> sodium reabsorption
- Increased action of the apical K+ channels -> potassium wasting in the urine -> hypokalaemia

In addition to the principal cells, the collecting duct is also made up of ~10% intercalated cells, which come in two types:
- Type A -> rich in hydrogen ions (type A = acidic)
- Type B -> rich in HCO3- (type B = basic)
The effect of aldosterone leads to more Na+ reabsorption than K+ secretion, leading to an electrochemical gradient that favours the secretion of H+ ions from the type A intercalated cells into the urine, causing a metabolic alkalosis.
