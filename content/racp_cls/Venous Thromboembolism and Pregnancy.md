# Venous Thromboembolism and Pregnancy
#bpt/obstetrics #bpt/racp_cls
- - -
### Overview
Although the risk of VTE is elevated throughout pregnancy, the highest risk is actually in the first six weeks postpartum. This is related to derangements in all of the elements of Virchow’s triad:
- Hypercoagulability related to alterations in levels of clotting factors
- Endothelial injury - at delivery or through pre-eclampsia
- Stasis - increased venous capacitance and intraabdominal venous compression 

The risk of VTE is elevated by all increased exposure to hormones, but pregnancy and the puerperium are the highest risk periods by far, with the VTE risk per 100,000woman-years being 3-30x times that associated with the newer generation combined oral contraceptives, and as high as 200x a healthy woman of childbearing age. 

The d-dimer is of limited utility in pregnancy. Although some women (~40%) in the first trimester will have a normal test, this number drops off as the pregnancy advances. Instead, the pregnancy-modified YEARS score is recommended to evaulate/rule out VTE. 

> [!NOTE] Maternal Code Blue
> In the event of a massive PE causing cardiorespiratory arrest, the code blue should begin with manual left uterine displacement to allow for adequate venous return. 

#### Choice of Imaging 
The fetal radiation dose is relatively low with either a CTPA or a V/Q scan (<1mGy), but the maternal breast dose of ratiation is ~50x higher with a CTPA than a V/Q scan. That being said, technical features can make a CTPA more difficult in pregnancy compared to non-pregnant women. Overall, CTPA is preferred if:
- Other lung pathology needs to be ruled out
- The patient is haemodynamically unstable
- V/Q is not easily available
Otherwise, V/Q is the preferred modality. 

#### Treatment
Low-molecular weight heparin for 3-6/12 (plus 6/52 prophylaxis postpartum) is the general cause. Warfarin is teratogenic in pregnancy, but can be used while breastfeeding. DOACs are not able to be used in either scenario.
