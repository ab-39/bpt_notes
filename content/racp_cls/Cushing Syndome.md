### Overview
Cushing _syndrome_ refers to the situation of cortisol excess, while Cushing _disease_ refers to when this arises due to an ACTH-secreting pituitary adenoma, which makes up about 70% of Cushing syndrome. The remainder is attributable to ectopic ACTH secretion (usu. SCLC, ~10%), primary causes in the adrenals (e.g. adenoma, ~20%), and exogenous steroids. An important differential is pseudo-Cushing syndrome (e.g. in depression, or with alcohol use).

The classical clinical picture of Cushing syndrome is of the patient with a moon face, buffalo hump, and central adiposity. In addition to this, there is often proximal muscle weakness and violaceous striae on the abdomen. 

Complications include hypertension, diabetes, osteoporosis, cardiovascular disease, and (often fungal) infections. 

Some patients can have _mild autonomous cortisol secretion_ - essentially a mild form of Cushings with an abnormal DST. Other differentials include the _pseudocushing_ states that can be induced by situations such as alcoholism, obesity, OSA, pregnancy, poorly controlled diabetes, or advanced CKD. 

The best utility in testing comes when there is a high pre-test probability for steroid excess. The following tests help us to establish if Cushing syndrome is present:
- 1mg dexamethasone suppression test - failure to suppress cortisol to less than 50nmol/L suggests that Cushing syndrome is, in fact, present (it does not differentiate between Cushing syndrome and Cushing disease - that would require a _high dose_ 8mg dexamethasone suppression test)
  - +: high NPV, useful for adrenal adenomas, useful in shift workers
  - -: false positives in cases of malabsorption, coadministration of CYP3A4 inhibitors (e.g. carbamazepine) or increased levels of CBG (e.g. OCP, pregnancy, chronic hepatitis)
- Late night salivary cortisol
  - +: most sensitive test for _recurrence_ of Cushing syndrome
  - -: not useful in shift workers or those with adrenal adenomas false positive if using topical hydrocortisone
- 24h urinary cortisol
  - +: results are independent of CBG levels (e.g. pregnancy); useful for cyclical Cushings
  - -: high individual variation requires multiple specimens, unreliable in renal impairment

Further tests in the cases of initially equivocal results can include the dexamethasone-CRH test (serial dexamethasone followed by a CRH level), and the DDAVP test (tests for Cushing disease, where tumours expressed V1b receptors -> increase in ACTH post DDAVP). 

Once this diagnosis is made, we then need to decide if the cortisol excess is ACTH dependent or independent:
- ACTH low = independent
  - Proceed to imaging the adrenal glands - most likely diagnosis is an adrenal adenoma
- ACTH high (or normal) = dependent - i.e. Cushing disease or ectopic ACTH 
  - Localisation of the disease is more difficult
  - Start with a pituitary MRI, but this can be challenging as:
    - Adenomas can be tiny (to the point of being invisible on MRI)
    - 10-20% of the general population can have small, nonfunctioning pituitary lesions
  - If there is nothing on the MRI, the best test is inferior petrosal sinus sampling 
    - Test the central:peripheral ACTH ratio - if >2 at baseline or >3 with CRH administration
  - If there is a macroadenoma, we can proceed to a high-dose dexamethasone suppression test to avoid IPSS
  - If IPSS is suggestive of ectopic ACTH, the most likely cause is a chest neuroendocrine tumour (e.g. SCLC) -> proceed to imaging of the chest 

> [!NOTE] Cushing Syndrome in Pregnancy
> Although vanishingly rare (there are 260 case reports of Cushing syndrome in pregnancy, as per UpToDate) the prevalence pattern is _reversed_ for Cushing syndrome in pregnancy: ~60% of pregnant women had ACTH-independent Cushing syndrome due to adrenal adenomas. 

Treatment:
- First line = transsphenoidal surgery
- Second line - e.g. for recurrent or persistent disease:
  - Repeat transsphenoidal surgery
  - Bilateral adrenalectomy -> Nelson syndrome (uncontrolled growth of ACTH adenoma)
  - Radiotherapy - takes months+ to establish control
  - Inhibit pituitary adenoma -> cabergoline, pasireotide 
  - Inhibit adrenal steroidogenesis -> ketoconazole (blocks CYP11A1 and CYP17A1, liver dysfunction, interacts with many drugs), metyrapone (mineralocorticoid shunting) - both ~50% effective
  - Osilodrostat -> quicker version of metyrapone, 75% effective, same mineralocorticoid effects
