# Psoriatic Arthritis
#bpt/rheumatology #bpt/racp_cls
- - -
### Overview
Psoriatic arthritis (PsA) is a seronegative, inflammatory arthropathy associated with the [[Psoriasis|eponymous skin disease]]. There are five recognised patterns of involvement:
1. Asymmetric mono/oligoarthritis (most common)
   - ’Rays not rows’ - unlike rheumatoid arthritis, PsA often affects several joints on one digit rather than adjacent joints
2. Symmetric polyarthritis
3. Axial spondyloarthritis - uncommon as the first presentation, but often develops later in the disease course
4. Distal interphalangeal arthritis with nail involvement
5. Arthritis obliterans (rare)
- - -
### Diagnosis 
CASPAR criteria define PsA as established inflammatory articular disease (arthritis/enthesitis), plus three points from:
- Skin psoriasis (2)
- Past or family history of psoriasis (1)
- Dactylitis - note that this means inflammation of the _whole_ finger - both tendons and joints (‘sausage fingers’) (1)
- Nail changes - pitting or onycholysis (1)
- Negative [[rheumatoid factor]] (1)
- New, juxta-articular bone formation on XR (1)
- - -
### Epidemiology
Approximately 3% of the population has psoriasis, and of them, ~15% will develop PsA. Men and women affected equally. Skin changes tend to precede joint disease. Around half of patients will be HLA-B27 positive. 

There are associations with heart disease, depression, metabolic syndrome, and fatty liver disease. 
- - -
### Pathogenesis
Like [[axial spondyloarthritis]], the pathophysiology of PsA is related to Th17 cells, IL-23, IL-17, TNF⍺, and teh JAK/STAT signalling pathway.  
- - -
### Investigations
Bloods:
- Acute phase reactants are occasionally elevated
- RF and CCP are typically negative

Plain Film:
- Erosion + new bone = ‘pencil in cup’ deformity
- Can get ankylosis of the hands 
- - -
### Management
Like other arthritides, NSAIDs provide effective symptom relief.

Systemic glucocorticoids are generally not recommended, although intra-articular injections may be used.

#### Conventional DMARDs
Relatively little evidence for MTX/SSZ/LEF. Importantly, these are _not_ effective if the patient’s main presentation is with [[axial spondyloarthritis]]. If the disease is peripheral only, a trial of cDMARDs may be warranted.

EULAR 2023 guidelines suggest an early trial of cDMARDs if mono/oligoarthritis occurs with any of:
- Elevated inflammatory markers
- Dactylitis
- Nail involvement
- Structural damage

#### Biologics + New DMARDs
In descending order of recommendation by ACR/NPF guidelines due to safety considerations:
- Anti-TNF -> adalimumab, certolizumab, etanercept, golimumab, infliximab
  - Note: etanercept is _not_ useful for uveitis or IBD
  - Generally the best - ACR/NPF guidelines recommends as the first line over cDMARDs and other biologics/SMIs
  - IFX: evidence for improvement in radiographic progression
- Anti-IL-17 -> secukinumab, ixekizumab
  - Note: _may_ make IBD worse, not useful in uveitis
- Anti-IL23/12 (p40) -> ustekinumab
  - Note: ineffective for [[axial spondyloarthritis]] and uveitis 
- Anti-IL23 (p19) -> guselkumab
- JAK1/3 inhibitor -> tofacitinib
- JAK1 inhibitor -> upadacitinib 
  - Note: ineffective for nail disease, no evidence in uveitis
- PDEIV inhibitor -> apremilast (not on PBS)
