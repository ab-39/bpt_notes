# Thrombolytic Therapy in Ischaemic Stroke
#bpt/neurology #bpt/racp_cls
- - -
### Who gets alteplase in [[ischaemic stroke]]?

Indications for tPA administration:
- Potentially disabling deficit **and**
- Last known well within 4.5h **or**
- Last known well within 4.5-9h/midpoint of sleep within 4.5-9h with favourable [[CT perfusion scan]] findings

Contraindications include:
- Haemorrhage on CT-B
- Extensive hypodensity on CT-B - concerning for subacute infarct -> risk of haemorrhagic transformation 
- Active, non-compressible systemic bleeding - risk v. benefit
- BP > 185/105mmHg or BSL < 2.7mmol/L - fix then treat 
- Other: IE, aortic dissection, malignant brain tumour, INR > 1.7, platelets < 100, DOAC < 48h unless reversible* or low-anti-Xa level
- - -
### Effects
Good:
- ~1/3rd of patients will have an improvement in functional status by three months

Bad:
- ~3% of patients will be worse off or dead

Meh:
- The vast majority of patients will have no benefit or harm from treatment
- This can be because they have no penumbra left to save, or because they had already ‘auto-reperfused’
- Importantly, alteplase also isn’t perfect at reopening arteries

### Risks of Thrombolytic Therapy
In addition to the obvious risk of bleeding, it is important to be aware of the risk of precipitating haemorrhagic transformation of the ischaemic stroke. This is due to a compromised blood-brain barrier in the affected area due to the underlying stroke. It should be noted that haemorrhagic transformation is almost universal with ischaemic stroke and does not always portend an unfavourable outcome, but carries a risk of mass effect. 

Orolingual angioedema:
- Occurs on contralateral side to stroke
- Considered to be bradykinin mediated
- Usually does not require intubation
- Traditional treatment is hydrocortisone
