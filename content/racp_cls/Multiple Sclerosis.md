### What is multiple sclerosis?
Multiple sclerosis is an autoimmune demyelinating disease of the central nervous system, defined in terms of dissemination in time and dissemination in space. 
- - -
### Pathophysiology
While this is a complex field, it appears that the key driver of inflammation in MS is the CD4+ T-cell, which drives an inflammatory response against the proteins within the myelin sheath of neurone in the CNS. These lymphocytes then recruit innate and CD8+ cells, leading to a destruction of the myelin sheath.

Attacks typically recur over time, and can resolve into either:
- Remyelination - incomplete
- Axonal death
- Redistribution of ion channels to enable some degree of signal transmission with concomitant mitochondrial stress

This leads to the key phenotypes of MS, the latter two of which tend to involve more neurodegeneration and redistribution of ion channels:
- Relapsing-remitting MS
- Primary progressive MS
- Secondary progressive MS

### Diagnostic Criteria 
The two ‘precursors’ of MS are the _isolated syndromes_:
- Clinically Isolated Syndrome = a single clinical event suggestive of demyelination
  - At t+20y, 80% of CIS patients with MRI lesions will convert to MS, compared to 20% with a normal MRI
  - The presence of oligoclonal bands in the CSF is another predictor of increased risk
  - Early treatment of CIS with DMT (IFN-β) will increase the time to conversion, but not change disability profile (_BENEFIT_ trial) -> tend to monitor CIS patients rather than treat
- Radiologically Isolated Syndrome = asymptomatic radiological lesions
  - RIS patients who are younger, have lesions in the spinal cord, or lesions that enhance with gadolinium have a 90% t+2y risk of developing MS, in contrast to 11% of patients without these factors
  - Early treatment of RIS patients with teriflunomide reduces the risk of MS by 76% at 96w (_TERIS_ trial)

#### McDonald Criteria
| Clinical Presentation                                        | Additional Information Needed for MS Diagnosis               |
|--------------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------|
| ≥2 attacks with<br>objective evidence of ≥2 lesions **or**<br>objective evidence of 1 lesion and a suggestive history | None                                                         |
| ≥2 attacks with objective evidence of 1 lesion               | Dissemination in space:<br>Radiological evidence of ≥1 lesion in an MS-typical region **or** await another clinical attack |
| 1 attack with objective evidence of ≥2 lesions               | Dissemination in time:<br>Simultaneous presence of enhancing and non-enhancing lesions at any time **or** await another clinical attack |
| CIS                                                          | DIS and DIT                                                  |
| Insidious neurological progression suggestive of MS          | 1 year of disease progression with 2/3 of:<br>1. Evidence of DIS in the brain<br>2. Evidence of DIS in the spinal cord<br>3. OCB in the CSF (evidence of DIT) |

What are the MS-typical regions of the CNS?
- Periventricular
- Juxtacortical
- Infratentorial - note that a high burden of infratentorial lesions are associated with a worse prognosis 
- Pericallosal 
- Spinal cord 

What are oligoclonal bands?
- IgG molecules found in the CSF, so named for their appearance on the Western blot
- Need to be found in the CSF _and not_ in the serum to be suggestive of MS
- New studies suggest that CSF κ-free light chains may be a promising test

### Epidemiology
MS is a relatively common condition, particularly in Australia, affecting roughly 60/100,000 persons. In general, MS tends to affect people at latitudes farther from the equator. Key risk factors include:
- Vitamin D level and sun exposure (although supplemental vitamin D does not appear to be protective)
- EBV seropositivity (OR 4.5) - likely a B-cell mediated effect 
- Smoking status
- Female sex
- Obesity 
- Genetic factors - HLA-DRB1, polymorphisms in the IL-2R

### Treatment of MS
[[Disease modifying therapy in MS]] tends to be classed based on efficacy and frequency of treatment:
| Efficacy                         | Continuous                                         | Pulse (with intercurrent monitoring) |
|----------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------|
| Low <br>(~30% risk reduction)    | Interferon<br>Glatiramer acetate<br>Teriflunomide  |                                      |
| Medium <br>(~50% risk reduction) | S1PR (fingolimod)<br>Dimethylfumarate              | Cladribine                           |
| High<br>(~70% risk reduction)    | Natalizumab<br>B-cell therapies (e.g. ocrelizumab) | Alemtuzumab<br>HSCT                  |
Early treatment with high-efficacy therapies reduces the long-term risk of disability progression.

### [[Allogeneic Stem Cell Transplant]] in MS
The theory behind HSCT is that eradication of the pathogenic immune repertoire, and its replacement with a new environment should reduce the activity of the disease. Incredibly, meta-analytic evidence has found this to be incredibly effective with a very low relapse rate compared to almost all drugs except ocrelizumab. 

Current evidence is looking into the use of Bruton’s tyrosine kinase inhibitors.

### MS and Pregnancy
Pregnancy is associated with a lower annualised relapse rate during pregnancy, with a rebound once birth is completed. A history of pregnancy is associated with a lower rate of conversion from CIS to MS. 

#### Choice of Therapeutics in Pregnancy:
| Timeframe                                 | Drugs                                                 |
|-------------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------|
| Cease while trying to conceive            | Alemtuzumab, fingolimod, cladribine, teriflunomide    |
| Cease six months prior to conception      | B-cell depletors/CD20 modulators                      |
| Safe to continue while trying to conceive | **Natalizumab**, dimethylfumarate, glatiramer acetate |
| During pregnancy (until T3)               | **Natalizumab**                                       |

### Symptomatic Management in MS
#### Spasticity
Non-pharmacological: exercise, PT, hydrotherapy
Pharmacological: baclofen, gabapentin, dantrolene, benzodiazepines, THC; further-line: baclofen pump; focal: botox 

#### Fatigue
Non-pharmacological: exercise, CBT, OT, aerobic training
Pharmacological: amantadine, modafinil, fampridine

#### Ambulation
Non-pharmacological: exercise
Pharmacological: fampridine

#### Ataxia/tremor
Non-pharmacological: PT
Pharmacological: propranolol, clonazepam, levetiracetam; focal: botox 

#### Bladder Dysfunction 
Non-pharmacological: sacral neuromodulation, intermittent self-catheterisation (atonia)
Pharmacological (good for OAB, not atonia): mirabegron, oxybutynin, botox

#### Sexual Dysfunction 
Non-pharmacological: CBT, pelvic floor PT
Pharmacological: sildenafil, intraurethral alprostadil

#### Pain
Non-pharmacological: PT, surgical intervention (e.g. for trigeminal neuralgia)
Pharmacological: amitriptyline, gabapentin, pregabalin, duloxetine; tramadol, capsaicin cream; trigeminal neuralgia - carbamazepine, lamotrigine
