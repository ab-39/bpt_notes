# Pulmonary Embolism
#bpt/respiratory #bpt/racp_cls

### What is a pulmonary embolus?
A pulmonary embolus is a clot affecting the pulmonary vasculature. Once established, the occlusion leads to an increase in dead space (as there is ventilation without perfusion) leading to a V/Q mismatch. The clot also increases pulmonary arterial pressures and affects RV afterload -> decreased RV function -> decreased LV preload -> reduced cardiac output. 

Although typically thromboembolic, it is important to remember that there can be non-thromboembolic causes of PE too, such as fat, tumour, air, amniotic fluid, and septic emboli. 

> [!IMPORTANT] Amniotic Fluid Embolism
> AFE is a significant cause of mortality in pregnancy. 
- - -
### Epidemiology
The incidence of pulmonary embolism is approximately 1/1000 persons per year in the USA, and the population prevalence in Australia is approximately 0.2%. 
- - -
### Clinical Features of PE
- Dyspnoea (~75%)
- Pleuritic chest pain (~66%, 3-7 days after event implying infarction)
- Cough (~37%)
- Haemoptysis (13%)
- Tachycardia (44%)
- Hypoxia is common but usually mild
- - -
### Risk Factors for PE
- Oestrogen/pregnancy
- Acute and chronic medical illness
- Recent surgery
- Travel (>4h)
- Malignancy
- Genetic risk
- Obesity 
- Metabolic syndrome
- Smoking
- - -
### Approach to PE
Algorithmic approach:
1. [[Well’s Score]]; if >4 -> CTPA; if ≤4:
2. PERC; if all negative -> PE excluded; if any positive:
3. d-Dimer -> if <0.5 -> exclude PE; if >0.5:
4. CTPA

Other tests include:
1. Bedside TTE -> look for signs of RV dysfunction/haemodynamic instability
2. Troponin -> often elevated in massive/submassive PE -> affects decision to treat
3. Fever/WCC/CRP -> may indicate infarction

### Diagnosis of PE
Some tests:
| Test                    | Findings/Notes                                               |
|-------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------|
| CXR                     | Important to rule out other causes of dyspnoea               |
| ECG                     | Typically nonspecific - most common sign is sinus tachycardia; look out for signs of right heart strain (e.g. high voltages in RV, right axis) or infamous S1Q3T3 |
| ABG                     | Increased A-a gradient, [[respiratory alkalosis]]            |
| V/Q                     | Look for an unmatched perfusion defect; good at ruling out PE<br>Needs to be done as a perfusion-only scan in pregnant women |
| CTPA                    | Look for filling defects                                     |
| USS                     | Doppler of lower limbs for ?DVT                              |
| TTE                     | Investigate right heart function, particularly important in haemodynamically unstable patients |
| MRA                     | Lower sensitivity than CTPA                                  |
| Pulmonary <br>Angiogram | Only if diagnosis is equivocal                               |

### Treatment of PE
Treatment of PE is a question of risk-benefit analysis:
- Low risk -> risk of bleeding can lead to higher mortality than the PE itself
- Intermediate risk -> consider planning for lysis if patient deteriorates
- High risk - usually haemodynamically unstable -> early thrombolysis 

Duration of treatment depends on the aetiology of the PE:
| Provoked PE                 | Unprovoked PE       |
|-----------------------------|---------------------|
| Transient RF -> 3/12        | 3-6/12              |
| Persistent RF -> indefinite | Consider indefinite |
Submassive PE -> 6-12 months
Massive PE -> indefinite

#### Choice of Therapy 
NOACs are the treatment of choice today:
- Rivaroxaban 
- Apixaban (lower bleeding risk)

If the patient relapses on a NOAC, the next line of therapy would be warfarin. 

In hospital, patients are usually treated with LMWH, whereas massive PE is usually treated with an unfractionated heparin infusion. 

For massive PE with evidence of haemodynamic compromise (SBP <90mmHg or drop >40mmHg from baseline) -> systemic or catheter directed thrombolysis

If for some reason anticoagulation is contraindicated, the patient may require an IVC filter, which will need to be removed after 3 months. 

Rarely, patients may receive a thrombectomy or VA-ECMO.

### Complications of PE
Acute:
- Sudden death
- Shock
- Arrythmia

Chronic:
- Chronic Thromboembolic Pulmonary Hypertension
- Recurrent PE
- - -
### Follow-Up for Patients with PE
Prognosis:
- Overall very good! Mortality ~0.2%
- Although this is higher for massive PE (5-30% depending on presence of shock)

Investigations:
- Massive PE (particularly if symptomatic) -> repeat imaging and TTE to rule out CTEPH
- Note that defects can persist without being clinically relevant

Prevention:
- Some patients may need anticoagulation as discussed above 
- Plan pregnancies carefully 
- [[Thrombophilia screen]] (particularly if unprovoked)
- Malignancy screening (contextually) 

#### Chronic Thromboembolic Pulmonary Hypertension 
Persistence of symptoms after three months of anticoagulation is strongly suggestive of chronic thromboembolic pulmonary disease. If this is associated with [[pulmonary hypertension]], the diagnosis of CTEPH can be confirmed with V/Q scanning. Management is typically with a pulmonary endarterectomy or alternatively balloon angioplasty. Warfarin is the anticoagulant of choice for CTEPH.
