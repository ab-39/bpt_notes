### What is portal hypertension?
Portal hypertension is defined as a **hepatic venous pressure gradient >5mmHg** (i.e. the difference in pressure between the portal and hepatic veins). Although it can be caused by pre-hepatic causes (typically VTE) or post-hepatic causes (typically VTE/Budd-Chiari or right heart failure), the vast majority of cases are caused by hepatic [[cirrhosis]], and the majority of complications classically associated with cirrhosis - ascites, portosystemic shunting, splenomegaly, and encephalopathy - are in fact due to the development of portal hypertension. 

Other causes of portal hypertension include:
- Prehepatic -> portal or splenic vein thrombosis, splenomegaly
- Intrahepatic:
  - Presinusoidal -> schistosomiasis, PBC, PSC, vinyl chloride, sarcoidosis
  - Sinusoidal -> arsenic, drugs, schistosomiasis, amyloidosis, acute fatty liver of pregnancy
  - Postsinusoidal -> venoocclusive disease, Budd-Chiari syndrome, vitamin A toxicity
- Posthepatic -> right heart failure, constrictive pericarditis

### Diagnosis 
The gold standard for diagnosis of portal hypertension is invasive measurement of the HVPG by finding the hepatic wedge pressure (WHVP, proxy for the portal venous pressure) and subtracting from it the free hepatic venous pressure (FHVP). This yields three possibilities:
- HVPG ≤5mmHg -> no portal hypertension 
  - Note that the normal pressures in the portal system are also <5mmHg 
- HVPG >5mmHg -> portal hypertension
- HVPG >10mmHg -> clinically significant portal hypertension (CSPH)

Instead of proceeding with angiography, FibroScan can be used as a surrogate for establishing compensated advanced chronic liver disease:
- <5kPa -> normal
- <10kPa -> no cACLD
- <15kPa -> possible cACLD
- <20kPa and plts >150 -> rules in cACLD, rules out CSPH, no need for endoscopy
- 25+kPa -> rules in CSPH

### Complications of portal hypertension
We consider cirrhosis to have “decompensated” when synthetic function is no longer preserved, and there is evidence of jaundice or complications of portal hypertension. Decompensation of cirrhosis reduces mean survival from 12 to just 2 years.

#### Ascites
Ascites, the accumulation of fluid in the peritoneal cavity, is the most common decompensation of cirrhosis. It arises due to the combination of increased hydrostatic pressure and reduced oncotic pressure _within_ the liver itself leading to the extravasation of fluid from the liver. Ascites in cirrhosis is defined by a **serum-to-ascites albumin ratio (SAAG) ≥11g/L**, although this finding is not specific for cirrhosis as it reflects ascites _due to portal hypertension_, which can also occur in heart failure, Budd-Chiari syndrome, portal vein thrombosis, and schistosomiasis.

> [!NOTE] Low-Gradient Ascites
> Ascites with a SAAG <11g/L is suggestive of peritoneal carcinomatosis, peritoneal tuberculosis, pancreatitis, serositis, or the [[nephrotic syndrome]].

Low protein ascites (<15g/L, particularly <10g/L) is considered a higher risk for SBP. All new ascites patients should receive a diagnostic paracentesis with cytology and culture to investigate for malignancy and SBP respectively. 

 Treatment is typically symptomatic, without mortality benefit, and consists of:
- Lifestyle measures - reduced salt diet, fluid restriction
- Pharmacotherapy - spironolactone:frusemide in a 5:2 ratio up to 400:160mg/day, with amiloride reserved for some refractory cases
- Interventions - paracentesis (requires albumin replacement), TIPS

Complications include hepatic hydrothorax, a typically right-sided [[pleural effusion]] that does _not_ warrant pleuredesis. 

#### Spontaneous Bacterial Peritonitis
SBP is the infection of ascitic fluid due to the breakdown in the gut mucosal barrier leading to translocation of bacteria into the fluid. Although SBP is often culture negative, the most commonly cultured organisms are, therefore, likely to be gut flora.
 
> [!IMPORTANT] Surgical Intervention
> Ruptured viscus or perforated bowel, both of which require urgent surgical attention, should be considered if >2 organisms are cultured from ascitic fluid, or if ≥2 of the following are present:
> - Total protein >10g/L
> - Glucose <2.8mmol/L
> - LDH ≥ULN for serum LDH

A neutrophil count >250/µL (corrected for RBCs in the sample if traumatic) is diagnostic of SBP. Presentation can range from asymptomatic, to a classical sepsis picture, to an _alternative_ complication of cirrhosis. 

The treatment is typically with high-dose ceftriaxone or piperacillin-tazobactam, typically with concurrent albumin for the first three days. Nonselective β-blockers should also be discontinued. 

Reduction in neutrophil count by 25% after 48h is a good prognostic marker. Patients with a prior episode of SBP are given prophylaxis with trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole or norfloxacin until the ascites is resolved. Primary prophylaxis is considered in those with CPC cirrhosis and protein <10g/L.

Steps that can be taken to prevent SBP include:
- Diuresis 
- Early and aggressive treatment of localised infections
- Restriction in the use of PPIs

#### Portosystemic Shunting
Unchecked portal hypertension leads to retrograde flow through the venous system, and lead to engorgement of capillary beds shared by the portal and systemic systems - most often in the oesophagus ([[oesophageal varices]]), and also in the stomach and rectum (haemorrhoids) (there is also recanalisation of the umbilical vein through a similar process, leading to _caput medusae_). Varices are a very common complication, affecting around 75% of cirrhotics (eventually). Screening is recommended if LSM >20kPa and platelets are <150, and not otherwise. Preventive treatment is with nonselective β-blockers or endoscopic ligation, which have been found to have equal efficacy. Β-blockade is recommended in the first instance as it treats the underlying cause of variceal development and bleeding. Secondary prophylaxis is with combination therapy, with EVL beginning from 6-8 weeks after index bleed. 

The primary complication of varices is rupture of the variceal wall due to increased tension, leading to bleeding in proportion to the portal pressure. The risk of haemorrhage is 5-15%, depending on factors such as size, Child-Pugh status, the ‘red-wale’ sign on the varyx (a ‘cherry red spot’ indicating increased tension), ongoing alcohol use, and HVPG >12mmHg.

#### Hepatic Encephalopathy
HE is a neuropsychiatric manifestation of advanced liver disease and portal hypertension that can present in a varied fashion ranging from altered sleep-wake regulation to total coma (West Haven criteria). The pathogenesis is complex, but fundamentally relates to a triad of portosystemic shunting (i.e. toxins bypassing the detoxification of the liver), hepatic insufficiency, and sarcopenia (due to the role of muscle in absorbing ammonia), combined with the increased production of ammonia from glutamine. Note that gaseous anaemia is likely more important, as it can cross the blood-brain barrier in a pH dependent fashion (alkalosis is worse). 

> [!NOTE] Ammonia
> Ammonia is produced by enterocytes during the digestion of protein; in a healthy liver it is subsequently converted to glutamine. Muscle is an important site for the removal of extrahepatic ammonia, therefore, muscle wasting can contribute to higher ammonia levels. Measuring ammonia is not a useful test in predicting prognosis in this group of patients. Hepatic encephalopathy is graded clinically in terms of orientation, lethargy, apathy etc. No biochemical tests are included unlike in the Child Pugh or MELD scores.

Diagnosis is clinical, with **asterixis** being the classical finding, although a CTB should be completed on the first presentation to rule out other causes. Ammonia is typically only of use in ruling _out_ HE; a Stroop test can be used as a useful rule-in test.

Treatment involves reversal of the precipitant, lactulose (which acidifies the colon, promoting the conversion of NH3 -> NH4+ which is excreted), and rifaximin (which alters the gut microbiota). 

Treatments that have _not_ been found to be effective include protein restriction, given that many patients with cirrhosis have cormorbid sarcopenia, and pre-/probiotics. There is evidence, however, that replacing animal protein with vegetable protein mau be beneficial.

### Management 
Nonselective β-blockade (usually carvedilol or propranolol) is recommended for prevention of decompensation of cACLD with CSPH.
