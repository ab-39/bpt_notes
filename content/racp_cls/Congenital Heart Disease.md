### Overview 
Congenital heart disease is a common problem, occurring in approximately 1% of live births, with an increased risk in the children of mothers with CHD (~5%). Although there are genetic and environmental factors, most cases are idiopathic, with the exception of a few key established associations:
- Trisomy 21 -> VSD
- 22q deletion -> great artery malformations
- Lithium -> Ebstein abnormality, TV abnormalities
- Rubella -> peripheral PA stenosis
![](Congenital%20Heart%20Disease/image.png)

The various congenital heart disease syndromes can be classified according to the nature of the lesion and their physiological consequences:
| Shunts                                     | Stenotic Lesions   | Cyanotic Heart Disease              |
|--------------------------------------------|--------------------|-------------------------------------|
| Atrial septal defect (step up in RA)       | Pulmonary stenosis | Univentricular Heart                |
| Ventricular septal defect (step up in RV)  | Aortic stenosis    | Truncus arteriosus                  |
| Patent ductus arteriosus (step up in PA)   | Aortic coarctation | Transposition of the great arteries |
| Atrioventricular septal defect             |                    | Tetralogy of Fallot                 |
| Anomalous pulmonary veins (step up in SVC) |                    |                                     |

Note that with time, left-to-right shunt will cause chronically elevated blood flow and thus pressures in the pulmonary vasculature, causing [[pulmonary hypertension]]. This will, in turn, lead to increased right ventricular pressures and ultimate reversal of the shunt into a [[right-to-left shunt]] - a complication which is termed **Eisenmenger physiology**. This, as well as large ASD or VSD, can present as **cyanotic heart disease**, which will present with hypoxia, erythrocytosis, and possible paradoxical emboli.

### Epidemiology
In children with no family history, the risk of developing congenital heart disease is approximately 1%, and this increases with parental (particularly maternal) or sibling history to as high as 7%. 
- - -
### Atrial Septal Defect
ASDs, particularly secundum defects, are the commonest type of CHD, affecting around 1/500 live births, representing ~10% of congenital heart defects. Although these usually present in childhood, patients can remain asymptomatic until adulthood. 

Typically causes a left-to-right shunt, although like any shunt, with time this can reverse due to [[pulmonary hypertension]] and the patient can develop Eisenmenger physiology. ASDs are associated with anomalous pulmonary venous drainage, with one of the pulmonary veins (usu. RUPV) draining into the RA. 

The defects themselves can be located in the body of the atrial septum (secundum), in the area near the atrioventricular valves (primum), or into the venous sinus. Secundum defects are more amenable to closure percutaneously. Intervention is indicated in the presence of:
- Symptoms
- RV dilatation/impairment
- Embolic events 

The majority of ASDs can be closed percutaneously with an Amplatzer device, but surgery may be required for large defects without sufficient surrounding tissue for the device to ‘grip’ on to, or if there is anomalous pulmonary venous drainage.

### Atrioventricular Septal Defect
This is defeat of the walls spanning both atrium and ventricle, often with a single AV valve. 
- - -
### Patent Ductus Arteriosus 
A patent ductus arteriosus is a persistent connection between the aorta and one of the pulmonary arteries, causing a left to right shunt, which can lead to LV dilatation and eventual failure. The ‘classical’ murmur is a continuous systolic murmur extending into diastole heard best beneath the left clavicle. 
- - -
### Pulmonary Stenosis 
Stenotic pulmonary valve lesions can be valvular, subvalvular, or supravalvular, with the location affecting choice of treatment:
- Subvalvular = muscular -> not amenable to BPV 
- Valvular -> amenable to BPV
- Supravalvular -> may be amenable to BPV or stenting

Pulmonary outflow tract stenosis is associated with the Noonan syndrome (Ch12) and the William syndrome. 
- - -
### [[Aortic Stenosis]]
Congenital aortic stenosis is often associated with a bicuspid aortic valve. Other key problems include coarctation lor hypoplasia of the aorta, or aneurysms of the aorta. The **Ross Operation** has been shown to be of benefit in congenital AS, and involves autografting the patient’s own PV into the AV position, and using a donor graft to replace the AV.

### Coarctation of the Aorta 
Coarctation of the aorta is associated with bicuspid aortic valve disease, and runs a spectrum from a very mild narrowing to a so-called interrupted aortic arch. The most common site is the early descending aorta just after the division of the left subclavian artery (where the PDA was in utero):

### Tetralogy of Fallot
Tetralogy of Fallot is a sequence of four cardiac defects:
- Pulmonary stenosis 
- VSD
- RVH
- Overriding aorta (i.e. mixed blood entering the aorta due to rightward migration of the vessel)

In TOF, there is an issue with diversion of blood flow to the aorta from the pulmonary circulation. To overcome this, some patients require shunting of blood from an aortic branch (e.g. the subclavian a.) to the pulmonary system through a **Blalock-Taussig shunt**. 

TOF causes cyanotic heart disease, and as such is typically fixed surgically in childhood. The main long-term complications of TOF repair are pulmonary regurgitation, pulmonary restenosis, arrhythmias, heart failure, and aortic root dilatation. 

The main ongoing issue after repair is pulmonary regurgitation. Typically, once the indexed RV volume exceeds 160mL/sqm, PV replacement is required which can be done percutaneously or surgically.

### Transposition of the Great Arteries
In transposition of the great arteries, the aorta comes off the RV (instead of the LV), but there is some subtlety as to what happens with the RV itself:
- d-TGA -> the aorta emerges from the RV, which is on the right-hand side of the heart
- l-TGA -> the aorta emerges from the RV, which is on the _left-hand side_ of the heart 
  - (**L** transposition = **L**ess bad)
![](Congenital%20Heart%20Disease/image%203.png)<!-- {"width":436} -->

Dextrotransposition of the great arteries is not compatible with life, as the deoxygenated venous blood from the systemic circulation is being sent straight back out into the aorta. This was traditionally treated with an ‘atrial switch’ that led to the LV supplying the lungs and the RV supplying the rest of the body. Predictably, this was associated with a high rate of atrial arrhythmias and RV dilatation/failure. In modern times, the trend is to treatment with ‘arterial switch’, which is exactly what the name implies. This does, however, carry risks of anastomotic stenoses, insufficiency of the coronary arteries (which have been implanted into the neoaorta), and progressive aortic dilatation.

Levotransposition is also called a ‘double transposition’, as the right ventricle has also been transposed onto the left side. Unlike d-TGA, this _is_ compatible with life, and may be detected later in life, in which case it is strongly associated with heart block, heart failure, and TR/RV dysfunction.

### Ebstein Anomaly
Ebstein anomaly is a tricuspid valvular abnormality due to defects in embryological development of the TV, which is strongly associated with eventual RV failure. It is also highly associated with [[Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome]]. The most common presentation is with [[tricuspid regurgitation]], although occasional stenosis is also possible. Surgical repair is the most common treatment. 
- - -
### Truncus Arteriosus
TA refers to the situation in which the aortic and pulmonary trunks combine into one large vessel that supplies both the systemic and pulmonary vasculatures. 
- - -
### Univentricular Physiology 
When a patient has univentricular physiology, the palliative **Fontan procedure** can be used to directly wire the SVC/IVC into the pulmonary circuit. This is commonly used with a palliative approach in patients with hypoplastic left heart syndrome. 
- - -
### Clinical Presentation
Key ‘tricky’ points to be aware of:
- Altered anatomy (e.g. dextrocardia) is more common 
- Shunt surgery may lead to lower arterial pressures in one arm
- Peripheral pulse may be inaccurate due to cyanosis or arrhythmias 
- Intracardiac shunt may lead to hypoxia at rest
