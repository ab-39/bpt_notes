### Proteinuria
Proteinuria is treated by inhibition of [[the renin-angiotensin system]] to reduce glomerular pressures, primarily through ACEi or A2RB drugs.

Recently, the _DAPA-CKD_ trial has established the efficacy of SGLT2i in CKD for multiple outcomes, with a nearly unbelievable [[number needed to treat]] - 19! There are side effects of these drugs, however, particularly genital mycoses, UTIs, and euglycaemic ketoacidosis.

The AASK study also established evidence for β-blockers in reducing proteinuria.

### Hypertension
Hypertension in CKD largely arises as a result of dysregulation of the renin-angiotensin system, and also due to the retention of solutes and fluid. Moreover, hypertension is an important cause of CKD.

One of the first signs of blood pressure dysregulation in CKD is the loss of nocturnal hypotension.

The 2012 KDIGO guidelines suggest the following BP targets:
- ≤140/90mmHg if no albuminuria
- ≤130/80mmHg if albuminuria -> use ACEi/A2RB

These outcomes are based on data from the _SPRINT_ (diabetes excluded) and _ACCORD_ (diabetes only) trials. _SPRINT_ provided evidence for intensive blood pressure lowering, while _ACCORD_ did not. The _CLICK_ trial suggested that there may be a role for thiazide/thiazide-like diuretics in improving uACR, at the expense of eGFR.

### Anaemia 
Anaemia in CKD is complex, due to an interplay of:
- Dysfunction of EPO secreting cells in the juxtaglomerular apparatus 
- Iron deficiency
- Anaemia of chronic disease 

We define anaemia as:
- Hb <120g/L (M)
- Hb <110g/L (F)

The initial approach to anaemia in CKD is as for iron deficiency, as this is often the main cause. Iron stores should be repleted, preferable intravenously. As per [KDIGO](https://kdigo.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/KDIGO-2025-Anemia-in-CKD-Guideline_Public-Review-Draft_Nov42024.pdf):
> *In people with anemia and CKD not receiving dialysis or treated with peritoneal dialysis (CKD G5PD), we suggest initiating iron if:*
> *• ferritin <100 ng/ml (<100 µg/l) and transferrin saturation (TSAT) <40%, or*
> *• ferritin ≥100 ng/ml (≥100 µg/l) and <300 ng/ml (<300 µg/l), and TSAT <25%.*

 Erythropoietin-stimulating agents (ESAs) are the next line of treatment, and are not without side effects (generally pro-thrombotic).The goal of these two treatments is to maintain Hb 110-115g/L, and to prevent blood transfusions so as to minimise the risk of HLA sensitisation in patients who are possible candidates for [[renal transplantation]]. The data for this target comes from the _CHOIR_ trial, which showed an improvement in outcomes with a target of 113g/L vs 135g/L. Similarly, the _TREAT_ study found that in patients where 130g/L was targeted and ESAs were used, there was an increased risk of stroke. The most common side effect of EPO is hypertension, so much so that EPO use is contraindicated in severe hypertension. Other side effects include rash, bone pain, flu-like symptoms, and pure red cell aplasia (rare). 

There are newer therapies on the horizon, particularly investigating the role of drugs that directly modulate the hypoxia-inducible factor. These drugs may increase the amount of iron available for erythropoiesis by increasing intestinal iron absorption and/or decreasing iron sequestration.

 ### Acidosis
In CKD, the ability to synthesise ammonia is lost (ammonia being a nitrogenous compound), and ammonia cannot, therefore, act in its role as the chief urinary buffer of acids. This leads to an impaired ability to excrete hydrogen, and a subsequent metabolic acidosis. 

The aim of treatment is to optimise the HCO3- level, but what this optimal level is unclear - likely somewhere in the range of 24-26mmol/L. There is some evidence that NaHCO3 _may_ slow progression in moderate CKD, but this can also lead to fluid retention. A new drug, veverimer, binds HCl in the GIT and excretes it, and is showing promise.

### Hyperkalaemia 
Hyperkalaemia in CKD arises secondary to reduced GFR and thus clearance of potassium. Additionally, this is likely compounded by the inhibition of the renin-angiotensin system by many of the treatments used for CKD (e.g. ACEi, A2RB). 

The ideal K+ in non-dialysis CK patients is likely in the range of 4-5mmol/L.

Management involves reduction/cessation of RAAS inhibition, and then secondly consideration of potassium binders: calcium/sodium polystyrene sulphonate (Resonium) or patiromer.

### Dysregulation of the Calcium-Phosphate Axis
The derangement of phosphate in CKD is a well established phenomenon. Historical treatment of this was likely associated with oversuppression of [[parathyroid hormone]], and excessive calcification due to calcium loading, which has lead to a change in treatment in the last twenty years, with the emergence of treatments such as sevelamer and cinacalcet, and the even more recent emergence of iron-based phosphate binders.

Key hormones/molecules:
- Phosphate (PO4)
- Calcium (Ca2+)
- [[Parathyroid hormone]]
  - Suppressed by Ca2+ and vitamin D
  - Increases levels of Ca2+
  - Increases levels of FGF23
  - Increases levels of bitamin D
- FGF (fibroblast growth factor) 23
  - Phosphaturic hormone 
  - Increased by vitamin D
  - Suppresses vitamin D
- Vitamin D
  - Increases GI absorption of calcium
