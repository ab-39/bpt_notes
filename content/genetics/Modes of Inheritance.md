### Autosomal Dominant
A pattern of inheritance in which one copy of a gene (i.e. heterozygosity) is sufficient for the individual to be affected. These genes are transmitted by affected males and females, and do not ‘skip’ generations. For an affected parent, the risk of having affected offspring in 50%. Example pedigree:
![](Modes%20of%20Inheritance/image.png)
Key modifiers:
- **Expression** = the degree to which a disorder is expressed in an _individual_
- **Penetrance** = the proportion of people with a mutation who are affected
- **Anticipation** = increasing severity and reducing age of onset with successive generations, usually due to successive expansion of trinucleotide repeats 
- - -
### Autosomal Recessive 
A pattern of inheritance in which two copies of a gene (i.e. homozygosity or compound heterozygosity) is required for an individual to be affected. This leads to carriers existing, and the eventual emergence of disease in a single generation. Males and females are affected equally. 
![](Modes%20of%20Inheritance/image%202.png)
Can offer patients with a family history of autosomal recessive (or X-linked recessive) condition asymptomatic carrier testing prior to conception.

#### The Two-Thirds Rule
For an autosomal recessive trait when both parents are carriers, the possible outcomes are affected (25%), carrier (50%), or unaffected (25%). If we _know_ an individual from the second generation to be unaffected (e.g. the classic example - they are the sibling of an affected individual) their probability of being a carrier needs to be calculated with conditional probabilities:
> $Pr(Carrier|Unaffected)$
> $= Pr(A \cap B) / Pr(B)$ 
> $=(1/2)/(3/4)$
> $=2/3$ 

This can also be interpreted in light of Bayes’ theorem:
> $Pr(Carrier|Unaffected)$
> $=Pr(Unaffected|Carrier) * Pr(Carrier) / Pr(Unaffected)$
> $=1 * (2/4) / (3/4)$
> $= 2/3$

### X-Linked Recessive
A pattern in which males with only one copy of a gene on the X chromosome are affected, while females with a single copy are carriers. This leads to males being predominantly affected, but they then cannot (by definition) pass the condition on to their son (to whom they have given a Y chromosome). That being said, while males are typically the only ones who demonstrate the full form of the disease, many women who are carriers of XLR alleles can still manifest limited forms, typically due to atypical Lyonisation, e.g.:
- [[neurology/Dystrophinopathies|Duchenne muscular dystrophy]] -> females often develop cardiomyopathy
- X-linked adrenoleukodystrophy -> most females develop mild myelopathy later in life
![](Modes%20of%20Inheritance/image%203.png)
Note that _male-to-male_ transmission makes an X-linked trait impossible, and women _can_ be affected if their father is affected and their mother is a carrier.

### Mitochondrial
mtDNA from the sperm cells are broken down after fertilisation, meaning that all of an individual’s mtDNA comes from the mother (i.e. females are affected and pass on the condition, males are affected but cannot pass on the condition). Due to heteroplasmy, the number of mutant mtDNA copies in a given maternal cell can vary, meaning that the manifestiations of mitochondrial disorders can do so too. 
![](Modes%20of%20Inheritance/image%205.png)
Note that _male-to-male_ and _male-to-female_ transmission make an mitochondrial trait impossible. Consanguinity does not affect risk in mitochondrial inheritance. 
- - -
### Incomplete Penetrance
Incomplete penetrance refers to individuals who have a given genotype but do _not_ express the phenotype. 
![](Modes%20of%20Inheritance/image%204.png)
This is a categorical variable for individuals, but a continuous one for populations.

### Variable Expressivity
This refers to the spectra of manifestations of diferent genetic conditions.

### Imprinting
Genomic imprinting is the process by which only one copy of a gene in an individual (either from their mother or their father) is expressed, while the other copy is suppressed. Unlike genomic mutations that can affect the ability of inherited genes to be expressed, genomic imprinting does not affect the DNA sequence itself. Instead, gene expression is silenced by the epigenetic addition of chemical tags to the DNA during egg or sperm formation. Epigenetic tags on imprinted genes usually stay in place for the life of the individual.
- - -
### Linkage
Linkage, as related to genetics and genomics, refers to the closeness of genes or other DNA sequences to one another on the same chromosome. The closer two genes or sequences are to each other on a chromosome, the greater the probability that they will be inherited together.
