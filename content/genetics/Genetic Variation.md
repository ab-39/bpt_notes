### Overview
Although genetic variations (_né_ mutations) are not necessarily pathogenic, they are often implicated in to aetiology of genetic disease.

A _variation_ is simply a change in an individual’s genome, and these can be common (e.g. single nucleotide polymorphisms that occur in ~1/300 base pairs or 1% of the population) or rare (e.g. mutations specific to individuals). Most new variations are introduced from the father via the sperm.
- - -
### Types of Genetic Variants
#### By “how” they occur…
Germline variants:
- Present in all cells of the body including germ cells
- Inherited from parent to offspring
- Can (but not necessarily) lead to the development of disease

Somatic variants:
- Occur after conception, i.e. acquired during an individual’s lifetime
- Due to environmental factors or errors in cell division
- Generally not present in germ cels and thus not inherited
- Can (but not necessarily) lead to the development of disease 

#### By “where” they occur…
At the level of the chromosome:
- Variations that result from a change in the number or structure of chromosomes, e.g.
  - Numerical - loss or gain of entire chromosomes
  - Structural - e.g. deletion, duplication, or rearrangement of a section of a chromosome
- Can be balanced or unbalanced:
  - Balanced - rearrangement, but no overall gain or loss. Can, however, result in the production of germ cells with incomplete sets of chromosomes. 
  - Unbalances - gain or loss of genetic material, often with profound effects.

At the level of the gene:
- Variations that result from a change in a person’s DNA sequence, including:
  - Single nucleotide variants
  - Insertions or deletions
  - Nucleotide repeats
  - Copy number variants - duplications and deletions that are a normal part of genetic architecture 
- The effects of these depend on several factors, including the function of the gene, the number of variants, the effects on the encoded protein, inheritance, and where the variant is expressed

### Epigenetic Regulation
Epigenetic modifications influence the activation or deactivation of genes through reversible alterations to the chromatin structure. The main mechanism by which this is achieved is DNA methylation, the process by which DNA methyltransferase links CH3 groups to cytosine bases to form 5-methylcytosine. This occurs in regions known as CpG islands, frequently where promoter regions for genes are located. 

The other main mechanism of epigenetic modification is histone acetylation, which leads to increased expression of the relevant gene. Mutations leading to dysregulation of histone deacetylases (HDACs) play an important role in cancer, e.g. _RUNX1_ in AML.
