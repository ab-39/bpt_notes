# Giant Cell Arteritis
#bpt/rheumatology #bpt/racp_cls
- - -
### Overview
Giant cell, or temporal, arteritis is a granulomatous, medium-to-large-vessel [[vasculitis]] classically affecting the temporal artery. Diagnosis requires three of the five (old) ARA criteria:
1. Age at disease onset ≥50y
2. Localised headache of new onset
3. Tender or decreased pulse of temporal artery
4. ESR > 50mm/h
5. Biopsy with necrotising arteritis 

Although this disease is most notorious for affecting the temporal arteries, with an attendant risk of blindness, it can affect only large vessels (e.g. axillary artery, subclavian artery) in 25% of cases. In 14% of cases, there may be aneurysm of the thoracic aorta. There is an association with polymyalgia rheumatica. 

The gold standard diagnostic test for GCA is bilateral long biopsies of the temporal arteries, which has a high sensitivity, and imaging is also useful. It is improtant to remember that biopsy _can_ be negative due to skip lesions, insufficient sample, or isolated LVV. Inflammatory markers such as CRP and ESR are useful for serial monitoring. The ‘classic’ finding on imaging is the **halo** sign - a hypoechoic thickening of the vessel walls due to vasculitic oedema.  

The mainstay of treatment for GCA is steroids, which can be commenced up to a week before biopsy, and are usually continued for two years. Low-dose aspirin is also used. Diagnosis of GCA requires prompt treatment to reduce the risk of visual loss.

### Phenotypes of GCA
There are four main clinical patterns of GCA:
1. Isolated cranial GCA = headache, jaw claudication, scalp tenderness (~80%)
2. Symptomatic large vessel vasculitis with or without associated cranial signs = claudication, pulseless limb +/- GCA symptoms (~9%)
3. Isolated fever or inflammatory response (~9%)
4. Isolated polymyalgia rheumatica with vasculitis on imaging (~2%)

#### Complications of GCA:
- Aortic aneurysm - worst >5y after diagnosis 
- - -
### Vision in GC
The most dreaded complication of GCA, occurring in about 30% of patients, is visual loss, which can be:
- Partial or complete
- Transient or persistent
- Typically sudden (in which case it is usally permanent)
- Painful
- Associated with diplopia (due to ischaemia of the extraorbital muscles)
- Associated with ptosis, INO, etc. 

The most common cause of visual loss in GCA is anterior ischaemic optic neuropathy due to vasculitic occlusion of the posterior ciliary arteries. Other causes to be aware of include posterior ischaemic optic neuropathy, central retinal arterial occlusion, and choroidal ischaemia. 

Funduscopic findings associated with AION in the setting of GCA include:
- Optic nerve head oedema/pallor
- Cotton wool spots (ischaemia of the retina)
- Optic disc pallor with cupping
- Cherry-red spot from occlusino of the sentral retinal artery

### Pathogenesis of GCA
Cascade of events:
- Activation of dendritic cells in the adventitia of the blood vessels (TLR-7)
- This causes inflammatory infiltrate into the media and proliferatoin of smooth muscle leading to luminal narrowing and ischaemia
- As well as formation of tissue giant cells

This tends to affect medium and large sized arteries which possess an internal elastic lamina. 
- - -
### Diagnosis
GCA is a _clinical_ diagnosis that should be _confirmed_ by imaging or biopsy.

In addition to the discussion above, the most useful imaging modalities are:
- USS (‘halo’ sign and compression test revealing thickened arterial wall)
- MRI + Gd contrast (high NPV 98%, but poor PPV 48%)
- 18-FDG PET
- CT angiography

It is important to remember that imaging is most useful in the first 48-96h of initiation of steroid therapy, after which its sensitivty drops off. Serum BAFF and IL-6 correlate with disease activity. 
- - -
### Treatment
Patients presenting with visual symptoms -> pulsed IV methylprednisolone for three days. Development of visual loss _after_ initiating visual loss is <1%. 

The side effects of steroids are significant, with a particularly high risk of infection.

More recently, the _GIACTA_ study has investigated the role of tocilizumab, an [[immunology/Interleukin 6|interleukin 6]] inhibitor, in treating GCA. In this study, the tocilizumab + steroid arm had a higher rate of sustained remission at week 52 compared to the steroid alone group, with a lower total dose of steroids. The caveat being that optic ischaemia was an exclusion criteria for the trial. Tocilizumab is PBS approved for GCA in Australia. Note that inherent to the mechanism of tocilizumab is blockage of the production of CRP, making monitoring of disease activity difficult.

There are also studies in Germany investigating the role of [[immunology/Interleukin 17|interleukin 17]] antagonism with secukinumab in GCA. 

Other treatments include:
- Aspirin (may inhibit IFNγ) - particularly if there is actually flow limiting cerebral artery disease 
- Methotrexate 
- Leflunomide
- Tocilizumab

#### Complications of Treatment:
- Infections - particularly in the first year
- Osteoporosis
- Cataracts
- Effect on diabetes control 
- Minimal effect on cancer risk
