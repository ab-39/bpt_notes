# Atrial Fibrillation
#bpt/cardiology
- - -
### Overview
The supraventricular tachycardia _par excellence_, an ‘irregularly irregular’ rhythm that is characterised by the complete absence of P-waves on the ECG. AF can fall into four categories:
- First-diagnosis
- Paroxysmal AF -> terminates spontaneously or with intervention within 7d
- Persistent AF -> episodes that are not self-terminating
- Permanent AF -> once the decision has been made to no longer aim for rhythm control

Atrial fibrillation is associated with a _suite_ of complications:
- Increased mortality
- LV dysfunction - often secondary to cardiomyopathy or mitral annular dilatation
- Dementia
- Depression
- Stroke
- - -
### Management Strategies
Patients with AF should be evaluated holistically. Firstly, they should receive long-term monitoring (e.g. a loop recorder) to investigate for ‘silent’ AF. Secondly, their risk factors, particularly weight and OSA, should also be addressed. Beyond this, there are two major strategies for treatment. Conventional thought was that rate control was non-inferior to rhythm control in terms of outcomes, although newer trial data (e.g. [_EAST AFNET 4_](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2019422)) are challenging this, favouring a rhythm control strategy. 

#### Rate Control
_Accepting the ongoing underlying arrhythmia_, the goals is to prevent symptoms and cardiac dysfunction from rapid ventricular rates. In general, the aims are:
- HR <80bpm at rest (lenient: <110bpm)
- HR <110bpm with moderate exercise

First line treatments for rate control are:
- β-Blockers - e.g. metoprolol tartrate
  - Significantly reduce rate + symptoms, but also limit functional capacity
- Non-dihydropyridine CCBs - i.e. diltiazem or verapamil
  - Reduce exercising HR and symptoms most 
- Digoxin
  - Best effect on resting HR, may improve functional capacity
  - Substrate for p-glycoprotein, which is inhibited by amiodarone -> in combination therapy, reduce the dose of digoxin by ~50%

#### Rhythm Control
Aim to restore and maintain sinus rhythm. The ‘gold standard’ therapy for this is direct current cardioversion, but this requires a patient either being in new AF (<48h), or being anticoagulated. Despite being excellent at restoring SR, the long-term maintenance of SR is modest at best with DCR. The current recommendation for patients presenting with new AF is to ‘watch and wait’ - institute rate control and anticoagulation, and then cardiovert them within 48h.

The best treatment for long-term maintenance of SR is pulmonary vein isolation with catheter ablation (usually either cryoablation or pulsed field ablation, the latter being safer by exploiting specific tissues’ specific sensitivities to electric fields), or by disrupting fibrillation pathways with the Cox maze procedure.

### AF and Stroke Risk
AF is a significant risk factor for ischaemic stroke, and this risk is quantified with the CHA2DS2-VA(Sc) score:
| Factor            | Points            | Cumulative Score | Annual Stroke Rate |
|-------------------|-------------------|------------------|--------------------|
|                   |                   | 0                | 0.8%               |
| CCF               | 1                 | 1                | 1.3%               |
| HTN               | 1                 | 2                | 2.2%               |
| Age ≥75y          | +1 (i.e. total 2) | 3                | 3.2%               |
| Diabetes mellitus | 1                 | 4                | 4.0%               |
| Stroke/TIA Hx     | 2                 | 5                | 6.7%               |
| Vascular disease  | 1                 | 6                | 9.8%               |
| Age ≥65y          | 1                 | 7                | 9.6%               |
| Sex (female)      | 1                 | 8                | 6.7%               |
| **Maximum**       |                   | 9                | 15.2%              |
The current guidelines recommend _not_ counting a point for sex -> the CHA2DS2-VA score. 

A CHA2DS2-VA score ≥2 is a class I recommendation for anticoagulation with a DOAC:
- Dabigatran 150mg BD = direct thrombin inhibitor ([_RE-LY_](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa0905561)) 
  - Contranindicated if CrCl <15mL/min, generally not used if CrCl <30mL/min
  - Renally cleared 
  - Can be reversed with idarucizumab
  - Substrate for p-glycoprotein
  - Dose adjust to 110mg BD if any of:
    - Age ≥80y
    - Concomitant verapamil use (increases plasma concentration of verapamil through p-glycoprotein inhibition) - although note that itraconazole causes the highest increase in plasma Pradaxa levels 
- Rivaroxaban 20mg PO daily  = factor Xa inhibitor ([_ROCKET-AF_](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1009638))
  - Contraindicated if CrCl <15mL/min
  - Primarily metabolised by the CYP450 system in the liver (3A4), also a substrate for p-glycoprotein
  - Can be reversed with andexanet alfa or else partially with beriplex
  - Reduce dose to 15mg PO daily if CrCl 15-50mL/min
- Apixaban 5mg PO BD = factor Xa inhibitor ([_ARISTOTLE_](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1107039)) 
  - Primarily metabolised by the CYP450 system in the liver (3A4), also a substrate for p-glycoprotein
  - Can be reversed with andexanet alfa or else partially with beriplex
  - Dose reduce to 2.5mg PO BD if 2/3 factors present:
    - Age ≥80y
    - Body weight ≤60kg
    - sCr ≥133µmol/L 

Note that if a patient is on a drug that strongly induces warfarin or p-glycoprotein (e.g. carbamazepine), warfarin remains the anticoagulant of choice. 

Patients who have had previous ischaemic heart disease or intervention who are on single antiplatelets and _now_ need a DOAC, the antiplatelet can be ceased. 

There appears to be less benefit with anticoagulation in patients with AF on dialysis. Part of this appears to be related to warfarin, and most DOACs can be used while on dialysis. 

The 2015 BRIDGE trial, demonstrated that in AF patients with mean CHADS2 score of 2.3, bridging anticoagulation with LMWH resulted in no difference in arterial thromboembolism at 30 days. Therefore, it could be argued that bridging anticoagulation therapy is not required for elective procedures in AF patients with intermediate or low stroke risk.

### Valvular AF
So-called _valvular_ AF is a nebulous term, that refers to atrial fibrillation in the presence of mitral valvulopathy or valve replacement. Clinically, the significance is that patients who:
1. Have a mechanical prosthetic valve
2. Have moderate to severe mitral stenosis
3. Rheumatic mitral stenosis 
Are not recommended (as per ESCardio) to use DOACs for anticoagulation. In most other types of valvular heart disease, DOACs are typically alright. 

In “valvular” patients, the optimal agent for anticoagulation is **warfarin**, which has robust trial evidence for superior safety and efficacy.

### Regular AF?
In some cases, patients with AF can develop complete heart block (often under the influence of AV nodal blocking agents), leading to the emergence of an escape rhythm, which can be regular.

### The Problem with Flecainide
Flecainide, a class Ic antiarrhythmic, can be used _with caution_ in AF. It causes organisation of the wavefront into AFL, but also slows the conduction to below 300bpm, allowing this to be conducted by the AV node into a [[broad complex tachycardia]]. This is why flecainide is always co-administered with an AV nodal blocking agent (e.g. metoprolol).
