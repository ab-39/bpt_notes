# Oesophageal Varices

### Overview
Varices are a common complication of cirrhosis, with around 75% of cirrhotics eventually developing them, typically at a rate of 7%/y. Bleeding from varices is a common occurrence (~10%/y.) and is typically higher in higher grades of [[cirrhosis]]. 

Various factors influence the risk of bleeding:
- Varix size
- Child-Pugh grade
- Endoscopic stigmata - ‘red wale’ sign
- Continued alcohol use
- Hepatic venous pressure gradient >12mmHg 
- Previous bleeding 

Management of variceal bleeding begins with resuscitation, and early management is often pharmacological:
- Blood to a ‘restrictive’ target, aiming Hb >70g/L (evidence for increased mortality with more aggressive targets)
- Octreotide and terlipressin control bleeding in 80% of cases
  - 5-day course to avoid early re-bleeding 
  - Terlipressin is preferred as it is intermittent dosing 
- PPIs have little efficacy, but may reduce risk of post-banding bleeding 
- Antibiotics (ceftriaxone) reduce risk of re-bleeding, infection, and death (typically a five-day course)

> [!CAUTION] Fluid Resuscitation
> While important if patients are bleeding, caution should be exercised with aggressive fluid resuscitation as raising portal pressures can also worsen bleeding. 

Patients with suspected variceal bleeding should undergo endoscopy within 12hrs, and receive banding.

Patients may also develop gastric varices, which are graded according to the Sarin classification as either gastro-oesophageal (GOV1, GOV2), or isolated (IGV1, IGV2). Management is not as clear cut as for oesophageal varices, with better evidence for glue-based therapy than banding. There is a risk of systemic embolisation with glue injection. 

If this treatment fails, patients can be stabilised with a Sengstaken-Blakemore tube or a Danis stent which can remain in situ for a maximum of 24-36h. Escalation of therapy can be to salvage TIPS (radiology) or stenting. The latest EASL guidelines suggest early TIPS for all patients who have achieved haemostasis with EVL and have a Child-Pugh score <13. 

The main pharmacotherapeutic option for varices in both primary and secondary prevention is nonselective β-blockade, with a dose of 12.5mg carvedilol daily being the typical target, if tolerated with respect to heart rate and blood pressure. This dose is a target and not titrated to haemodynamics.
