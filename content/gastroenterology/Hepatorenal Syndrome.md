### Acute Kidney Injury 
[[Acute kidney injury]] is a very common occurrence (~60% of inpatients) in [[cirrhosis]], and is a predictor of mortality. As most patients with cirrhosis are sarcopenic, the eGFR and absolute creatinine are a poor indicator of real renal function, and so the change in creatinine definitions of AKI (>26.4µmol/L/48hor >50% of baseline/wk) are more commonly used, additionally, urine sodium is <10mmol/L. AKI in cirrhosis is typically pre-renal, and management is the same as for any AKI, with the added possibility of terlipressin if hepatorenal syndrome is present.

### Hepatorenal Syndrome
Although the pathogenesis of hepatorenal syndrome remains poorly understood, it is a well-characterised clinical phenomenon, consisting of ascites and AKI. Classically, it has been taught that hepatorenal syndrome is a ‘functional’ renal disorder without intrinsic renal pathology, although this is being challenged of late and has been dropped from the most recent criteria. The only definitive treatment for HRS is liver transplantation, with [[pharmacology/Terlipressin|terlipressin]] and albumin used to bridge them to this point. 
![](Hepatorenal%20Syndrome/image.png)
Hepatorenal syndrome is divided into two broad groups:
- HRS-AKI = acute-subacute increase in serum creatinine relative to basline without response to volume expansion in a patient with cirrhosis and ascites
- HRS-NAKI = CKD in a patient with cirrhosis and ascites

#### Management
The mainstay of treating HRS-AKI is correcting the volume deficit with albumin (1g/kg/d). Terlipressin can also be used to help maintain renal perfusion, but is associated with significant cardiac adverse effects. The only intervention that actually improves survival, however, is liver transplantation.
