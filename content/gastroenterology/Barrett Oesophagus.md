# Barrett Oesophagus

### Overview 
Barrett oesophagus refers to metaplastic change of the distal oesophagus from squamous epithelium to columnar epithelium in keeping with gastric, gastric cardiac, or intestinal morphology. Intestinal epithelium with goblet cells carries the highest risk of adenocarcinoma. Barrett oesophagus confers a relative risk of 40-100 times for oesophageal adenocarcinoma, although the absolute risk is still relatively low. 

In most definitions, there needs to be ≥1cm for diagnosis, and anything over 3cm is referred to as ‘long-segment’. 

Barrett oesophagus is caused by exposure to gastric acid and bile, and is, as such, strongly associated with [[gastro-oesophageal reflux disease]]. 

The prevalence of Barrett oesophagus increases with age, and typically affects 1-5% of the population, or about 10% of patients with GORD. Male sex, white race, family history, and smoking are important risk factors. 

Management of Barrett oesophagus begins with PPI to manage oesophagitis, and the risk of malignancy is usually managed through endoscopic surveillance. Twice-daily PPI and aspirin has been shown to reduce risk of progression to adenocarcinoma (_AspECT_ trial). Areas of high-grade dysplasia can undergoing endoscopic mucosal resection along with radio-frequency ablation of the rest of the metaplastic area. Surveillance interval ranges from six months for low-grade dysplasia (although RFA can be considered), to 3-5y if dysplasia is absent. Surgery can now be completed endoscopically with good results.

Barrett oesophagus has a very high risk of recurrence, so surveillance endoscopy is recommended annually for at lesast five years after treatment.
