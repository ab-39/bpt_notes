# Alcoholic Liver Disease
#bpt/gastroenterology
- - -
### Overview 
In quantities greater than ~4-6std/d for men or 2-3std/d for women over a duration longer than 10-12 eyars, alcohol willl cause liver damage fairly reliably by altering the NADH/NAD ratio, and disrupting hepatic fatty acid cycling, and through the directly toxic effects of its metabolites. 

In general, this is a chronic presentation with symptoms such as jaundice, fever, and anorexia. Biochemistry typically reveals a moderate elevation of the transaminases, with AST usually 2x the ALT, due to deficiencies of pyridoxal 5’-phosphate. There is often a moderate leucocytosis. 

Maddrey’s discriminant function is calculated to guide treatment:
> $DF = [4.6 * (PT - PT_{ULN})] + Bilirubin/17$ 

In general, a DF >32 suggests that the patient is suffering from severe disease, and would benfit from the administration of steroids, although threre is conflicting evidence as to whether or not this improves mortality. Other scores that can be used to determine severity of disease include the [MELD score](https://www.mdcalc.com/calc/10437/model-end-stage-liver-disease-meld)and the [Glasgow Alcoholic Hepatitis Score](https://www.mdcalc.com/calc/680/glasgow-alcoholic-hepatitis-score). 

Histology typically includes macrovesicular steatosis that is characterized by a single large vacuole or fat droplet occupying the entire hepatocyte, pushing the nucleus to the edge of the cell. Hepatocytes may also contain Mallory-Denk hyalines surrounded by neutrophils (termed Mallory-Denk bodies), which are more characteristic of alcohol-associated steatohepatitis than NASH.

Once treatment is instituted, a [Lille score](https://www.mdcalc.com/calc/2024/lille-model-alcoholic-hepatitis)>0.45 is indicative of a poor response to steroids. Prednisolone is the steroid of choice, as (unlike prednisone) it is not metabolised by the liver.
