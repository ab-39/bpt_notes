### Indications for Heart Transplantation
The following conditions may be eligible for heart transplantation if there is an expected survival benefit and a reasonable prospect of returning to an active lifestyle:
- Severe symptomatic heart failure
- Severe left ventricular systolic or diastolic _dysfunction_ (**not** just EF)
- VO2max  ≤14mg/kg/min
- Heart failure survival score - medium-to-high risk
- Seattle heart failure model score one-year survival <80%
- No contraindications to heart transplantation:
  - Age by itself is not a contraindication, although the procedure is rarely offered to those above seventy
  - The presence of comorbidities that result in high mortality/morbidity/risk, e.g.:
    - Active malignancy
    - Uncontrolled infection
    - Complicated diabetes
    - Obesity (BMI >35)
    - Non-adherence or inability to comply with complex medical advice/therapy
    - Non-compliance with pre-transplant workup/vaccinations
    - Substance use (need six months of abstinence prior to listing)
  - The presence of irreversible conditions that preclude proper rehabilitation taking place post-transplant
  - Pulmonary hypertension:
    - PASP >60mmHg
    - Transpulmonary gradient ≥15mmHg 
    - PVR >5WU
- In some cases - angina refractory to all forms of revascularisation

In essence, the indication for heart transplantation is _end-stage heart disease_, which may manifest in many ways:
- Irreversible cardiogenic shock
- Intractable symptomatic heart failure 
- Need for permanent mechanical support
- Recurrent ventricular arrhythmias/AICD shocks
- Intractable angina despite maximal therapy

### Complications
![](Approach%20to%20a%20Heart%20Transplant/image.png)
#### Rejection
- Hyperacute = rare nowadays
- Acute cellular rejection = T-cell mediated 
- Antibody mediated rejection = B-cell mediated

Not so important to know the grades (1R, 2R, 3R…), but know that treatment is generally for 2R+.  Monitored with frequent biopsies in the first year: incidence of biopsies requiring augmentation of immunosuppression in the first year is 20%, and then uncommon thereafter. 

Common precipitants for rejection include:
- Weaning of immunosuppression
- Nonadherence
- Infection

#### Cardiac Allograft Vasculopathy
A form of rejection - _not_ the usual lipid core atherosclerotic plaque we’re used to, but a more diffuse intimal thickening. Tends _not_ to cause angina as the transplanted heart is denervated.
![](Approach%20to%20a%20Heart%20Transplant/image%202.png)<!-- {"width":303} -->
Responsible for 32% of deaths after 5y! Tend to screen with CTCA or ESE. Risk factors = donor age, CMV status, and incidence of rejection episodes. Treat with:
- Statins
- Augmentation of immunosuppression (particularly everolimus or sirolimus = proliferation inhibitors)
- Revascularisation
- Retransplant

### Monitoring of Graft Function
Regular:
- Echocardiography
- cMRI
- Transjugular endomyocardial biopsy:
  - Weekly for the first four weeks
  - Every two weeks for the next six weeks
  - Monthly for the next three months
  - Every three months until the end of the first year
- - -
### Immunosuppression
1. Induction - patients tend not to know about this. Usually reserved for high risk patients. 
2. Maintenance - starts off strong, weans over time, tailored to patient _but always lifelong_
3. Rejection - means immunosuppression needs to be increased

That said, the treatment is usually a combination of TAC +/- MMF +/- Pred +/- EVL (rarely)
