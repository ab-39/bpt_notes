# Geriatric Syndromes in the Long Case
#bpt/clinical
- - -
### Overview
Key geriatric syndromes:
- Continence
- Delirium
- Dementia
- Falls and balance
- Medications
- Pain
- Legal issues and future planning

> [!IMPORTANT] 
> No older patient should leave a long case without getting a timed up-and-go/gait assessment **and** a mini-cog.
- - -
### Continence
- Definitions:
  - Urinary incontinence -> involuntary loss of urine, 4x more common in women, can be:
    - Stress -> urinary leakage on effort or exertion
    - Urge -> a sense of urgency leading to incontinence, usually due to detrusor overactivity
    - Mixed
    - Overflow -> a continuous dribble due to detrusor underactivity
  - Faecal incontinence -> involuntary loss of faeces, can be:
    - Passive -> caused by a lack of awareness of stool passing
    - Urge -> characterised by a desire to defaecate, but an inability to retain stool
  - Flatus incontinence 
- Burden -> embarrassment, anxiety, depression, lower rates of self-reported good health, sexual dysfunction and lower workforce participation; higher carer burden
- Risk factors for  incontinence:
  - Urinary:
    - Age
    - Female sex (always consider atrophic vaginitis)
    - Obesity
    - Parity 
    - Hysterectomy
    - Family history
    - Smoking
    - Constipation
    - Diabetes
    - Depression
    - Dementia
    - Alcohol, caffeine 
    - Neurological conditions
    - Medications - diuretics, cholinesterase inhibitors, SSRIs, CCBs, etc.
  - Faecal:
    - Older age
    - Diarrhoea
    - Faecal urgency
    - Constipation and impaction
    - Urinary incontinence
    - Diabetes
    - Laxative or antibiotic use
    - Medications can cause constipation - anticholinergics, iron, opioids, NSAIDs, etc. 
- Management - goal is to make meaningful improvements in a non-burdensome way:
  - Can aim for independent continence, dependent continence, or controlled incontinence
  - Main goal is to maintain dignity
  - Urinary incontinence:
    - Lifestyle factors - weight loss, restriction of caffeine/alcohol, management of constipation
      - NB. fluid restriction is _not_ recommended, as concentrated urine is more irritating -> rather, small volumes of fluid throughout the day are recommended
    - Specific exercises - pelvic floor exercises, bladder training
    - Continence aids:
      - Disposable pads
      - Bed protection
      - Catheters
    - Cessation of troubling medications
    - Certain medications can help:
      - Atrophic vaginitis -> topical oestrogen
      - Urge incontinence -> antimuscarinic drugs (e.g. oxybutynin, risk of anticholinergic effect and effect on cognition) +/- BoTox injections
      - Stress incontinence -> limited options 
  - Faecal incontinence:
    - Supportive measures
    - Medication review
    - Medical therapy, aiming to:
      - Increase stool bulk - e.g. fibre
      - Prevent impaction - e.g. softening agents, Movicol

### Falls
> [!WARNING]  
> There is no such thing as a “mechanical” fall - falls always occur as a combination of intrinsic, extrinsic, and environmental risk factors in the context of a precipitating event.

- Risk Factors
| Intrinsic               | Extrinsic                           |
|-------------------------|-------------------------------------|
| History of falls        | Slippery/uneven surfaces            |
| Fear of falling         | High-risk activities (e.g. ladders) |
| Advanced age            | Stairs                              |
| Female sex              | Loose rugs                          |
| Living alone            | Electrical cords, etc.              |
| Neurological conditions | Cluttered environment               |
| Frailty/sarcopenia      | Poor lighting                       |
| Orthostatic hypotension | Unsuitable footwear                 |
| Cognitive impairment    | Restraints                          |
| Urinary incontinence    | Multifocal lenses                   |
| Medications             |                                     |
| Alcohol                 |                                     |
- Precipitating event - e.g. trips/slips, delirium, change in medication, dizziness, etc.
- Location of the fall may give a clue into the characteristics of the faller:
  - Younger -> outside, while doing some sort of activity
  - Older, frailer -> inside, more association with disability
- Offending medications:
  - Benzodiazepine (increased risk ~ 48%)
  * Antipsychotics
  * Antidepressants (including  tricyclic and SSRIs)
  * Antiepileptics
  * Opioid analgesics
  * Diuretics
  * Antihypertensives including calcium channel blockers, beta blockers, prazosin, moxonidine  etc.

- Examination findings relevant to falls:
  - **Timed up and go** - do this for all elderly patients (or others at risk of falls) in a long case:
    - The person is tested using his/her usual footwear and gait aid.
    - They are seated in a standard chair with arms.
    - On the word 'go' the person rises from the chair without the use of arm-rest if possible, walks 3 meters, turns, returns to the chair and sits down
    - A “normal” time is <10s
    - Note:
      - Sitting balance -> ?cerebellar problem
      - Transfers from sitting to standing -> proximal muscle strength
      - Gait pattern and stability
      - Ability to turn without staggering
    - A time >10s indicates an increased risk of falls
  - Rombergs test - the Romberg is a test of **proprioception, not cerebellar dysfunction**. It is positive when the patient is able to stand with feet together and eyes open without losing balance but is unable to remain steady with the eyes closed.
    This occurs because the patient is using their eyes to compensate for the lack of sensory feedback they are receiving from their lower extremities.  A functional correlate of this might be when a person can't close their eyes when showering.
  - **Pastor Test** - the examiner stands behind the person and gives a brief tug backwards on the shoulders. The person is warned prior to the tug and is asked to try to stay standing. The same thing can be done from the front as a sternal push. Balance reactions are graded:
    - Normal = 0 = one or two steps to balance
    - Slight impairment = 1 = three to five steps to balance, but unaided
    - Mild impairment = 2 = five or more steps, but unaided
    - Moderate impairment = 3 = stands safely, no postural response, falls if not caught by the examiner
    - Severe impairment = 4 = loses balance spontaneously or with just a slight perturbation
