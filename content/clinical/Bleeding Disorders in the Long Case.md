# Bleeding Disorders in the Long Case
#bpt/clinical
- - -
### Overview
Relatively common problem, among the inherited bleeding disorders it is important to be aware of the haemophilias:
- Haemophilia A = factor VIII deficiency
- Haemophilia B = factor IX deficiency

As these are inherited conditions, family history is very important:
- X-linked recessive disorders mainly affect males whose daughters are obligate carriers of the gene defect
- Female carriers may be less severely affected than males due to skewed x-inactivation
- 30% are spontaneous mutations

> [!TIP] Severity
> Spontaneous haemarthrosis or intramuscular bleed -> severre
> Bleeding with minor trauma or surgery -> moderate 
> Rare spontaneous bleeding -> mild 

#### Diagnosis:
* Prolonged APTT.
* FVIII/FIX level – correlates with bleeding phenotype.
* Genetic studies.

> [!NOTE] Taking the History
> - Quantify the number of bleeds annually
> - Take a detailed family history
> - Enquire into the effect on childhood 

##### Bleeding Sites:
- 90% of bleeds -> into joints and muscles, in order of involvement:
  - Knees (> half)
  - Elbows
  - Ankles
  - Shoulders
  - Wrists
- Intramuscular second most common -> psoas bleeds are a medical emergency
- Symptoms = acute pain -> swelling
  - Need to treat rapidly
- - -
### Treatment
Usually with recombinant factor VIII, or now in some cases emicizumab (a bispecific antibody that mimics the activity of factor VIII). Treatment regimens include:
- Treatment on demand (“episodic”)
- Continuous prophylaxis
  - Primary -> patient’s with moderate to severe factor deficiency
  - Secondary -> aim to break cycle of joint bleeding and development of arthopathy
- - -
### Complications
Four key complications of haemophilia:
1. Joint destruction due to haemarthroses, leading to a number of orthopaedic abnormalities (worsened by obesity)
2. Development of inhibitor antibodies
3. Psychosocial issues and chronic pain syndrome
4. Transmission of blood borne infection – not seen with recombinant therapy: HCV, HBV, HIV
- - -
### Inhibitors 
These are anti-fVIII antibodies that neutralise exogenous factor VIII and pose a major challenge in the treatment of haemophilia. Suspect if:
- Severe haemophilia that does not respond to treatment (occurs in ~25% of patients)
- Mild-moderate haemophilia that changes bleeding phenotype (occurs in ~10% of patients)

In patients with inhibitors who develop an acute bleeding, bypassing agents (e.g. NovoSeven/recombinant fVIIa or FEIBA) can be used. 
- - -
### Managing Comorbidities:
- AF - anticoagulation thought to be safe in those with factor levels >30%, but the Watchman procedure is likely a safer long-term option
- PCI/stroke - DAPT likely safe but may require more intense prophylaxis
- - -
### Perioperative Management
Management of bleeding for surgery:
* Managed with infusion or bolus of factor concentrate – maintain levels ~70-100%.

⠀Role for VTE prophylaxis:
* Compared with other patients undergoing knee or hip replacement haemophiliacs appear at lower risk of VTE compared to 'non-haemophilic' population.
* Generally not used.

⠀Anesthesia and Analgesia.
* Generally try to avoid neuraxial anaesthesia.
* COX2 specific NSAIDs are safe and effective!
- - -
### Psychosocial response to chronic illness
The following issues often arise:
* QOL issues
* Depression
* Unemployment and financial stress
* Increasing disability
* Family stressors
* Relationship issues – discussing HIV/HCV with potential partners
* Chronic pain syndrome and opiate dependence
