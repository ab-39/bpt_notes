# Guillain-Barré Syndrome
#bpt/neurology #bpt/racp_cls
- - -
### Overview
GBS is a common cause of acute, ascending flaccid paralysis, ‘classically’ associated with a _Campylobacter jejuni_ (NB. “campylus” = curved, it is a Gram-negative curved rod) infection in preceding weeks (although other infections can cause it too). The mechanism is to do with cross-reactive antibodies attacking nerve gangliosides. The characteristic laboratory finding of GBS is _albuminocytological dissociation_ - that is, an increase in the CSF total protein without a concomitant increase in serum albumin, caused by disruption of the blood-brain barrier. Treatment is with IVIg or PLEX, and many patients need ventilatory support during recovery. The course is typically self-resolving. GBS is associated with AIDP, and several variants, including the feted Miller-Fisher Syndrome and the CNS Bickenstaff Brainstem Encephalitis, also exist. 
- - -
### Epidemiology
GBS is the most common cause of acute flaccid paralysis, with an estimated annual global incidence of 1-2 per 100,000 person-years. 
- - -
### Diagnosis
To diagnose GBS, there **must** be:
1. Progressive muscle weakness of more than one limb
2. Areflexia or hyporeflexia 

Diagnosis of GBS is typically clinical, as CSF and serum changes may take some time to develop, and early commencement of immunomodulatory therapy is critical. 
- The ‘classic’ CSF finding is albuminocytological dissociation = normal cell counts with elevated protein

### Subtypes
GBS can be subtypes based on electrophysiology or clinical presentation.
- Electrophysiology 
  - AIDP - acute inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy (most common, 90%)
  - AMAN - acute motor axonal neuropathy
  - AMSAN - acute motor sensory axonal neuropathy 
- Clinical
  - Classic sensorimotor
  - Pure motor
  - Paraparetic
  - Pure sensory
  - Miller-Fisher variant
  - Bickenstaff brainstem encephalitis 
- - -
### Pathophysiology
While GBS is thought to be an acute autoimmune disorder caused by an environmental trigger, its exact pathophysiology remains unknown. 

The most common trigger is _Campylobacter jejuni_ infection (30% of cases), with other infections (COVID, Zika, flu, HIB) and noninfectious stimuli (surgery, pregnancy, vaccinations) also being possible triggers. 
- - -
### Clinical Features
The clinical features of GBS are diverse, and reflect various degrees of injury to various types of nerves. Most patients have a diarrhoeal or respiratory patients 4-6w prior to neurological onset. The peak of symptoms is typically within 4 weeks, after which recovery begins. 

#### AIDP
Predominantly motor polyradiculopathy with acute, progressive symmetric weakness involving proximal and distal muscles. The classical pattern is ascending, although it may begin proximally. Tendon reflexes are reduced or absent. Respiratory muscle weakness requiring ventilation is common, as is cranial nerve involvement. Dysautonomia of variable severity also occurs commonly. Sphincter dysfunction is rare. 

#### AMAN
Most common axonal form of GBS, particularly common in Asia and South America. Motor loss typically begins in the lower limbs, but can begin in the upper limbs or cranial nerves. Loss of reflexes typically corresponds to disease severity. Minimal sensory and autonomic dysfunction.

#### AMSAN
Less common than, but typically more severe than AMAN. Typically severe involvement of motor, sensory, and autonomic fibres. Poor prognosis. 

#### Clinical Subtypes
| Type                                      | Features                                                     |
|-------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------|
| Classic sensorimotor GBS                  | Rapidly progressive symmetrical weakness and sensory signs, absent or reduced tendon reflexes; nadir within 2 weeks  |
| Pure motor                                | Motor weakness without sensory signs                         |
| Paraparetic                               | Paresis restricted to the legs                               |
| Pharyngeal-cervical-brachial              | Weakness of pharyngeal, cervical, and brachial muscles without leg involvement |
| BIlateral facial palsy with paraesthesias | Also reduced reflexes                                        |
| Pure sensory                              | Acute or subacute sensory neuropathy without other deficits  |

### Treatment 
Typically with IVIG and PLEX in the acute stage. Combination therapy is not recommended. Oral corticosteroids are contraindicated, and IV corticosteroids appear not to add much to IVIg. A novel treatment, ANX005, an anti-C1q antibody, appears to have efficacy, but eculizumab and rituximab do not. 
- - -
### Prognosis
Most patients generally start recovering at two weeks, and are fully recovered by 12m, although some may have persistent deficits. The mortality rate is 3-7%, typically due to respiratory failure or infection. Age >40y, preceding diarrhoea, short interval to nadir, mechanical ventilation, high-grade neurologic deficits, and persistently low CMAPs are poor prognostic signs.
