### What is Parkinson’s Disease?
Parkinson’s disease is a common neurodegenerative disease resulting from the accumulation of ⍺-synuclein proteins in the substantia nigra of the basal ganglia. 
- - -
### Epidemiology
Parkinson’s disease is _very_ common - affecting 1-2% of people after their sixtieth year. It has an equal worldwide distribution, with a slight preponderance for men. 
- - -
### Aetiology
The exact aetiology of Parkinson’s remains unknown.
There is a genetic association with some genes known to increase risk, but causative genes are extremely rare and are typically associated with early-onset disease with a strong family history. These genes include:
- _SCNA_
- _LRRK2_
- _PRKN_
- _PINK1_
- _DJ1_
- - -
### Diagnostic Criteria
The diagnostic criteria for established PD are:
- Absence of exclusion criteria
- Two or more supportive criteria (slowness, stiffness, tremor)
- Absence of red flag features (e.g. early cognitive decline, abnormal eye movements)

The diagnostic criteria for probable PD are:
- Absence of exclusion criteria
- A balanced number of supportive criteria and red flag features

### Clinical Signs and Symptoms of PD
#### Cardinal Features:
1. Decrementing bradykinesia 
2. Resting tremor (usually unilateral at onset) 
3. Rigidity
4. Postural instability 

#### Other Motor Features:
- Micrographia
- Masking of facies
- Reduced blinking
- Hypophonia
- Dysphagia
- Freezing
- Falling
- - -
### Nonmotor symptoms of Parkinsons
- Constipation -> increase fluid intake and dietary fibre
- Bladder dysfunction (parallels disease progression) -> exclude other pathology, pelvic floor exercises, avoid anticholinergics, mirabegron (alpha agonist) is the best optoin
- Postural hypotension (dysautonomia) -> optimise dopaminergics, encourage hydration and dietary Na, posture, etc., off licence fludrocortisone or pyridostigmine, midodrine
- Sialorrhoea -> topical ipratropium, gums/lollies 
- Bulbar symptoms
  - Dysphagia -> speech therapy
  - Dysphasia
- Neuropsychiatric symptoms
  - Impulse control issues (typically treatment side effect)
  - Depression and anxiety -> mirtazapine, counselling, caution with TCAs due to anticholinergic effects

### Parkinson’s Plus Syndromes
#### Dementia with Lewy Bodies 
DLB is another synucleinopathy that has a mean age of onset of 75y, and accounts for 20% of late onset dementias. It is characterised by early cognitive and neuropsychiatric features, particularly hallucinations, and Parkinsonism symptoms are typically symmetrical. Prognosis is poor, typically 5-8y. 

The following Parkinson Plus Syndromes are typically distinguished from Parkinson’s disease by their rapid progression, symmetrical presentation., and propensity to cause falls. 

#### Multisystem Atrophy
MSA is a rare (2 in 100,000) disorder that typically sets on at a young age (mean: 50y), and predominantly in men. Clinical features include dysautonomia (incl. erectile dysfunction, orthostatic hypotension, etc.), ataxia, dysarthria, [[REM sleep behaviour disorder]], etc. MRI shows selective degeneration of pontocerebellar tracts (Hot cross bun sign). Survival is typically <10y, and there are no good treatment options.

#### Progressive Supranuclear Palsy
PSP is a rare (2 in 100,000) tauopathy that has a mean onset late in middle age (65y) with a slight male preponderance. It typically presents with a symmetrical akinesia, early falls (typically backwards), vertical diplopia and gaze paralysis (downgaze palsy is most specific, usually a late sign), axial extension while walking and axial rigidity, and dysarthria/dysphagia. Impaired speech is a key cognitive abnormality associated with PSP. 

REM sleep behaviour disorder is usually absent. The response to levodopa is typically poor. The ‘classic’ sign on MRI is of midbrain atrophy with a preserved pons, leading to the famous ‘hummingbird’ appearance:

#### Corticobasilar Degeneration
Another rare tauopathy, CBS typically presents with asymmetric dystonic contractions of one hand and cortical symptoms such as apraxia, agnosia, alien limb phenomenon, and focal limb myoclonus, eventually progressing to dementia.

### Treatment of Parkinson’s Disease 
#### Levodopa 
Dopamine precursor with an enzyme inhibitor (e.g. carbidopa, benserazide) that is useful for reducing the rigidity of Parkinson’s disease rather than the tremor. As neurones in the basal ganglia continue to die, however, the patient needs increasing doses of the drug, which can increase the risk of side effects.

#### Dopamine agonists 
These drugs stimulate D2-D4R, and can be used as initial or adjunct therapy. Not as effective as levodopa. Can cause daytime somnolence or impulse control issues.

#### Monoamine Oxidase Inhibitors (Type B)
e.g. selegiline. Prevent the degradation of dopamine. Typically reduce off time. Can be used as monotherapy or adjunct therapy (safinamide is only used as an adjuvant). 
- - -
### Advanced disease
Dyskinesias and ‘wearing off’ phenomena.
Dyskinesias arise from ‘overshooting’ the level of dopamine in the brain, causing involuntary movements. Options:
- Long-acting therapy -> ‘stable’ levels
- Dopamine agonists
- COMT-inhibitors (e.g. entacapone) -> reduce dopamine degradation in the gut
- Amantadine -> block dopamine reuptake while also preventing dyskinesias by acting on the NMDA-R
- Apomorphine (subcutaneous) -> dopamine agonist
- Duodopa -> PEG infusion (key thing here is bypassing the stomach which can be affected by gastroparesis)
- DBS -> typically in sub thalamic nuclei 

Note that when medication results in psychotic symptoms, best practice is to cease all other drugs than levodopa first, as it is the most efficacious. If delusions/psychoses persist despite this, reasonable choices of antipsychotic medication include quetiapine, clozapine, or pimavanserin. Risperidone and haloperidol are typically too risky.
