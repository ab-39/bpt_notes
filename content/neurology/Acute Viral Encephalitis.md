# Acute Viral Encephalitis
#bpt/neurology #bpt/infection #bpt/racp_cls
- - -
### What is acute viral encephalitis?
Acute viral encephalitis is a medical emergency that can cause severe disability or death. It is typically caused by infection of the brain parenchyma with herpes simplex virus. Encephalitis is defined as encephalopathy accompanied by at least two of:
- Fever
- Seizures **or** focal neurological findings
- CSF pleocytosis
- EEG findings suggestive of encephalitis
- Neuroimaging findings suggestive of encephalitis

Practically, we can think of encephalitis as consisting of a triad of fever, headache, and altered consciousness.
- - -
### Epidemiology
Common and highly fatal:
- ~5/100,000/y
- ~5% CFR

#### Causative Organisms
- Most commonly [[herpes simplex virus]] (~75%, HSV1 >> HSV2)
- VZV
- Enteroviruses - most common cause of viral meningitis
- Arboviruses - e.g. [[Japanese encephalitis]] 

Clinical signs can point to specific organisms:
- Rash -> VZV, HHV6, rickettsial fever
- Parotitis -> mumps
- Erythema nodosum -> TB, histoplasmosis (granulomatous infection)
- Mucous membrane involvement -> herpesvirus 
- Concurrent or prodromal URTI -> influenza, mycoplasma 
- Lower motor neuron signs/weakness -> WNV (most common cause of epidemic encephalitis in the USA)

#### Non-Infectious Encephalitis
Consider autoimmune encephalitides:
- [[NMDA Encephalitis|Anti-NMDAR encephalitis]] 
- [[Anti-LGI1 encephalitis]]
- Anti-CASPR2 encephalitis
- [[Paraneoplastic encephalitis]] (also called ‘limbic’ encephalitis)
- - -
### Differential Diagnosis
Acute to subacute onset global cerebral dysfunction essentially points at one of three underlying aetiologies:
1. Encephalitis
2. Encephalopathy
3. [[neurology/Acute Disseminated Encephalomyelitis|Acute disseminated encephalomyelitis]]
- - -
### Diagnosis
The diagnostic criteria for encephalitis require:
- Major criterion - altered mental status persisting for >24h with no alternatie cause identified
- With two or more minor criteria:
  - Fever ≥38ºc 
  - Generalised or partial seizures (not attributable to a known seizure disorder)
  - New onset of focal neurologic findings
  - CSF leucocytosis (>5/µL)
  - Neuroimaging abnormalities
  - EEG abnormalities 
- - -
### Investigations
Bloodwork tends to be nonspecific, pointing to infection. EEG is recommended urgently in suspected encephalitis, as it can help to differentiate focal encephalitis from a diffuse encephalopathy. Urgent MRI brain is recommended, and the specific pattern of signal change can help to identify particular causes:
- Frontotemporal -> HSV
- Thalamus -> JEV
- Diffuse punctate lesions -> Nipah virus
- Disseminated lesions in basal ganglia and brainstem -> Eastern equine encephalitis (Togavirus)

CSF analysis typically shows raised opening pressure and pleocytosis with normal glucose and normal-slightly high protein. The absence of pleocytosis should suggest to the physician to consider alternative (e.g. metabolic) causes for the patient’s presentation. Elevated neutrophils is suggestive of bacterial meningitis or ADEM. CSF PCR is useful for viral aetiologies.

MRI is typically the preferred modality of neuroimaging, often with characteristic findings, e.g.:
- FLAIR hyperintensities in the temporal lobes -> HSV encephalitis
- Asymmetrically increased signal and oedema in the thalamus/BG -> JEV

EEG can yield additional information:
- Periodic lateralised epileptiform discharges -> HSV encephalitis
- Rhythmic delta activity at 1-3Hz with superimposed beta bursts at 20-30Hz (“extreme delta brush pattern”) -> anti-NMDAR encephalitis

### Management
Prompt initiation of aciclovir is the mainstay of management for the viral encephalitides:
- 10mg/kg TDS 
  - Needs to be reduced in renal impairment
  - 14 day course in immunocompetent hosts
  - 21 day course in immunocompromised hosts 
    - Consider repeat LP for HSV CSF PCR in this cohort  
- Safe in pregnancy (class B) 

There may be a role for adjunctive steroids.
- - -
### Prognosis
Poor - mortality is almost 100%. 
There may be complications:
- IIH
- Venous thrombosis
- Epilepsy
- SIADH

Favourable outcomes are noted in young patients with good premorbid neurological function. Worst outcomes tend to occur in older patients and newborns, those with JEV or mycoplasma, GCS <6, ICU admission, status epilepticus, focal signs, abnormal/persistent imaging findings. 

Late sequelae are numerous, including chronic amnestic states, Parkinsonism/encephalitis lethargica (von Economo’s disease), Kluver-Bucy syndrome (hyperorality and hyper sexuality), epilepsy,  etc.
