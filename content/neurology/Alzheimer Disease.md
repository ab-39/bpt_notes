### Overview
The ICD-11 identifies Alzheimer disease as a syndrome of insidious onset of cognitive impairment, with memory typically the first reported complaint. The course is slow but inexorable decline with impairment in memory and additional cognitive domains as the disease progresses. Additional symptoms such as depression and apathy are common early in the disease course, while psychotic symptoms, aggression, gait disturbance, and even seizures can evolve in later stages.

### The Amyloid Hypothesis 
The characteristic biomarkers of Alzheimer disease are β-amyloid and tau proteins. Debate continues, however, as to whether these are responsible for driving the development of the disease, or if they are merely ‘gravestone’ markers.  A Cochrane review has found that CSF and serum amyloid is, at best, a sensitive but not specific test for AD. 

In contrast, plasma phosphorylated tau appars to be a much better test, on par with the amyloid PET scan.

### Assessment
A number of validated tools exist, e.g.:
- sMMSE
- MOCA
- ACE-III
- RUDAS
- Kimberley cognitive assessment 

Blood tests to rule out deficiency.
Structural imaging (usu. MRI) looking for:
- Focal lesions
- Atrophy:
  - Hippocampal -> Alzheimer disease
  - Parietal -> [[young-onset dementia]]
  - Diffuse atrophy
- DWI/SWI
- Gadolinium
- MRA

Second-line tests:
- FDG PET looking for hypometabolism:
  - AD -> temporal and parietal
  - FTD -> frontal and temporal
  - PPA -> asymmetric (L > R)
    - svPPA -> temporal love
    - nfvPPA -> frontal lobe 
- Lumbar puncture
- Amyloid PET - diminishing PPV in the elderly, but good specificity
- EEG - typically slowed in dementia, but not specific 
- Brain biopsy with prion precautions

### Management
#### Non-Pharmacological
It is important to ensure that the patient’s pre-existing cognitive state is optimised prior to the introduction of new therapies:
- Minimise anticholinergic drugs (there is a destruction of cholinergic neurons in AD)
- Correct sensory impairment (e.g. hearing aids result in a 48% slowing in cognitive decline)
- Offer group cognitive stimulation therapy for those with MCI/early AD

Do _not_ offer:
- Acupuncture
- Ginseng
- Vitamin E
- Non-invasive brain stimulation (e.g. TMS)
- Task-specific cognitive training (1-1) - no evidence
- Nutritional drinks

#### Pharmacological Therapies
~Acetylcholinesterase Inhibition~
- Indicated for the treatment of mild-moderatae AD (MMSE >10) after diagnosis by a specialist
- E.g.: donepezil, galantamine, rivastigmine
  - Similar efficacy and safety
  - Can switch if no response or intolerance
- Benefits:
  - 40-70% of patients
  - Improvements in cognition and function, _not_ mortality
  - Temporary - usu. 6-12 months before worsening 
- ADRs:
  - Typically GI - N/V/D, anorexia, weight loss
  - CV - bradycardia, dizziness, syncope (vagally mediated)
  - Vivid dreams
  - Contraindicated if: peptic ulcer disease, ureteric obstruction, heart block/bradyarrhythmias
- Check ECG, weight, and medication interactions before commencement

~NMDA-R Antagonism~
- Memantine
- Mechanism: reduces glutamate signalling -> reduces neurodegneration
- Indicated for the treatment of moderate-severe AD (MMSE 10-14) in patients intolerant of/contraindicated AChEIs
- ADRs: well-tolerated (GI upset, dizziness, drowsiness, headache)
- Contraindicated if: seizures/epilepsy
- Improvement in cognitive function and behavioural disturbances versus placebo

~Anti-Amyloid Therapies~
- Humanised monoclonal antibodies against cerebral β-amyloid plaques (lecanemab, donanemab)
- Weight-based infusion
- Evidence for both slowing in cognitive decline (~30%) and also positive alterations in disease-specific biomarkers 
- Safety considerations = ARIAs (‘amyloid-related imaging abnormalities’):
  - Oedema (ARIA-E)
  - Haemorrhage (ARIA-H)
  - ~15% with lecanemab and ~25% with denanemab
  - Most common early in disease course and if patient _APOE4_ positive/heterozygous 
  - Risk as high as 40% in homozygotes -> generally do not use anti-amyloid therapies
  - Generally (~90%) asymptomatic and not requiring drug cessation

#### Managing BPSDs
Treating BPSDs _must_ begin with investigating the presumed precipitant and optimising non-pharmacological approaches. Pharmacological management can include:
- Depression -> no clear evidence for antidepressants (unless depression pre-dates the AD diagnosis)
- Agitation -> SSRI (most evidence for citalopram)
- Severe symptoms:
  - Antipsychotics associated with increased risk of stroke and death
  - Should be avoided, but may be needed to be used in the case of severe agitation
  - Psychosis -> risperidone has the best evidence base
