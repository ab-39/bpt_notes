# Methaemoglobinaemia
#bpt/general
- - -
### What is methaemoglobinaemia?
Methaemoglobinaemia is an oxidisation of the iron in haem molecules from Fe2+ (ferric) to Fe3+ (ferrous), which is unable to bind oxygen. This increases the affinity of the remaining three ferric haem molecules for oxygen, thereby causing a left-shift in the [[oxygen dissociation curve]]. This causes reduced oxygen delivery to the tissues and a ‘functional anaemia’.

Note that methaemoglobin can normally accumulate through the day to day process of oxidation in the body - this is normally corrected by NADPH and NADPH methaemoglobin reductase (a combined effort of cytochrome b5 - an electron acceptor - and cytochrome b5 reductase).

### Causes of methaemoglobinaemia 
There are three congenital causes of methaemoglobinaemia:
1. Cytochrome b5 reductase deficiency - the most common
2. Haemoglobin M disease - an autosomal dominant haemoglobinopathy - uncommon 
3. Cytochrome b5 deficiency - rare 

And numerous acquired causes:
- The presence of direct oxidants:
  - Nitrites - sodium nitrite, amyl nitrite 
  - Nitrates - GTN, nitroprusside
  - Antimalarials - pentaquine
  - Methylene blue*
- The presence of indirect oxidants:
  - Aromatic hydrocarbons - aniline, naphthalene, nitrobenzene
  - Sulfonamides - Bactrim, dapsone
  - Local anaesthetics - benzocaine, prilocaine
  - Nitrofurantoin
- - -
### Clinical Presentation of Methaemoglobinaemia 
Symptoms and signs of methaemoglobinaemia are generally vague, but include dyspnoea, hypoxia, cyanosis, and malaise. 

The ‘classic’ findings are:
- Spo2 ~85% - ordinary pulse oximetry relies on lights of two wavelengths: one to detect oxyHb, and one to detect deoxyHb, the ratio of which gives the SpO2. Methaemoglobin absorbs light maximally at other wavelengths, and so once it is present in the blood at about 30%, the ratio of light at the two wavelengths is 1:1, giving an SpO2 of 85%
- Refractory hypoxaemia - because the SpO2 is incorrectly registered
