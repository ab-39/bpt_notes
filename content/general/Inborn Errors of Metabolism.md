### Disorders of Haem Biosynthesis
The biosynthetic pathway of haem is complex, but broadly involves a few key intermediaries which are acted on by a few key enzymes (in brackets):
- Succincyl-coA and glycine as the initial substrate (ALA-synthase)
- δ-Αminolevulenic acid/ALA (ALA-dehydratase)
- Porphobilinogen (Hydroxymethylbilane synthase)
- Hydroxymethylbilane (Uroporphynogen III synthase)
- Uroporphynogen III (Uroporphynogen decarboxylase)
- And, after a few steps, ultimately, haem

Disorders of this pathway lead to two broad categories of disorders, each with multiple subtypes:
- Hepatic porphyrias - typically characterised by neurovisceral symptoms
- Erythropoietic porphyrias - typically characterised by cutaneous symptoms

The most common porphyria, _porphyria cutanea tarda_, is caused by accumulation of uroporpynogen III due to a deficiency of URO-decarboxylase. This is either inherited in an AD fashion, or develops sporadically. It is characterised by blistering, photosensitive lesions and the absence of neurovisceral symptoms, despite being a hepatic porphyria. Urinary porphobilinogen levels are normal, but urinary ALA and serum porphyrins are elevated.

*Acute intermittent porphyria* is a common MCQ question despite being exceedingly rare, and is characterised by the onset of neurovisceral attacks after puberty. It is caused by autosomal dominant mutations in the genes encoding hydroxymethylbilane synthase, and is consequently associated with significantly elevated urine porphobilinogen levels. Treatment is generally with intravenous hemin, although _galvosiran_, an siRNA therapy silencing the _ALAS1_ mRNA gene leading to reduced PBG levels.

### Lysosomal Storage Diseases
The continuous generation of lysosomes requires ongoing synthesis of hydrolases, membrane proteins, and membranes themselves, all of which arise from the Golgi apparatus and endoplasmic reticulum. Abnormalities of _any_ of these biosynthetic steps can lead to an LSD, all of which converge on a final common pathogenetic pathway: abnormal accumulation of substances (typically some form of lipid, but also glycosaminoglycans, oligosaccharides, glycogen, and other macromolecules) in the tissues. Treatment is generally with recombinant enzyme therapy. 

Some of the more prevalent LSDs include:
1. Hexosaminidase deficiency = *HEXA* mutation = Tay-Sachs disease
   - Autosomal recessive 
   - Three phenotypes:
     - Infantile onset -> neurodegeneration and death
     - Juvenile onset -> ataxia and dementia, death in adolescence
     - Adult onset -> dementia; progressive motor weakness with spinocerebellar and LMN signs
   - Highly prevalent among Ashkenazi Jews 
2. β-Glucocerebrosidase deficiency = *GBA1* mutation  = Gaucher disease 
   - Autosomal recessive deficiency of β-glucocerebrosidase leading to accumulation of sphingolipids in the brain, liver, spleen, and marrow
   * Can be non-neuropathic (type I), acute neuropathic (type II), or chronic neuropathic (type III)
   * Cardinal finding is macrophages with accumulated sphingolipids in the lysosome = *Gaucher* cells
   * Type II and IIIa typically present with neurological symptoms, while types I and IIIb have a range of manifestations - hepatosplenomegaly, cytopenias, failure to thrive, interstitial lung disease, abnormal modelling of metaphyses (leading to the Erlenmeyer flask abnormality), and increased risk of some cancers
   * Diagnosis is either with WBC enzymatic assays, sequencing of the *GBA1* gene, or the detection of Gaucher cells in the marrow
   * Treatment is with recombinant glucocerebrosidase (e.g. Imiglucerase)
3. ⍺-Galactosidase deficiency = _GALA_ mutation = Fabry disease 
   - X-linked, as prevalent as 1/3,500 in some populations
   - Clinical manifestations: angiokeratomas, hypohidrosis, corneal and lenticular opacities, acroparaesthesiae, CKD (less common in females), cardiac fibrosis, etc.
   - Treatment:
     - Acroparaesthesiae - gabapentin, carbamazepine
     - Migalastat - chaperone protein that stabilises endogenous protein 
4. Acid sphingomyelinase deficiency = _SMPD1_ mutation = Niemann-Pick disease
   - Autosomal recessive
   - Three types:
     - Type A = rapidly progressive CNS disease
     - Type B = later-onset, hepatomegaly and cirrhosis with replacement of Kupffer cells by foam macrophages laden with sphingomyelin
     - Type C = autosomal recessive deficiencies of NPC1/2 protein leading to progressive CNS disease
   - Enzyme replacement therapy currently under investigations

### Glycogen Storage Disorders 
There are thirteen glycogen storage disorders, the most common of which are summarised here. 
| Class        | Defect                              | Epidemiology | Characteristics                                              | Management                                                   |
|--------------|-------------------------------------|--------------|--------------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------|
| I/von Gierke | Glucose-6-phosphatase (_G6PC_)      | ~25% of GSDs | Profound fasting hypoglycaemia with mild ketosis, hyperlipidaemia (‘doll-like facies’), hepato/renomegaly (due to glycogen++), hyperuricaemia (gout), lactic acidosis | Frequent PO glucose loads, avoid fructose and galactose      |
| II/Pompe     | Lysosomal ⍺-glucosidase (_GAA_)     | ~15% of GSDs | Infant: profound myopathy, ventilatory failure, cardiomyopathy<br>Adult: mild myopathy<br>Elevated CK | Lifestyle: high protein diet with complex carbohydrates, avoid strenuous exercise<br>Medical: IV ⍺-glucosidase <br>Monitor for cardiac complications |
| III/Cori     | Glycogen debranching enzyme (_AGL_) | ~25% of GSDs | Less severe form of von Gierke disease; normal lactate; normal gluconeogenesis; may be associated with cardiomyopathy | High glucose, high protein (gluconeogenesis) diet            |
| V/McArdle    | Myophosphorylase (_PYGM_)           | ~2% of GSDs  | Increased glycogen in the muscle but an inability to break it down -> painful muscle cramps and myoglobinuria with strenuous exercise. Demonstrates a ‘second wind’ phenomenon due to increased muscle blood flow. Characterised by a flat venous lactate curve and elevations in ammonia during exercise.  | Mild to moderate exercise + high carbohydrate diet + creatine  |
