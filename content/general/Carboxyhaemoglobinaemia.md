# Carboxyhaemoglobinaemia
#bpt/general
- - -
### Overview 
Carboxyhaemoglobinaemia is a consequence of carbon monoxide poisoning. Carbon monoxide is the leading cause of poisoning in the United States, and arises from hydrocarbon combustion (e.g. fires, faulty gas heaters, paint stripper/methylene chloride). CO has approximately 200 times the affinity of O2 for haem, and outcompetes it in binding to haemoglobin.  Once bound, it induces an allosteric change in the haemoglobin molecule that further discourages the binding of oxygen, shifting the [[oxygen dissociation curve]] to the left. CO also unleashes a multitude of directly toxic effects, including mitochondrial dysfunction and lipid peroxidation. 

Signs and symptoms of CO poisoning are quite vague, with most patients presenting with a headache. Carboxyhaemoglobin levels are confirmed on [[respiratory/Arterial Blood Gas|arterial blood gas]], with the caveat that the SaO2 will be normal as the PaO2 will be normal due to normal diffusion. The standard pulse oximeter _cannot_ distinguish between carboxy- and oxy-haemoglobin, and so will yield normal readings. Co-oximetry on an ABG sample overcomes this problem. 

Treatment is supportive, consisting of 100% O2 delivery through high-flow nasal cannulae, as this reduces the half life of carboxyhaemoglobin.

Long term outcomes are poor, especially for the brain and heart as they are highly metabolically active organs.
