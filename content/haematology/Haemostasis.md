### Overview
Haemostasis is a homoeostatic mechanism that aims to maintain blood within the vasculature in a fluid state. To achieve this, when the vessels are injured, blood forms a localised clot to arrest bleeding, which eventually disintegrates during wound healing. Famously, the three interacting factors that determine haemostasis were outlined by Rudolf Virchow:
- Blood
- Endothelium
- Flow

In the normal state, the endothelium works to inhibit platelet adhesion through:
- Actively inhibiting aggregation - e.g. through PGI2, tPA, etc.
- Inactivating pro-thrombotic chemicals - e.g. through ADPase, thrombomodulin, etc.

### Primary Haemostasis
In response to injury or high shear stress, the endothelium is disrupted, revealing the subendothelial matrix and its stores of [[von Willebrand factor]]. Additional von Willebrand factor is released from the Weibel-Pallade bodies in the endothelium. vWF binds platelets at the GPIb-V-IX receptor:
![](Haemostasis/image%202.png)
This triggers activation of platelets via GPIIb-IIIa, which causes morphological change of platelets and irreversible binding to the matrix:
![](Haemostasis/image%203.png)
Once activated, platelets degranulate, causing recruitment of additional platelets and triggering secondary haemostasis:

### Secondary Haemostasis (For Beginners…)
![](Haemostasis/unknown.png)
The goal of the coagulation cascade is to create the prothrombinase complex (Xa/Va) via either pathway, and ultimately achieve two things:
- Convert prothrombin (II) to thrombin (IIa)
- Which will convert fibrinogen (I) to fibrin (Ia), which will form a clot

> The common pathway is made up of the four smallest dollar denominations:
> - $10
> - $5
> - $2
> - $1

There are two pathways by which this can occur:
- The tissue factor pathway:
  - Tissue factor (III)
  - Factor VII
- The activated partial prothrombin pathway:
  - Contact factors
  - Factor XII
  - Factor XI
  - Factor IX
  - Factor VIII

> [!TIP] Mnemonic
> The factors of the intrinsic/aPTT pathway form a word chain:
> ***T* welv *E* leve *N* in *E* igh *T* en**

In addition to this, there is an important clot-stabilising factor: factor XIII.

#### The Cell-Based Model of Coagulation
This aims to reflect the actual physiological process of clotting somewhat more accurately through three overlapping phases:
1. Initiation - the generation of trace amounts of thrombin at sites of injury (roughly correlating to the tissue factor pathway)
2. Amplification - assembly of factor complexes on activated platelets (roughly correlating to the aPTT pathway)
   - This explains away two deficiencies of the ‘cascade model’:
     - Why haemophiliacs bleed despite having an intact tissue factor pathway - because they cannot amplify
     - Why deficiencies of the contact factors and factor XII rarely result in a significant bleeding phenotype - because the ‘actual’ activation of the aPTT pathway occurs via the tissue factor pathway 
3. Propagation - the culmination of the aPTT pathway in a thrombin burst that activates fibrinogen; fXIII crosslinking fibrin polymers 
4. Termination - fibrinolysis re-establishes blood flow

### Physiological Antocoagulation
![](Haemostasis/image%206.png)
There are three key natural anticoagulants which regulate the process of clot formation:
- Antithrombin III - inactivates factors Xa and IIa, and also factors IXa, XIa, and XIIa
- Proteins C and S - inactivate factors Va and VIIIa
- Tissue factor pathway inhibitor - deactivates the extrinsic tenase complex

Additionally, once a clot is formed, it also undergoes degradation by _plasmin_ into fibrin degradation products (most notably the d-dimer). The process is:
- Circulating plasminogen is activated by either:
  - Tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) - released from the endothelial cells in response to damage
  - Urokinase (uPA) - found in the plasma and urine
  - Naturally inhibited by the plasminogen activator inhibitors 1 and 2 (latter only in pregnancy)
- Plasmin degrades fibrin
  - Naturally inhibited by ⍺2-antiplasmin

### Measuring Haemostasis and Coagulation
There are three main tests in use to test the various arms of the coagulation cascade. 

> [!WARNING] 
> These tests _predate_ the discovery of the coagulation cascade and thus do not give us any ‘real’ information. They need to be interpreted in light of the patient’s clinical situation. 

1. The Prothrombin Time (PT)
   - This measures the time is takes for clot to be triggered by thromboplastin _in vitro_
   - Thromboplastin is a mix of tissue factor, phospholipids, and calcium
   - It is reflective of the activity of the tissue factor and common pathways
   - To standardise reporting, this is compared with a reference value to give the _international normalised ratio_ 
2. The Activated Partial Thromboplastin Time (aPTT)
   - This measures the time it takes for a clot to be triggered by contact activation with silica/ellegic acid, phospholipids, and calcium
   - It is reflective of the activity of the aPTT/intrinsic and common pathways
3. The Thrombin Time (TT)
   - This meausres the time it takes for _exogenous_ thrombin to convert fibrinogen to fibrin 
   
Note that factor XIII deficiency will cause bleeding without abnormal clotting studies, as will platelet disorders.
