# Chronic Myeloid Leukaemia
#bpt/haematology #bpt/racp_cls
- - -
### Overview
Chronic myeloid leukaemia is a clonal myeloproliferative disorder of haematopoietic stem cells that is defined by a single mutation - the ‘Philadelphia chromosome’: translocation t9;22, leading to the formation of the fusion gene _BCR-ABL_, which results in constitutively active downstream signalling that results in uncontrolled proliferation of aberrant stem cells. 

This is an inevitably fatal disease that carries a risk of transformation to either acute myeloid or lymphoid leukaemia of ~25% p.a.

### Epidemiology 
CML accounts for ~15% of cases of leukaemia, with a slight male preponderance. It is a disease of the elderly, with a median age at diagnosis of 60y. There is no familial component.
- - -
### Aetiology
CML arises due to sporadic mutations. The only established risk factor is ionising radiation. 
- - -
### Clinical Presentation 
The majority of patients present in the indolent phase, typically with an incidentally noted leucocytosis or thrombocytosis. Rarely, patients present in a blast crisis. 

#### Prognostic Factors
There are three main scoring systems for CML are SOKAL, EURO, and EUTOS, based on age, spleen size, platelet count, and blast count.
- - -
### Monitoring 
Current European guidelines suggest molecular monitoring with frequency based on the percentage of BCR-ABL in the peripheral blood.

### Treatment 
The mainstay of treatment for CML is BCR-ABL tyrosine kinase inhibitors. 

The oldest and most common of these is _imatinib_ given at a dose of 400mg daily. Its common side effects include fluid retention, MSK pain, GI issues, skin rash, and fatigue. Key trial: IRIS.

The newer generation of TKIs include dasatinib, which is more potent than imatinib. It leads to deeper and more rapid molecular responses, but the overall clinical trajectory is the same. Side effects include pulmonary hypertension and pleural effusion. Other second generation drugs include nilotinib, with side effects including CV events and pancreatitis.

Second line treatment requires guidance from BCR-ABL kinase domain mutation analysis. Specifically, the T315I ‘gatekeeper’ mutation requires treatment with ponatinib or asciminib, side effects of which include pancreatitis, fatigue, nausea, and headache. 

Note that tyrosine kinase inhibitors are not safe in pregnancy. IFN⍺ is a possible alternative.  

In CML, allogeneic bone marrow transplantation used to be the gold standard of treatment, and remains to be used in patients who are resistant to second line therapies or who present in blast crisis.
