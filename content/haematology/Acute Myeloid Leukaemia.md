# Acute Myeloid Leukaemia
#bpt/haematology #bpt/racp_cls
- - -
### Overview 
AML is a disorder of mutations in early haematopoietic precursor cells that alter normal growth and differentiation. This results in an accumulation of large numbers of abnormal, immature, myeloid cells called _blasts_, which can divide and proliferate, but not differentiate. The clinical presentation often encompasses:
- Anaemia/fatigue
- Fevers
- Thrombocytopenia -> mucosal bleeding
- Gingival enlargement
- Bone pain
- Infiltrative symptoms - skin rash, breathlessness, etc.
- Infection
- Leucostatic symptoms
In some cases, particularly with APML, the patient can present _in extremis_ with disseminated intravascular coagulation. 
- - -
### Epidemiology
Like most blood cancers, AML is a disease of the elderly, with a median age at diagnosis of 70y. About 1,000 cases are diagnosed each year in Australia. 

A large number of mutations are idiopathic but ionising radiation and chemical exposure are also common causes.  There are also numerous familial disorders associated with an increased incidence of AML (e.g. Fanconi anaemia), and other syndromes (e.g. Down syndrome) which have a similar association.
- - -
### Pathogenesis
Like [[normal haematopoiesis]], AML is maintained by a pool of self-renewing leukaemic stem cells, that are usually quiescent and resistant to chemotherapy. This can lead to high rates of relapse. 
- - -
### Diagnosis
Diagnosis of AML _requires_ a bone marrow biopsy, and is confirmed by:
1. Morphology - >20% myeloblasts on aspirate
2. Flow cytometry confirming the myeloid lineage - CD13, CD117
3. Cytogenetics/molecular studies/karyotyping:
   - t(8;21)
   - inv(16)
   - t(16;16)
   - t(15;17) = PML-RARα
   
#### Risk Stratification
There are over 50 genes implicated in AML, all of which carry different prognoses. 

In general, they are categorised as follows:
| Favourable                                        | Intermediate                     | Unfavourable                       |
|---------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------|------------------------------------|
| Core binding factor mutations (e.g. t8;21, inv16) | FLT3 internal tandem duplication | Multiple cytogenetic abnormalities |
| NPM1 without FLT3                                 | All other patients               | Mutated TP53                       |
|                                                   |                                  | Secondary AML                      |

### Treatment 
After completing baseline screening and cardiac function, induction typically occurs with ‘7+3’:
- Cytarabine continuous infusion for seven days
- Anthracycline for three days (daunorubicin or idarubicin)

This leads to about a month of profound cytopenias, that require transfusion support and are often associated with febrile neutropenia and fungal infections. 

After this, remission (defined as <5% blasts) is investigated with another bone marrow biopsy. If remission is successful, consolidation will be carried out with:
- Low risk patients -> high dose cytarabine 
- Medium-high risk patients -> ASCT

Recent developments have led to targeted therapies for AML:
- FLT3 mutations -> add inhibitors: midostaurin (first-line), gilteritinib (relapse)
- Core binding factor mutations -> add anti-CD33: gemtuzumab + ozogamicin
  - Note that this is an antibody-drug conjugation, that exploits the CD33 to have the drug internalised and then released _within_ the cancer cell
  - VOD/SOS is a possible side effect of treatment with 7+3 GO induction treatment, and can be treated with defibrotide 
- IDH mutations -> inhibitors: ivosidinib, enasidinib (not available in Australia)
- KMT2A rearrangements -> menin inhibitors

That being said, the above regimen is typically only recommended for patients who are ‘fit’ - i.e. under the age of 75 and without major comorbidities. For those who are ‘not fit’, treatment is typically with the BCL2 inhibitor venetoclax and the hypomethylating agent azacitidine. There is still a risk of cytopenias, and (of course) of TLS with venetoclax.

### Acute Promyelocytic Leukaemia
APML is a unique subtype of AML that is  characterised by malignant promyelocytes and profound coagulation abnormalities. On a molecular level, the defining feature is the abnormal fusion protein PML-RARα that is driven by the t(15;17) karyotype. Histologically, we see so-called ["faggot" cells](https://www.cellwiki.net/en/aberrations/promyelocytes-faggot-cells) with numerous azurophilic granules called _Auer rods_. 
![](Acute%20Myeloid%20Leukaemia/big_5911be8aa9fbd.jpg)
These patients have an elevated PT and aPTT, classically with a low fibrinogen - i.e. DIC.

> [!TIP] Mnemonic
> 15, 16, 17
> P is the 16th letter of the alphabet
> The key cytogenetic abnormality is a (15;17) translocation

APML is a medical emergency and needs to be treated promptly if it is suspected. In the first instance, DIC needs to be controlled with transfusions of platelets, cryoprecipitate (for the fibrinogen) and FFP (for the INR). Treatment of the cancer itself is with:
- All-trans retinoic acid
- Arsenic

One side effect of therapy can be ‘differentiation syndrome’: the rapid differentiation of promyelocytes, leading to a rush of neutrophils into the organs, characterised by multiorgan dysfunction, fever, and oedema. The most commonly affected organ is the lungs with ARDS. Treatment is with dexamethasone 10mg BD.
