# Haemolysis
#bpt/haematology #bpt/racp_cls
- - -
### Overview
Red cell destruction can occur both naturally, at the end of their 120 day lifespan, or else during disease. Haemolysis can be divided according to its location:
- Extravascular -> within the reticuloendothelial system -> minimal release of haemoglobin into the bloodstream
  - Where ‘normal’ haemolysis occurs at the end of an RBC’s lifespan - this normally occurs when ~5% of RBCs pass per cycle through the cords of Bilroth (as opposed to the sinuses)
- Intravascular -> within the blood vessels -> significant release of free haemoglobin into the bloodstream
Haemolytic anaemias are typically caused by both mechanisms, with one predominating. 
- - -
### Causes
Causes of haemolysis can be divided into three groups:
1. Defects _within_ red cells, e.g.
   - Dysfunction of enzyme controlled metabolism (e.g. G6PD, pyruvate kinase)
   - Abnormal haemoglobins (e.g. HbS)
   - Defects of haematopoiesis (e.g. thalassaemia, MDS)
2. Loss of structural integrity of the membrane and cytoskeleton, e.g.:
   - Hereditary sphero/elliptocytosis (= spectrin deficiency)
   - Paroxysmal nocturnal haemglobinuria (loss of protective CD55/59 leading to complement mediated damage)
   - Antibody-mediated damage
3. Damage by extrinsic factors
   - Mechanical trauma
   - Microangiopathic conditions
   - Chemical toxins  (e.g. lead)
   - Infections (e.g. malaria, _Clostridium welchii_) 
   - Drug-induced (typically by oxidative haemolysis):
     - Dapsone
     - Sulphonamides
     - Antimalarials
     - TMP-SMX
     - Napthalene

### Morphology
- Fragmented red cells - characteristic of MAHA
- Spherocytes - missing pale centre
- Bite cells - arising from oxidative damage

As well as evidence of bone marrow compensation:
- Polychromatic erythrocytes = reticulocytes 
- Nucleated erythrocytes

### Investigations
By specific diagnosis:
- Hereditary spherocytosis -> EMA dye binding test (does _not_ bind to missing lysine in spherocytes)/flow cytometry/osmotic fragility test 
- Thalassaemia -> electrophoresis
- Sickle cell disease -> electrophoresis
- Enzyme deficiencies -> specific assays
- Autoimmune haemolytic anaemia -> direct antiglobulin test (which can be ‘cold’/IgM or ‘hot’/IgG)
  - Cold -> red cell agglutination 
  - Warm -> spherocytes 
- Drug-induced haemolytic anaemia -> RBC G6PD
- Mechanical stress -> RBC morphology + AKI, coagulation screen, fibrinogen assay
- PNH -> flow cytometric analysis of RBC and PMN GPI-linked antigens (CD55 and CD59)

NB:
- Direct antiglobulin test -> add Coombs reagent (anti-human and anti-complement) to patient RBCs -> observe for agglutination
- Indirect antiglobulin test -> add group O RBCs and Coombs reagent to patient serum -> observe for agglutination 
- Haptoglobin -> protein that scavenges haemoglobin from the bloodstream to minimise oxidative toxicity from free haem, leading to phagocytosis of Hp-Hb dimers by the macrophages of the reticuloendothelial system. Importantly, this protein is *not* recycled and hence is rapidly depleted in large volume haemolysis

Urinary haemosiderin is the most specific test for intravascular haemolysis.
